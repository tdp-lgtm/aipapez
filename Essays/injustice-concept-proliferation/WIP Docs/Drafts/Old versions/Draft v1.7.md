## 1. The Coining Boom

Compare two coinages. In the first, a patient reports disabling pain after surgery. Her chart describes her as anxious. Her report is discounted, and the discounting tracks her group membership: reports like hers, from patients like her, are systematically read as symptoms rather than testimony. The pattern recurs across thousands of charts. Call this case *Dismissed*. The pattern has a name: testimonial injustice, a wrong done to someone in her capacity as a knower (Fricker 2007). The name brought recognition: it entered clinical training and guidelines, and the patient gained a way to say what had happened to her.[^1] The coinage worked.

In the second, imagine a paper coining the term "ambient injustice" for the diffuse worseness of life under modern social arrangements. The worseness is real. It is socially produced and unevenly borne. But the concept is deliberately unlocalized: it names no particular capacity in which anyone is wronged, no route by which the wronging works, and nothing that would count as responding to it. The diffuseness is deliberate. Call this *Ambient Injustice*. Something here has misfired, and not because the badness it names is unreal. The difference between these two coinages is this essay's subject.

The specimens are not isolated. Since Fricker (2007), the family has grown quickly: contributory injustice (Dotson 2012), discursive injustice (Kukla 2014), epistemic exploitation (Berenstain 2016), affective injustice (Srinivasan 2018; Archer and Mills 2019), ontic injustice (Jenkins 2020), attention-based epistemic injustice (Smith and Archer 2020), zetetic wronging (Friedman 2025). They form a genre with a shared shape: identify a recurrent bad, name it with the injustice-suffix, and argue that the name fills a gap in our resources for understanding and responding to it (Jenkins 2020 frames the genre in these terms). Is this practice good or bad, for philosophy and for the societies philosophy addresses?

Two camps have ready answers. The Inflation View says the practice debases its own currency. Injustice-talk, on this view, is a medium of exchange: new coinages cheapen every note in circulation, and the boom is that debasement in progress. The view transposes a worry with a distinguished history in the theory of rights (Alston 1984; Griffin 2008 (verify)). The opposing camp says the worry is confused. Concepts are non-rival, and a new concept costs no one anything. And where genuine wrongs go unnamed, naming them is not expenditure but repair. On the practice's own founding theory, leaving people without concepts for their experiences is itself an injustice (Fricker 2007; Nikolaidis 2020). Each camp gets its best form in §2.

I argue that both camps are wrong about what a coinage draws on, and that seeing this yields a test that vindicates the practice, but only conditionally.

> **The Investment View.** Coining an injustice concept draws on a scarce public resource: not the validity of moral claims, which does not dilute, but the capacity of others to take claims up. A coinage is justified when it is *backed* — when it fixes who is wronged and in what capacity, by what mechanism, and what shape a response could take. Every coinage draws on that capacity; a backed coinage yields answerable claims in return, and an unbacked one yields nothing.

The practice's flagship coinages are backed. So the coining boom has been good for philosophy and for society: good because the coinages meet the condition, not because the practice has momentum.

Three qualifications bound the claim. The Backing Condition is a standard of success, not a licensing regime: nobody should be stopped from coining. An unbacked coinage does not name an unreal bad. The failure lies in the concept's construction, not in the world. The verdict itself is comparative and defeasible, not a blanket blessing of whatever gets coined next. §2 states the rival positions at their strongest and separates two phenomena the debate runs together. §3 argues that both misidentify what a coinage draws on. §4 builds the Backing Condition and states the central argument. §5 tests the practice's actual coinages. §6 answers the three strongest objections. §7 concludes.

## 2. Two Views About Coining

Start with a missing distinction. Proliferation is the addition of new kind-concepts: "ontic injustice" names a kind nothing else named. Creep is the stretching of an existing concept's extension, outward to qualitatively new phenomena or downward to milder ones; Haslam calls these horizontal and vertical concept creep (Haslam 2016; Haslam et al. 2020). "Trauma" reaching ever milder adversities is creep. Coining "zetetic wronging" is proliferation. The critical literature runs the two together, but they fail in different ways. This essay is about the first.

Creep degrades a concept from inside. As unlike cases accumulate under one term, its mechanism blurs, and its own users lose the ability to say what it picks out. The best-documented costs in the critical literature are creep costs: the trivialization findings show that broadened concepts come to seem less serious (Haslam et al. 2020). Stretching has a second documented cost. When a new wrong is made intelligible by assimilating it to a normatively familiar label, the label imports a flawed analogy and groups together cases whose wrongs differ in kind. Maitra shows both distortions at work in "statutory rape," which can leave its subjects choosing between communicating their experience under the available label and understanding it accurately (Maitra 2018). A fresh kind-concept, coined for the new wrong itself, is the alternative to both failure modes.

Proliferation, by contrast, leaves existing concepts untouched and adds a new one whose credentials can be assessed at birth. Even Haslam's own review concedes that the benefits of expansion occur where broadening amounts to naming: "emotional abuse" gave a nameless maltreatment a label under which it could be identified and protested (Haslam et al. 2020): creep does good where it approximates coining. If proliferation is bad, it must be bad in some other way. The Inflation View, restated to target proliferation properly, says how.

In international law, Alston watched new human rights being "conjured up," proclaimed by one body after another with "no prior discussion." Their "inordinate vagueness" eased adoption, precisely because states could vote for them without committing to anything. He warned that such proliferation "would be much more likely to contribute to a serious devaluation of the human rights currency than to enrich significantly the overall coverage provided by existing rights" (Alston 1984 (verify page)). Note his exact target: "not so much... the proliferation of new rights but rather the haphazard, almost anarchic manner in which this expansion is being achieved" (Alston 1984 (verify page)). Griffin makes the philosophical version of the point: inflationary pressure debases the determinateness of the term itself (Griffin 2008 (verify)). The picture is monetary: normative language is a common currency, each coinage issues new denominations, and unbacked issues devalue every note.

Transposed to injustice-concepts, the worry is not silly, and I grant two things to it. First, something really is finite; §3 says what. Second, some coinage behavior really is extractive: a career can be made by a coinage whether or not the concept was needed. Note where Alston himself ended. He canvassed substantive quality-criteria for new rights and then abandoned them, because in an intergovernmental assembly their application would be "quintessentially political" (Alston 1984 (verify page)). He retreated to procedure instead, proposing that new rights carry what he called an *appellation contrôlée*: a certification, on the model of wine labels, that the prescribed process was followed. Philosophy's forum is different: a coinage paper can be assessed substantively, clause by clause, and that possibility is what this essay takes up. What the Inflation View owes us, and never supplies, is the identity of the scarce medium. Its own metaphor fails there, as §3 shows.

The opposing view at its strongest begins from non-rivalry. Concepts are not consumed by use: my deploying "ontic injustice" leaves you no less of it. And on the practice's founding theory, conceptual gaps are themselves sites of injustice. When a significant area of one's social experience is "obscured from collective understanding owing to a structural identity prejudice in the collective hermeneutical resource" (Fricker 2007, ch. 7), those who lack the name are disadvantaged in making sense of, and in claiming against, what happens to them. On this view coining consumes nothing. It is repair, and the presumption favors more of it.

The strongest published defense of proliferation adds a rider worth noticing. Nikolaidis argues that concept proliferation disrupts epistemic injustice, but only when paired with critical tools for sorting beneficial concepts from harmful ones, tools he assigns to concept-users rather than gatekeepers (Nikolaidis 2020). Grant the non-rivalry of concepts, and grant that licensing regimes overshoot. But the rider concedes what the official position denies: that sorting is needed. If a new concept truly cost nothing, sorting would not matter.

So the two camps talk past each other at a fixed point: neither says what, if anything, a coinage draws on.

## 3. What a Coinage Draws On

Calling something an injustice does more than sort cases into a category: it makes a claim. The genre's own papers show this: they conclude not with a taxonomy but with a call to recognize a wrong, reform a practice, or respond to a group of people (Jenkins 2020; Friedman 2025). Feinberg's account of rights explains why this matters: claiming, he argues, is what lets a person stand up, demand, and hold others accountable. A world without claiming, Feinberg's Nowheresville, is morally deformed however benevolent its inhabitants (Feinberg 1970). Injustice-ascription extends that activity to kinds. A coinage paper makes a standing, general claim on behalf of everyone who falls under the new kind: to coin "K-injustice" is to create a form that anyone in the kind's extension can file.

A minimal contrast shows this verdictive force at work. "Epistemic bad luck" and "epistemic injustice" can sort the very same events. But only the second demands anything of anyone (Fricker 2007 marks the boundary; Dotson 2012 contests exactly where it falls, and the contest itself shows that the suffix carries a demand). I fix two terms for what follows. Uptake is what a claim calls for: recognition and a response, owed to the claimant. Uptake capacity is the finite social ability to supply them. What is owed attaches to uptake; what is scarce attaches to uptake capacity. Some uses of the suffix are merely classificatory despite their verdictive appearance; the analysis targets the practice's central, thick use, the one that makes coining worth wanting.

The first result tells against the Inflation View's metaphor: validity does not dilute. Suppose a regime tortures A, and it emerges that it also tortures B. No one thinks the wrong done to A has thereby shrunk. Recognizing more wrongs does not thin the wrongness of any. The Inflation View can keep its conditional: if coinages consumed validity, dilution would follow. But coinages do not consume validity, because bindingness is not a currency. In the rights domain, Theilen's deconstruction of the inflation idiom makes the same point against the economic metaphors themselves (Theilen 2021). Even the apparent counterexample confirms this. With imperfect duties of aid, more recognized claimants do thin what each receives. But what thins there is fulfillment, not bindingness. Each claimant is still owed as much; what shrinks is how much arrives. The case points at the real scarcity.

One qualification matters: this result defeats only the naive currency reading. Alston's own serious version, which targets the manner of coining rather than coining itself, is not refuted here but relocated; §4 supplies what his forum could not.

Why does the inflation idiom survive its own bad metaphor? Because something nearby really is scarce, and the critics reached for the wrong thing. What is finite is not the validity of claims but the world's capacity to take them up. That capacity is attention, in the well-worn sense in which attending to one thing "implies withdrawal from some things in order to deal effectively with others" (James, quoted in Kitsik 2025). More concretely, it is institutional processing: dockets, curricula, complaint routes, news cycles, training hours; funding, space, and time are the attentional resources of collective agents (Gardiner 2022).

Consider *The Docket*. An ombuds office files complaints under a set of headings, each with its own intake route and triage weight. Add one heading, and the expected uptake of every pending claim shifts. No claim has lost validity; processing has simply been rationed differently. This is a structural fact, not a psychological one. It is the institutional face of what Kitsik calls the commitment mechanism: institutions publicly committed to addressing a category must prioritize what falls under it, which is why a change in categories moves funding, procedure, and research agendas (Kitsik 2025). Institutions organize their rationing by kind. To coin a kind is to enter that rationing.

Uptake capacity is not mere throughput; its allocation can itself be owed. Within the practice's own recent literature, Smith and Archer argue that paying someone less attention than she is due as an epistemic agent is itself a distinct epistemic injustice (Smith and Archer 2020).

The nearest prior work on conceptual change agrees about what is at stake. Kitsik argues that the familiar worry about inflated problem-concepts (that they damage communication and understanding) fails, because linguistic resources are flexible; what changed usage really moves is the community's allocation of collective attention (Kitsik 2025). Kitsik stops there, leaving the verdict to first-order politics: do the newly indicated problems deserve priority? This essay adds a condition that comes before that negotiation and takes no side on priorities. An unbacked coinage cannot even enter the negotiation as an answerable claim-kind.

One might object that attention does not track how many concepts exist: people attend to what is vivid, not to what is named. But concepts channel allocation even so. Kitsik documents two mechanisms. One is the emotional charge of the terms, with a caveat that charge can backfire. The other, more robust mechanism is institutional commitment, which attaches to categories and moves resources whenever categories move (Kitsik 2025). The argument needs no more than this: coinages are a real channel of uptake-capacity allocation, though not the only one.

The practice's founding concept already treats uptake failure as a wrong. Testimonial injustice just is wrongful recognition-failure: a hearer's prejudice deflates the credibility a speaker is owed (Fricker 2007, ch. 1). I extend the insight from the hearer-and-speaker case to the practice's own workings, and I mark the extension honestly as an extension. Even for a reader who refuses it, the next paragraph's argument goes through.

Who, exactly, is disserved when an unbacked kind is coined? There are two distinct parties. Existing claimants come first. The new kind enters *The Docket*'s rationing and dilutes the uptake their claims are owed, without any offsetting gain; what is diluted is attention that was owed somewhere. The coinage's own would-be claimants come second. They are told they are wronged and handed a form that could not be answered even by a willing audience. Under *Ambient Injustice* there is nothing to look for, no one to believe, and no shape a response could take. Whether this second disservice is a wronging, as the practice's own logic suggests, or only a serious waste, I leave open. Even as mere waste, it wastes a good owed elsewhere. That is all my conclusion needs.

One distinction guards all of this. The failure that condemns a coinage is non-uptake rooted in its own structure: unanswerability. It is not the coiner's failure when an answerable claim meets an audience's refusal. That refusal is the audience's wrong. The practice already has a name for it: contributory injustice, the case in which marginalized speakers can articulate their claims within their own resources and dominant audiences willfully refuse uptake (Dotson 2012). Prophetic coinages, coined into predictable refusal, stand vindicated. Answerability, not actual uptake, is the standard. Nor does the point prove too much: only verdictive coinages create claim-kinds; analysis and description demand no uptake. So coinages divide by what their draw on uptake capacity achieves, and the dividing property is the subject of the next section.

## 4. The Backing Condition

Start with a rule too simple to work: a coinage is justified if and only if it names a real bad. *Ambient Injustice* breaks this rule at once. The bad it names is perfectly real; the coinage is still idle. Realness, then, is not what does the work.

Add a clause requiring the coinage to fix whom it wrongs, and *as what*: as knower, as inquirer, or as member of a constructed kind. Suppose our imagined author amends the paper: ambient injustice wrongs its victims "as residents of modernity." A capacity is now named, and the claim still cannot be filed: a named capacity without a mechanism leaves a claim with an owner but no route.

Add the mechanism: the route by which the arrangement wrongs the capacity, such as a prejudicial credibility deficit, a refusal of standing concepts, or a wrongful construction of the kind itself. The next repair meets a hard problem, supplied by the practice's most important cases rather than by anything invented here. Structural wrongs have no culprit at whom a mechanism could point. Fricker says so about her own second concept: "hermeneutical injustice, whether incidental or systematic, involves no culprit. No agent *perpetrates* hermeneutical injustice — it is a purely structural notion" (Fricker 2007, ch. 7 (verify page)). If the final clause demanded a respondent, the condition would disqualify the coinages that matter most. So the third clause must ask for something short of a perpetrator: an articulable shape a response could take, not the identity of a wrongdoer.

The condition can now be stated in full. A coinage "K-injustice" is backed if and only if it fixes (i) the capacity: who is wronged, and as what; (ii) the mechanism: the route by which the wronging works; and (iii) the redress-shape: what responding to the claim would be, where the shape may be shared and forward-looking rather than culprit-directed. In plain words, a backed coinage tells you what to look for, whom to believe, and what helping would mean. Backing, in short, means answerability: clause (iii) demands an articulable response-shape, not institutions currently willing to respond.[^2] The clauses answer to what normative language is for: "an important part of the point of normative language is to help us understand the nature of wrongs, and to direct us towards remedies" (Maitra 2018 (verify page)). Clauses (i) and (ii) serve the understanding; clause (iii) the direction.

What does backing achieve? Begin with the comparative claim, which is all the conclusion needs. Every coinage enters *The Docket*'s rationing; what differs is what the entry yields. A backed coinage yields answerability: claims filed under it can be recognized and responded to. An unbacked coinage draws the same attention and yields nothing. Backed coining does not escape scarcity; nothing does. The difference is that the capacity a backed coinage draws goes to claims that can be answered.

Backed coinages often do more than draw on existing capacity: they create it, and the historical record supports the claim. Testimonial injustice did not merely enter the clinic's triage: it made a recognizable pattern out of a thousand opaque charts, and the pattern entered training and guidelines. A good category bundles detection, address, and treatment-shape, cutting the recognition cost of every future case. The capacity-creation claim is empirical and offered only as a strengthener; the comparative claim carries the argument by itself. Grant the critics their scarcity premise in full, and their conclusion still reverses under the condition.

A test that everything passes is no test. So failure should be exhibited: once in a constructed case, and once from the record. *Ambient Injustice* fails all three clauses by design: no capacity fixed, no route, no response-shape. And history supplies real unbacked coinages. Alston's record includes two examples. The World Tourism Organization proclaimed that "tourism has become increasingly a basic need, a social necessity, a human right." Galtung compiled a "world waiting list" of candidate rights that reached down to the right to sleep. Both show the profile Alston documented, no prior discussion and a vagueness that eased adoption: unbackedness, clause by clause, in the very domain where the inflation worry was born (Alston 1984 (verify page)). Clause-satisfaction is checkable in a coinage's own text; that is why the next section's tests can be run at all.

An economist will reply that the condition tests coinages one at a time while scarcity bites in total: even individually backed coinages could jointly congest triage, as forty thousand diagnostic codes would swamp a clinic that runs well on forty. The reply is the comparative claim again: wherever the total ceiling lies, the ordering holds at every scale. A backed coinage added at the margin yields answerable claims for the capacity it draws; an unbacked one does not. So the condition ranks coinages wherever the ceiling sits. Backed coinages also nest. A stated mechanism can be compared with the mechanisms of existing kinds, so each backed coinage can be located relative to them: contributory injustice sits under epistemic injustice because its mechanism specifies one more way uptake fails. A locatable coinage shares the triage routes of its neighbors instead of demanding a new heading of its own, which slows the congestion the economist fears; an unbacked coinage, stating no mechanism, cannot be located at all. I concede plainly that some absorption limit exists in principle; nothing in my verdict depends on where it lies.

A conceptual engineer will press a different objection: stipulation does not control uptake. What a philosopher writes on the page, the three clauses included, may never become the concept a community actually deploys. Content drifts, garbles, or gets adopted in name only. On Cappelen's account, the process of meaning change is largely "incomprehensible and outside human control" (Cappelen 2020 (verify page)). Concede the gap in full. What follows is not what the objector needs. The Backing Condition is an ex ante condition: it concerns what coiners control at the time of coining, not what happens afterward; due diligence, not a guarantee of results. Pro tanto justification, justification so far as this consideration goes, tracks exactly that. The clauses are also implementation-relevant properties: what makes a concept teachable, filable, and actionable is fixed capacity, mechanism, and response-shape (§3's mechanisms again). Backing is the coiner's best available answer to the implementation gap, not a casualty of it. And a coinage taken up in name only, its content garbled as it spreads, has simply degraded into creep: that is where proliferation and creep meet.

The central argument can now be assembled.

> **The central argument.**
> P1. To coin "K-injustice," in the practice's central use, is to create a claim-kind: a standing form under which members of K can claim. (Defended in the first two paragraphs of §3.)
> P2. Claim-kinds do not consume validity: bindingness does not dilute. (§3, the torture example.)
> P3. Claim-kinds draw on scarce uptake capacity and create claims whose uptake is owed. (§3, *The Docket* through the ignored-claim argument.)
> P4. A backed coinage's draw on that capacity yields answerable claims; an unbacked coinage's draw yields nothing. (This section.)
> C. So backed coining is pro tanto justified and unbacked coining is pro tanto not: proliferation is vindicated when and because its coinages are backed.

## 5. Testing the Practice

Run the condition over the practice's coinages, oldest to newest, filling each clause from the coinage's own text. The verdict on the practice is the sum of these tests.

Take testimonial injustice first. The capacity is the subject as knower. The mechanism is an identity-prejudicial credibility deficit, Fricker's own formula for the central case (Fricker 2007, ch. 1). The redress-shape is the corrective virtue of testimonial justice in hearers, and its institutional cultivation (Fricker 2007, chs. 1, 7). All three clauses are filled by the introducing text, so the coinage passes. The test also shows why the coinage traveled: clinics and courts could run on it.

Ontic injustice is the hard case; take it slowly. Jenkins's definition: an individual suffers ontic injustice if and only if she is socially constructed as a member of a social kind whose construction consists, at least in part, in her being subjected to constraints and enablements that wrong her (Jenkins 2020). The capacity is the subject as bearer of moral entitlements, owed recognition respect. The mechanism is the mismatch between those entitlements and the constraints and enablements that partly constitute kind membership. Following Hampton, Jenkins calls this mismatch a moral injury: damage to the acknowledgement of a person's value. That fills the first two clauses. But clause (iii) looks hopeless at first: no response can undo the social construction of a kind. Jenkins supplies the shape herself, in two tracks. Where the wrongful constraints are contingent, the response is to reform the kind. When England and Wales ended the marital rape exemption in 1991, the social kind *wife* changed, and the label survived. Where the constraints are necessary to the kind, the response is abolition. Nothing that merited the name *slave* could fail to wrong its members (Jenkins 2020). Neither track names a culprit, which is exactly what clause (iii) requires.

So ontic injustice passes, and the working shows something more: backing is achievable off the easy path, and the coinage paper itself does the backing work. Jenkins devotes a section to what the concept gains us; the best coinage papers treat the backing question as their own.

Zetetic wronging is the newest exhibit. The capacity is the subject as a participant in group inquiry. The mechanism is violation (not mere infringement) of zetetic rights to aid, to non-impediment, and to influence over inquiry's course. Those rights are grounded in one interest: the exercise, within cooperative inquiry, of Epistemic Contribution, Fricker's name for the capability of contributing to shared epistemic resources, which Friedman borrows (Friedman 2025). The redress-shape is accountability standing among co-inquirers, norms that institutionalize the influence right (the lab meeting), and exit from the joint venture. Friedman adds, candidly as a conjecture, that distinctive ameliorative treatment is needed. The coinage passes, with an honest note of degree: the ameliorative side is the most programmatic so far, and its backing is largely inherited from the rights-form. The condition grades even where it passes. And Friedman's final section does explicit backing work: his cases differ in extension from testimonial injustice and epistemic oppression, and their remedies do not transfer (Friedman 2025).

Affective injustice shows the condition grading a real coinage in real time. At introduction, the capacity is the victim of oppression as an apt affective responder, forced into what Srinivasan calls "an invidious choice between improving one's lot and justified rage" (Srinivasan 2018). The mechanism is that others treat her apt anger as sufficient reason for dismissal, through a prejudicial stereotype. But Srinivasan's own footnotes leave this mechanism clause open: she is "open to the thought that the category of affective injustice should be narrowed" to counterproductivity rooted in underlying injustice (Srinivasan 2018, n. 44). The redress-shape is programmatic by her own account: the utopian lever, removing the first-order injustice, "is not a resolution that offers much hope," and the realistic lever, dissolving the false opposition of anger and reason, is sketched rather than specified (Srinivasan 2018). The verdict is graded, clause by clause, with later work supplying what was open (Archer and Mills 2019 on emotion regulation). The condition tracks a coinage's maturation.

"-Injustice" itself is a persuasive classification: it borrows a verdict's force at the moment of naming (Stevenson 1938). Backing is what earns the borrowing. Unbacked analysis remains respectable philosophy under a non-verdictive name, such as "epistemic bad luck" or "ambient burden," until the clauses can be filled. This is a naming norm rather than a censorship norm: it says what using the suffix requires, and it forbids nothing.

Turn now to the overall verdict. The flagship coinages pass, and the verdicts form a graded spectrum: full passes at the founding, a thin pass and an open-claused pass among the newer entries, and documented failures in the adjacent domain. The test produces worse verdicts as backing gets thinner. The practice also polices its own suffix from inside. Fricker urges that discriminatory epistemic injustice be kept "bounded and specific," while new phenomena "merit their own classifications" (Fricker 2017 (verify page)). The founder's policy favors bounded proliferation over stretching. Dotson individuates her three forms of epistemic injustice precisely by the order of change each requires: distinction by remedy-shape (Dotson 2012). Byskov introduces his stakeholder condition expressly "to avoid inflating the concept of epistemic injustice" (Byskov 2021 (verify page)). That the celebrated coinages pass does not mean the test flatters a canon. The canon is evidence of the filter: celebration has tracked backing. The condition also binds forward: any future coinage introduced with all three clauses open earns the *Ambient Injustice* verdict, whatever its subject matter and its politics.

The boom, then, has been investment rather than inflation, and the benefit is one benefit seen from two sides. For philosophy, the condition names a discipline internal to the genre: coinage papers that fill the clauses are better papers, and the best already do. For society, backed concepts arrive usable, recognizable at a glance and ready for institutions to run on. The critics' unease is real, but it tracks failures the condition names, creep-degraded uses and the occasional unbacked coinage, not coining as such.

## 6. Objections

The structural objection comes first. The deepest injustices of our time cannot be pinned on any particular wrongdoer. Structural processes are, in Young's words, "produced and reproduced by large numbers of people acting according to normally accepted rules and practices," so their harms "cannot be traced directly to any particular contributors." This is Young's case for replacing the liability model of responsibility, which requires an identifiable wrongdoer, with a social connection model, which does not (Young 2011, ch. 4 (verify page)). Suppose clause (iii) quietly requires a respondent after all. Then the Backing Condition disqualifies structural coinages across the board, and the Investment View ends up conservative, doing what inflation rhetoric usually does: protecting established claims against ones not yet established (Theilen 2021 presses this charge against the rights version).

But clause (iii) was built for exactly this case. It demands a response-shape, not a respondent. Young's social connection model is itself a redress-shape: responsibility that is shared, forward-looking, and political, an obligation "to join with others who share that responsibility in order to transform the structural processes" (Young 2011, ch. 4 (verify page)). A structural coinage backed by a theory like Young's passes the condition; ontic injustice passed in §5 on this same pattern. The clause excludes only coinages for which even a shape-level response cannot be articulated. I grant one thing: where no response-shape can be articulated at all, the coinage is not yet backed. That is the right verdict, the same unease *Ambient Injustice* elicits. The condition asks of coiners only the work the best structural theorists already do.

The equity objection turns the practice's own materials against the condition. Dominant audiences already refuse uptake of concepts coined by marginalized speakers, and that refusal is itself contributory injustice (Dotson 2012). Dotson draws a further lesson, this one about theorists: boundary-drawing can itself perpetrate the wrong. Dotson argues that Fricker's own delimitation of epistemic injustice by "epistemic bad luck" functioned as a closed conceptual structure, one that foreclosed forms of injustice not yet named: as she puts it, "a catchall theory of epistemic injustice is an unrealistic expectation" (Dotson 2012 (verify page)). A backing norm looks like exactly this kind of boundary-policing. It offers a respectable-sounding dismissal, "insufficiently backed," and places the burden on coiners working from inside the very hermeneutical gaps the practice exists to close. On this objection, the Backing Condition would institutionalize the wrong it claims to name.

I offer three replies and a concession. First, the condition assesses coinages, not coiners. Its clauses serve the claimant: capacity, mechanism, and redress-shape are what make a marginalized claim filable rather than easy to wave away, and satisfying it costs a paragraph, not a gatekeeper's permission. Second, the answerability standard blocks the worst abuse outright. Refusing an answerable claim is the refuser's wrong, not the coiner's (§3), so the condition can never be cited against a coinage merely because its audience refuses to listen. Third, the condition is an open structure in Dotson's own sense: it defines no boundary of injustice and forecloses no future form. It conditions coinages one by one, each carrying its own answerability rather than a verdict inherited from a general boundary. Nothing in it says what injustice is or where the category ends, so it has no boundary to foreclose with, and it therefore blocks the closed-structure move Dotson diagnoses instead of repeating it. Finally, one concession: any norm can be weaponized in application. But the answer lies in the norm's own content: a dismissal of a coinage as "insufficiently backed" fails if the coiner can in fact fill the clauses, and that check is more protection than a refused coiner has now.

The genus objection comes from the opposite direction. A checklist is not a theory. Verdictive labels need grounds: an account of what makes all of these cases *injustices*, and backing seems to let coiners skip that genus question indefinitely. On this objection, the Backing Condition is bookkeeping, not theory.

But backing is not a substitute for a genus-commitment; it is that commitment, stated operationally. Each clause forces the coiner to say whom this wrongs, how, and what answering it would be, and that is what having grounds means at the point of coining. The field's own history confirms the order of operations: the genus debate for the epistemic case proceeded productively after the coinages, on the material they provided; Byskov's five conditions systematize the injustices post hoc (Byskov 2021). I concede a further point the objection raises: the boundary between misfortune and injustice shifts historically, and a rigid rule would mislocate it (Shklar 1990 (verify)). That is why clause (iii) asks only for articulability, not for eternal metaphysics and not for current institutional readiness. What response-shapes can be articulated shifts historically too, and the condition tracks that shift instead of resisting it.

Does the condition pass its own test? Its capacity clause names claimants at large, as claimants. Its mechanism is the route §3 traced: unbacked coining manufactures unanswerable claims that dilute the uptake owed elsewhere. Its redress-shape is the condition itself, satisfiable in a paragraph. It passes, and passing is cheap. That is as it should be. A condition this cheap to satisfy cannot function as a barrier; the Investment View forbids nothing, and says only what coining a claim-kind commits a coiner to.

## 7. Conclusion

The question was whether the coining boom in injustice concepts is good or bad for philosophy and society. Answering it required finding what a coinage draws on. Three steps gave the answer. Validity does not dilute, so the Inflation View fails as stated. Uptake capacity is scarce, and its allocation is owed, so the view that coining is costless fails too. The Backing Condition asks a coinage to fix capacity, mechanism, and redress-shape, and it separates coinages by what their draw on uptake capacity yields: answerable claims, or nothing. Tested against the practice, the condition vindicates the flagship coinages, grades the newer ones, and condemns only those history already condemns. The verdict is comparative and defeasible, and it licenses no gatekeeping.

Return, finally, to the two coinages this essay began with. What separated testimonial injustice from *Ambient Injustice* was never sincerity, and never the reality of the bads each names. One told us what to look for, whom to believe, and what helping would mean; the other told us only that things are bad. The difference is backing: the discipline the boom's critics rightly demanded, and the discipline the boom's best work already had.

## 1. The Coining Boom

Compare two coinages. In the first, a patient reports disabling pain after surgery. Her chart describes her as anxious. Her report is discounted, and the discounting tracks her group membership: reports like hers, from patients like her, are systematically read as symptoms rather than testimony. The pattern recurs across thousands of charts. Call this case *Dismissed*. The pattern has a name: testimonial injustice, a wrong done to someone in her capacity as a knower (Fricker 2007). Where the name went, recognition followed: clinical training and guidelines, and a way for the patient herself to say what happened to her.[^1] The coinage worked.

In the second, imagine a paper coining the term "ambient injustice" for the diffuse worseness of life under modern social arrangements. The worseness is real. It is socially produced and unevenly borne. But the concept is deliberately unlocalized: it names no particular capacity in which anyone is wronged, no route by which the wronging works, and nothing that would count as responding to it. The diffuseness is the concept's selling point. Call this *Ambient Injustice*. Something here has misfired, and not because the badness it names is unreal. The difference between these two coinages is this essay's subject.

The specimens are not isolated. Since Fricker (2007), the family has grown quickly: contributory injustice (Dotson 2012), discursive injustice (Kukla 2014), epistemic exploitation (Berenstain 2016), affective injustice (Srinivasan 2018; Archer and Mills 2019), ontic injustice (Jenkins 2020), attention-based epistemic injustice (Smith and Archer 2020), zetetic wronging (Friedman 2025). They form a genre with a shared shape: identify a recurrent bad, name it with the injustice-suffix, and argue that the name fills a gap in our resources for understanding and responding to it (Jenkins 2020 frames the genre in just these terms). Is this practice good or bad, for philosophy and for the societies philosophy addresses?

Two camps have ready answers. The Inflation View says the practice debases its own currency: injustice-talk is a medium of exchange, new coinages cheapen every note in circulation, and the boom is the debasement in progress. The view transposes a worry with a distinguished history in the theory of rights (Alston 1984; Griffin 2008 (verify)). The opposing camp says the worry is confused. Concepts are non-rival; a new concept costs no one anything; and where genuine wrongs go unnamed, naming them is not expenditure but repair. On the practice's own founding theory, leaving people without concepts for their experiences is itself an injustice (Fricker 2007; Nikolaidis 2020). Each camp gets its best form in §2.

I argue that both camps are wrong about what a coinage draws on, and that seeing what it does draw on yields a test that vindicates the practice, conditionally.

> **The Investment View.** Coining an injustice concept draws on a scarce public resource: not the validity of moral claims, which does not dilute, but the capacity of others to take claims up. A coinage is justified when it is *backed* — when it fixes who is wronged and in what capacity, by what mechanism, and what shape a response could take. Every coinage draws on that capacity; a backed coinage yields answerable claims in return, and an unbacked one yields nothing.

The practice's flagship coinages are backed. So the coining boom has been good for philosophy and for society: because of the condition, not the momentum.

Three qualifications bound the claim. The Backing Condition is a standard of success, not a licensing regime: nobody should be stopped from coining. An unbacked coinage does not name an unreal bad: the failure is in the concept's construction, not the world. And the verdict is comparative and defeasible, not a blanket blessing of whatever gets coined next. §2 states the two rival positions at their strongest and separates two phenomena the debate runs together. §3 argues that both misidentify what a coinage draws on. §4 builds the Backing Condition and states the central argument. §5 tests the practice's actual coinages. §6 answers the three strongest objections. §7 concludes.

## 2. Two Views About Coining

Start with a missing distinction. Proliferation is the addition of new kind-concepts: "ontic injustice" names a kind nothing else named. Creep is the stretching of an existing concept's extension, outward to qualitatively new phenomena or downward to milder ones; Haslam calls these horizontal and vertical concept creep (Haslam 2016; Haslam et al. 2020). "Trauma" reaching ever milder adversities is creep; coining "zetetic wronging" is proliferation. The critical literature runs the two together, but they fail in different ways. This essay is about the first.

Creep degrades a concept from inside: as unlike cases accumulate under one term, its mechanism blurs and its own users lose the ability to say what it picks out. The best-documented costs in the critical literature are creep costs: the trivialization findings concern broadened concepts coming to seem less serious (Haslam et al. 2020). Stretching has a second documented cost. When a new wrong is made intelligible by assimilating it to a normatively familiar label, the label imports a flawed analogy and groups together cases whose wrongs differ in kind; Maitra shows both distortions at work in "statutory rape," whose subjects can be left choosing between communicating their experience under the available label and understanding it accurately (Maitra 2018). A fresh kind-concept, coined for the new wrong itself, is the alternative to both failure modes.

Proliferation, by contrast, leaves existing concepts untouched and adds a new one whose credentials can be assessed at birth. Even Haslam's own ledger concedes that the benefits of expansion sit where broadening amounts to naming: "emotional abuse" gave a nameless maltreatment a label under which it could be identified and protested (Haslam et al. 2020). That is, creep does good where it approximates coining. If proliferation is bad, it must be bad in some other way. The Inflation View, restated to target proliferation properly, says how.

In international law, Alston watched new human rights being "conjured up": proclaimed by one body after another with "no prior discussion," their "inordinate vagueness" easing adoption precisely because states could vote for them without committing to anything. He warned that such proliferation "would be much more likely to contribute to a serious devaluation of the human rights currency than to enrich significantly the overall coverage provided by existing rights" (Alston 1984 (verify page)). Note his exact target: "not so much... the proliferation of new rights but rather the haphazard, almost anarchic manner in which this expansion is being achieved" (Alston 1984 (verify page)). Griffin presses the philosophical version: inflationary pressure debases the determinateness of the term itself (Griffin 2008 (verify)). The underlying picture is monetary. Normative language is a common currency; each coinage issues new denominations; unbacked issues devalue every note in circulation.

Transposed to injustice-concepts, the worry is not silly. I grant two things to it at once. Something in the neighborhood really is finite; §3 says what. And some coinage behavior really is extractive: a career can be made by a coinage whether or not the concept was needed. Note where Alston himself ended. He canvassed substantive quality-criteria for new rights and abandoned them, because in an intergovernmental assembly their application would be "quintessentially political" (Alston 1984 (verify page)); he retreated to procedure, his appellation contrôlée. Philosophy's forum is different: a coinage paper can be assessed substantively, clause by clause, and that possibility is what this essay takes up. What the Inflation View owes us, and never supplies, is the identity of the scarce medium. Its own metaphor fails there, as §3 shows.

The opposing view at its strongest begins from non-rivalry. Concepts are not consumed by use: my deploying "ontic injustice" leaves you no less of it. And on the practice's founding theory, conceptual gaps are themselves sites of injustice. When a significant area of one's social experience is "obscured from collective understanding owing to a structural identity prejudice in the collective hermeneutical resource" (Fricker 2007, ch. 7), those who lack the name are disadvantaged in making sense of, and in claiming against, what happens to them. On this view coining consumes nothing. It is repair, and the presumption favors more of it.

The strongest published defense of proliferation adds a rider worth noticing. Nikolaidis argues that concept proliferation disrupts epistemic injustice, but only as one half of a pair whose other half is critical tools for sorting beneficial concepts from harmful ones, a sorting he assigns to concept-users rather than gatekeepers (Nikolaidis 2020). Grant the non-rivalry of concepts; grant that licensing regimes overshoot. But the rider concedes what the official position denies: that sorting is needed. If a new concept costs nothing, why does sorting matter?

So the camps talk past each other at a fixed point: neither says what, if anything, a coinage draws on. Ask that directly. When a philosopher coins an injustice concept, what does the coinage draw on?

## 3. What a Coinage Draws On

Calling something an injustice is not sorting; it is claiming. The genre's own papers show it: they end not in taxonomies but in calls: recognize this wrong, reform these practices, respond to these people (Jenkins 2020; Friedman 2025). Feinberg's classic account locates the value of rights in this very activity: claiming is what lets a person "stand up," demand, and hold others accountable, and a world without it, his Nowheresville, is morally deformed however benevolent its inhabitants (Feinberg 1970). Injustice-ascription is that activity's kind-level analogue, and not by metaphor. A coinage paper performs a standing, general claim on behalf of a kind's members: to coin "K-injustice" is to create a form under which anyone in the kind's extension can file.

A minimal contrast shows the verdictive force. "Epistemic bad luck" and "epistemic injustice" can sort the very same events; only the second demands anything of anyone (Fricker 2007 marks the boundary; Dotson 2012 contests its placement — the contest itself shows the suffix carries the demand). Two terms will be used with discipline throughout. Uptake is what a claim calls for: recognition and response, owed to claimants. Uptake capacity is the finite social ability to supply it. Owed-ness attaches to the first; scarcity attaches to the second. I concede that some tokens of the suffix are thin (mere classification in verdictive clothing); the analysis targets the practice's central, thick use, the one that makes coining worth wanting.

The first result goes against the Inflation View's metaphor: validity does not dilute. Suppose a regime tortures A. It then emerges that the regime also tortures B. No one thinks the wrong done to A has thereby shrunk; recognizing more wrongs does not thin the wrongness of any. Grant the Inflation View its conditional (if coinages consumed validity, dilution would follow) and deny the antecedent: bindingness is not a currency. In the rights domain, Theilen's deconstruction of the inflation idiom presses the same point against the economic metaphors themselves (Theilen 2021). Even the tempting counterexample confirms the line. With imperfect duties of aid, more recognized claimants do thin what each receives. But what thins is fulfillment, not bindingness. The case points straight at the real scarcity. And the verdict should be stated with care: this defeats only the naive currency reading. Alston's own serious version, which targets the manner of coining rather than the coining, is not refuted here but relocated; §4 supplies what his forum could not.

Why does the inflation idiom survive its bad metaphor? Because something nearby is scarce, and the critics reached for the wrong thing. What is finite is not the validity of claims but the world's capacity to take them up: attention, in the well-worn sense in which attending to one thing "implies withdrawal from some things in order to deal effectively with others" (James, quoted in Kitsik 2025), and, more tractably, institutional processing: dockets, curricula, complaint routes, news cycles, training hours. Funding, space, and time, Gardiner observes, are the key attentional resources of collective agents (Gardiner 2022).

Consider *The Docket*. An ombuds office files complaints under some number of headings; each heading carries an intake route and a triage weight. Add one heading, and the expected uptake of every pending claim shifts: not because any claim lost validity, but because processing is rationed. This is structural fact, not psychological conjecture. It is the institutional face of what Kitsik calls the commitment mechanism: institutions publicly committed to addressing a category must prioritize whatever falls under it, which is why category-change moves funding, procedure, and research agendas (Kitsik 2025). Kinds are the units institutions run on. To coin a kind is to enter that rationing.

Nor is uptake capacity mere throughput, normatively speaking; its allocation can be owed. Within the practice's own recent literature, Smith and Archer argue that paying someone less attention than she is due as an epistemic agent is itself a distinct epistemic injustice (Smith and Archer 2020). And the nearest prior work on conceptual change arrives at the same location from the other side: Kitsik argues that the familiar worries about inflated problem-concepts (that they damage communication and understanding) fail, because linguistic resources are flexible, and that what changed usage really moves is the community's allocation of collective attentional resources (Kitsik 2025). Kitsik locates the stake and hands the verdict to first-order politics: whether the newly indicated problems deserve priority. This essay adds a condition that is prior to that negotiation and neutral about its outcomes. An unbacked coinage cannot even enter the negotiation as an answerable claim-kind.

One might object that attention does not track concept-count: people attend to what is vivid, not to what is named. But concepts channel allocation even so. Kitsik documents two mechanisms, the emotional charge of the terms (with honest caveats about backfire) and, more robustly, institutional commitment, which attaches to categories and moves resources when categories move (Kitsik 2025). The argument needs no more than this: coinages are a non-trivial channel of uptake-capacity allocation, not the sole determinant.

The practice's founding concept already treats uptake failure as wronging. Testimonial injustice just is wrongful recognition-failure: a hearer's prejudice deflates the credibility a speaker is owed (Fricker 2007, ch. 1). The practice's founding insight, then, is that recognition can be owed — that a claim met with non-uptake is not merely unlucky. I extend this from the hearer-and-speaker case to the practice's own workings, and I mark the extension honestly as an extension; the next paragraph carries the argument even for a reader who refuses it.

Who, exactly, is disserved when an unbacked kind is coined? There are two definite parties. First, existing claimants: the new kind enters *The Docket*'s rationing and dilutes the uptake their claims are owed, and nothing is gained in return; the diluted good is not loose attention but attention that was owed somewhere. Second, the coinage's own would-be claimants: they are told they are wronged and handed a form that could not be answered even by a willing audience. Under *Ambient Injustice* there is nothing to look for, no one to believe, and no shape a response could take. Whether this second disservice is itself a wronging, as the practice's own logic suggests, or only a serious waste, may be left open: even counted as mere waste, it is waste of a good owed elsewhere; the conclusion I need survives.

One distinction guards all of this. The failure that condemns a coinage is non-uptake rooted in its own structure: unanswerability. It is emphatically not the coiner's failure when an answerable claim meets an audience's refusal. That refusal is the audience's wrong, and the practice has already named it: contributory injustice is the case in which marginalized speakers can articulate their claims within their own resources and dominant audiences willfully refuse uptake (Dotson 2012). Prophetic coinages, coined into predictable refusal, stand vindicated. Answerability, not actual uptake, is the standard. Nor does the point prove too much: only verdictive coinages create claim-kinds; analysis and description demand no uptake. So coinages divide by what their draw on uptake capacity achieves, and the property that divides them is the subject of the next section.

## 4. The Backing Condition

Start crude and repair in public. A first thought: a coinage is justified if and only if it names a real bad. *Ambient Injustice* breaks this at once. The bad it names is perfectly real; the coinage is still idle. Realness, then, is not what does the work.

Add a clause: the coinage must fix whom it wrongs, and *as what* — as knower, as inquirer, as member of a constructed kind. Suppose our imagined author amends the paper: ambient injustice wrongs its victims "as residents of modernity." A capacity is now named, and the claims are still unfilable: against what route? A named capacity without a mechanism leaves a claim with an owner but no route.

Add the mechanism, the route by which the arrangement wrongs the capacity: a prejudicial credibility deficit, a refusal of standing concepts, a wrongful construction of the kind itself. Now comes the hard break, and it comes from the practice's most important cases rather than from any invention of mine. Structural wrongs have no culprit at whom a mechanism could point. Fricker says so about her own second concept: "hermeneutical injustice, whether incidental or systematic, involves no culprit. No agent *perpetrates* hermeneutical injustice — it is a purely structural notion" (Fricker 2007, ch. 7 (verify page)). If the final clause demanded a respondent, the condition would disqualify the coinages that matter most. So the repair must aim lower than a perpetrator: at the shape of a response, not the address of a wrongdoer.

The finished condition can now be stated. A coinage "K-injustice" is backed if and only if it fixes (i) the capacity: who is wronged, and as what; (ii) the mechanism: the route by which the wronging works; and (iii) the redress-shape: what responding to the claim would be, where the shape may be shared and forward-looking rather than culprit-directed. In plain words, a backed coinage tells you what to look for, whom to believe, and what helping would mean. In two words, backing is answerability: clause (iii) demands an articulable response-shape, not institutions currently willing to respond.[^2] The clauses answer to what normative language is for: "an important part of the point of normative language is to help us understand the nature of wrongs, and to direct us towards remedies" (Maitra 2018 (verify page)). Clauses (i) and (ii) serve the understanding; clause (iii) serves the direction.

What does backing achieve? Begin with the comparative claim; it is all the conclusion needs. Every coinage enters *The Docket*'s rationing; what differs is what the entry yields. A backed coinage yields answerability: claims filed under it can be recognized and responded to. An unbacked coinage draws the same attention and yields nothing. The difference is not that backed coining escapes scarcity, since nothing does; it is that the capacity a backed coinage draws goes to claims that can be answered.

Often, backed coinages have gone further and created capacity, and here the record speaks. Testimonial injustice did not merely enter the clinic's triage; it made a recognizable pattern out of a thousand opaque charts, and the pattern entered training and guidelines. A good category bundles detection, address, and treatment-shape, cutting the recognition cost of every future case. This capacity-creation claim is empirical and offered as a strengthener only, with support from the tests in §5; the comparative claim carries the argument alone. Grant the critics their scarcity premise in full, and their conclusion still reverses under the condition.

A test everything passes is no test, so failure should be exhibited, once in a constructed case and once from the record. *Ambient Injustice* fails clause (i), fixing no capacity; clause (ii), fixing no route; and clause (iii), fixing no response-shape, by design. And history supplies real unbacked coinages. Alston's record includes the World Tourism Organization's proclamation that "tourism has become increasingly a basic need, a social necessity, a human right," and Galtung's "world waiting list" of candidate rights down to the right to sleep, proclamations whose documented profile, no prior discussion and a vagueness that eased their adoption, is clause-by-clause unbackedness in the very domain where the inflation worry was born (Alston 1984 (verify page)). Clause-satisfaction is checkable in a coinage's own text. That is why the tests of the next section can be run at all.

An economist will reply that scarcity is aggregate while the condition is marginal: even individually backed coinages could jointly congest triage, as forty thousand diagnostic codes would swamp a clinic that runs well on forty. The reply rests on the comparative claim. Whatever the aggregate ceiling, at every scale the ordering holds: a backed coinage added at the margin yields answerable claims for the capacity it draws, and an unbacked one does not, so the condition ranks coinages wherever the ceiling sits. Backed coinages also nest: the mechanism clause locates each new kind relative to existing kinds, which is why the actual family is a tree rather than a flat list of headings; an unbacked coinage, having no mechanism, cannot nest. And I concede the in-principle absorption limit plainly; nothing in my verdict depends on where it lies.

A conceptual engineer will press a different objection: stipulation does not control uptake. What a philosopher writes on the page, the three clauses included, may never become the concept a community actually deploys; content drifts, garbles, or is adopted in name only; on Cappelen's account the process of meaning change is largely "incomprehensible and outside human control" (Cappelen 2020 (verify page)). Concede the gap in full. What follows is not what the objector needs. The Backing Condition is an ex ante condition on what coiners control (due diligence rather than a guarantee of results); pro tanto justification tracks just that. Moreover, the clauses are implementation-relevant properties: what makes a concept teachable, filable, and runnable-on is fixed capacity, mechanism, and response-shape (§3's mechanisms again). Backing is the coiner's best available answer to the implementation gap, not a casualty of it. And a coinage taken up in name only, its content garbled in transit, is a concept degrading into creep: the essay's two categories meet where implementation fails.

The central argument can now be assembled.

> **The central argument.**
> P1. To coin "K-injustice," in the practice's central use, is to create a claim-kind: a standing form under which members of K can claim. (Defended in the first two paragraphs of §3.)
> P2. Claim-kinds do not consume validity: bindingness does not dilute. (§3, the torture example.)
> P3. Claim-kinds draw on scarce uptake capacity and create claims whose uptake is owed. (§3, *The Docket* through the ignored-claim argument.)
> P4. A backed coinage's draw on that capacity yields answerable claims; an unbacked coinage's draw yields nothing. (This section.)
> C. So backed coining is pro tanto justified and unbacked coining is pro tanto not: proliferation is vindicated when and because its coinages are backed.

Whether the actual practice is vindicated is now an assessable question.

## 5. Testing the Practice

Run the condition over the practice's coinages, oldest to newest, filling each clause from the coinage's own text. The verdict on the practice is the sum of these tests.

Take testimonial injustice first. The capacity is the subject as knower. The mechanism is an identity-prejudicial credibility deficit, Fricker's own formula for the central case (Fricker 2007, ch. 1). The redress-shape is the corrective virtue of testimonial justice in hearers, and its institutional cultivation (Fricker 2007, chs. 1, 7). All three clauses are filled by the introducing text, so the coinage passes. The test also shows why the coinage traveled: clinics and courts could run on it.

Ontic injustice is the hard case; take it slowly. Jenkins's definition: an individual suffers ontic injustice if and only if she is socially constructed as a member of a social kind whose construction consists, at least in part, in her being subjected to constraints and enablements that wrong her (Jenkins 2020). The capacity is the subject as bearer of moral entitlements, owed recognition respect. The mechanism is the mismatch between those entitlements and the constraints and enablements that partly constitute kind membership: a moral injury in Hampton's sense, damage to the acknowledgement of a person's value. That fills the first two clauses. But clause (iii) looks hopeless: no response un-constructs a social kind. Except that Jenkins supplies the shape herself, in two tracks. Where the wrongful constraints are contingent, the response is to reform the kind: when England and Wales ended the marital rape exemption in 1991, the social kind *wife* changed and the label survived. Where the constraints are necessary to the kind, the response is abolition: nothing that merited the name *slave* could fail to wrong its members (Jenkins 2020). Reform where the constraints are contingent, abolish where they are necessary: a response-shape without a culprit, which is what clause (iii) demands.

So ontic injustice passes the test, and the working shows something more. Backing is achievable off the easy path, and the coinage paper itself does the backing work: Jenkins devotes a section to what the concept gains us, asking in her own voice whether it earns its place. The best practitioners treat the backing question as their own.

Zetetic wronging is the newest exhibit. The capacity is the subject as participant in group inquiry. The mechanism is violation, not mere infringement, of interest-grounded zetetic rights to aid, non-impediment, and influence over inquiry's course; the grounding interest is the exercise of what Fricker calls Epistemic Contribution within cooperative inquiry (Friedman 2025). The redress-shape is accountability standing among co-inquirers, norms that institutionalize the influence right (the lab meeting), and exit from the joint venture; Friedman adds, candidly as a conjecture, that distinctive ameliorative treatment is needed. The coinage passes, with an honest note of degree: the ameliorative side is the most programmatic so far, its backing largely inherited from the rights-form. The condition grades; it does not rubber-stamp. And Friedman's own final section does explicit backing work: it argues that his cases differ in extension from testimonial injustice and epistemic oppression, and that their remedies do not transfer (Friedman 2025).

Affective injustice shows the condition grading a real coinage in real time. At introduction, Srinivasan fixes the capacity (the victim of oppression as apt affective responder, forced into "an invidious choice between improving one's lot and justified rage") and a mechanism: others treat her apt anger as sufficient reason for dismissal, via prejudicial stereotype (Srinivasan 2018). But her own footnotes leave the mechanism clause visibly open: she is "open to the thought that the category of affective injustice should be narrowed" to counterproductivity rooted in underlying injustice (Srinivasan 2018, n. 44). And the redress-shape is programmatic by her own account: the utopian lever, removing first-order injustice, "is not a resolution that offers much hope," and the realistic lever, dissolving the false opposition of anger and reason, is sketched rather than specified (Srinivasan 2018). The verdict is graded: clause (i) passes; clause (ii) is open between readings the author herself distinguishes; clause (iii) is programmatic at introduction, with later literature supplying what was open (Archer and Mills 2019). The condition tracks a coinage's maturation, one clause at a time.

"-Injustice" itself is a persuasive classification: it borrows a verdict's force at the moment of naming (Stevenson 1938). Backing is what earns the borrowing. Unbacked analysis remains respectable philosophy under a non-verdictive name ("epistemic bad luck," "ambient burden") until the clauses can be filled. This is a naming norm, not a censorship norm: it says what using the suffix requires, and it forbids nothing.

Now for the overall verdict. The flagship coinages pass, and the graded spectrum (full passes at the founding, a thin pass and an open-claused pass among the newer entries, documented failures in the adjacent domain) shows the test biting where maturity is lower. And the practice polices its own suffix from inside. Fricker urges that discriminatory epistemic injustice be kept "bounded and specific," while new phenomena "merit their own classifications" (Fricker 2017 (verify page)): the founder's policy is bounded proliferation, not stretching. Dotson individuates her three forms of epistemic injustice precisely by the order of change each requires: distinction by remedy-shape (Dotson 2012). Byskov introduces his stakeholder condition expressly "to avoid inflating the concept of epistemic injustice" (Byskov 2021 (verify page)). That the celebrated coinages pass is not the test flattering a canon; it is the canon evidencing the filter, for celebration has tracked backing. And the condition binds forward: any future coinage introduced with all three clauses open earns the *Ambient Injustice* verdict, whatever its subject matter and whatever its politics.

The boom, then, has been investment rather than inflation, and the benefit is one benefit seen from two sides. For philosophy, the condition names a discipline internal to the genre: coinage papers that fill the clauses are better papers, and the best already do. For society, backed concepts arrive usable: they can be recognized at a glance and run on by institutions. The critics' unease is real, but it tracks the failures the condition names, creep-degraded uses and the occasional unbacked coinage, not coining as such.

## 6. Objections

The structural objection comes first. The deepest injustices of our time resist assignment: structural processes are "produced and reproduced by large numbers of people acting according to normally accepted rules and practices," so their harms "cannot be traced directly to any particular contributors." That is Young's case for replacing the liability model with a social connection model of responsibility (Young 2011, ch. 4 (verify page)). If clause (iii) quietly demands a respondent, the Backing Condition disqualifies structural coinages wholesale, and the Investment View lands where inflation rhetoric tends to land: conservative, protecting yesterday's claims against tomorrow's (Theilen 2021 presses this charge against the rights version).

But clause (iii) was built for this. It demands a shape, not a respondent. Young's social connection model is itself a redress-shape: responsibility that is shared, forward-looking, and political, an obligation "to join with others who share that responsibility in order to transform the structural processes" (Young 2011, ch. 4 (verify page)). Structural coinages backed by such theory pass; ontic injustice passed in §5 on this very pattern. The clause excludes only coinages for which even shape-level response is inarticulable. I hold the concession that follows: where no response-shape can be articulated at all, the coinage is not yet backed; that is the right verdict, the same unease *Ambient Injustice* elicits. The condition asks of coiners only the work the best structural theorists already do.

The equity objection turns the practice's own materials against the condition. Dominant audiences already refuse uptake of concepts coined by marginalized speakers; that refusal is contributory injustice (Dotson 2012). Dotson's further lesson is about theorists: boundary-drawing itself can perpetrate the wrong. Fricker's own delimitation of epistemic injustice by "epistemic bad luck" functioned, Dotson argues, as a closed conceptual structure that foreclosed forms of injustice not yet named, and "a catchall theory of epistemic injustice is an unrealistic expectation" (Dotson 2012 (verify page)). A backing norm looks like just such boundary-policing — a respectable dismissal, "insufficiently backed," whose burden falls on coiners working from inside the very hermeneutical gaps the practice exists to close. On this objection, the Backing Condition would institutionalize the wrong it claims to name.

I offer three replies and a concession. First, the condition assesses coinages, not coiners. Its clauses are claimant-serving: capacity, mechanism, and redress-shape are what make a marginalized claim filable rather than easy to wave away, and meeting the condition costs a paragraph, not a gate. Second, the answerability standard blocks the worst abuse outright: refusing an answerable claim is the refuser's wrong (§3), so the condition can never be cited against a coinage merely because its audience will not listen. Third, the condition is an open structure in Dotson's own sense. It defines no boundary of injustice and forecloses no future form; it conditions coinages one by one, each carrying its own answerability. It thereby blocks the closed-structure move she diagnoses rather than repeating it. The concession: any norm can be weaponized in application. The answer to weaponization is the norm's own content (fill the clauses, and the dismissal fails), which is more than the status quo gives a refused coiner.

The genus objection comes from the opposite direction. A checklist is not a theory: verdictive labels need grounds, an account of what makes all of these *injustices*, and backing seems to let coiners skip the genus question indefinitely. On this objection the Backing Condition is a bookkeeping device wearing a theory's clothes.

But backing is the genus-commitment in operational form. Each clause forces the coiner to say whom this wrongs, how, and what answering it would be. That is what having grounds is, at the point of coining. The field's history confirms the order of operations: the genus debate for the epistemic case has proceeded productively after the coinages, on the material they provided: Byskov's five conditions systematize the injustices post hoc (Byskov 2021). I concede the deeper point beneath the objection: the boundary between misfortune and injustice is historically mobile, and rigid rules mislocate it (Shklar 1990 (verify)). That is why clause (iii) asks for articulability: not eternal metaphysics, and not current institutional readiness. What response-shapes can be articulated shifts historically, and the condition moves with it rather than fighting it.

Does the condition pass its own test? Its capacity clause names claimants at large, as claimants; its mechanism is unbacked coining's manufacture of unanswerable claims; its redress-shape is the condition itself, satisfiable in a paragraph. It passes; its cheapness is the point: the Investment View forbids nothing; it says what coining a claim-kind commits a coiner to.

## 7. Conclusion

The question was whether the coining boom in injustice concepts is good or bad for philosophy and society, and the answer required finding what a coinage draws on. Three steps gave the answer. Validity does not dilute, so the Inflation View fails as stated. Uptake capacity is scarce and its allocation owed, so the view that coining is costless fails too. The Backing Condition, which asks a coinage to fix capacity, mechanism, and redress-shape, separates the coinages whose draw on that capacity yields answerable claims from those that draw and yield nothing. Tested against the practice, it vindicates the flagship coinages, grades the newer ones, and condemns only the coinages that history already condemns. The verdict is comparative and defeasible, and it licenses no gatekeeping.

Return, finally, to the two coinages we began with. What separated testimonial injustice from *Ambient Injustice* was never sincerity, and never the reality of the bads they name. One told us what to look for, whom to believe, and what helping would mean; the other told us only that things are bad. The difference is backing: the discipline the boom's critics rightly demanded, and the boom's best work already had.

ALEC WALEN

## TRANSCENDING THE MEANS PRINCIPLE

(Accepted 20 May 2013)

**ABSTRACT.** A robust, if not absolute, prohibition on treating people merely as a means seems to sit at the core of common sense deontological morality. But the principle prohibiting such treatment, the ‘means principle’ (MP), has been notoriously hard to defend: both the subjective, intention-focused and the objective, causal-role-focused interpretations of what it means to use someone as a means face potent objections. In this paper, my goal is not to defend the MP, but to articulate and defend a new principle, which I call the Restricting Claims Principle (RCP), that explains why a person’s causal role is morally significant. The RCP broadens the basic frame of relevant considerations from the MP’s concern with the dyadic relationship between agent and patient to a global balance of patient-claims on an agent. It distinguishes two kinds of patient-claims that weigh in that balance: restricting and non-restricting. In most cases, these can be distinguished as follows: Restricting claims, if respected as rights, would restrict an agent from doing what she could otherwise permissibly do if the claimant (or his property) were absent; non-restricting claims, if respected as rights, would not in that way restrict an agent. Only restricting claims press to make others worse off than if the claimant were absent. The RCP holds that restricting claims must therefore be substantially weaker than non-restricting ones. The claims of those who would be used as a means are non-restricting, while the claims of those who would be harmed as a side effect are restricting. Thus the RCP can account for the same cases (mostly) as the MP, without having to rely on the MP to do so.

A prohibition on using people merely as a means, at least if the harm to them is significant, seems to sit at the core of common sense deontological morality. Consider these archetypes of impermissible acts<sup>1</sup>: framing an innocent person, even if in the hope of quieting a violent mob, angry about an unsolved crime; doing medical research on unconsenting human guinea pigs, even if in the hope of finding a cure for a disease that would otherwise claim many lives; allowing someone to bleed to death, when one could easily save him, even if his organs could then be used to save a number of lives; dropping bombs on innocent civilians far from any legitimate military target, even if in the hope of more quickly winning a war against an unjust aggressor nation. In all four cases, the familiar explanation for why an agent may not perform the acts in question is that she may not treat others merely as a means.<sup>2</sup>

The ‘means principle’ (MP) holds that it is impermissible to cause significant harm to others, or to allow significant harm to befall others, when the harm would result from using them *merely* as a means. Despite the fact that the MP seems to be central to common sense deontological morality, its status as a sound moral principle is in some doubt. A central reason why is that it is difficult to make sense of using another as a means in a way that allows it to be morally significant.<sup>3</sup>

There are two ways to interpret what it means to use another as a means – a subjective, intention-focused interpretation, and an objective, causal-role focused interpretation. On the subjective interpretation, for A to use B as a means is for A to act on an intention in which B figures as a means to some end that she has chosen to pursue. On the objective interpretation, for A to use B as a means is for A to perform an act in which B serves as a causal means by which A brings about an end which might be offered to justify what A does. Both face potent objections.

The objection to the subjective interpretation is that while the MP has implications for how an agent should form intentions with respect to others, in cases like the four cases with which I opened, intentions have only derivative or secondary significance.<sup>4</sup> What fundamentally matters is what is done to a patient (one acted upon by an agent), not what intentions an agent has. The claim that intentions are fundamentally significant misdirects agents to focus inwardly on how they think about others as they act, rather than outwardly on others and what (other than respectful attitudes) they are owed.<sup>5</sup>

I show how my alternative proposal can handle a range of difficult cases in Section III; whether the MP can likewise do so is not my concern. The objection to the objective interpretation is that causal role has no obvious moral significance. If we leave aside questions of how offensive certain treatment is, it is no worse to suffer a harm as means to some good than as a side effect of some other means being used to achieve that good. And it is not obvious why else the strength of a patient's claim not to be harmed or allowed to suffer a harm should turn on his causal role in bringing about a good. It is important to be clear here: I am not denying that a patient's claims might be stronger or weaker depending on whether he has played some causal role either in bringing about a problem that an agent confronts or helping her to address it. But that kind of significance for a patient's causal role is grounded on the idea that a person can be held responsible for what she chooses to do.<sup>6</sup> The objective interpretation of the MP gives a patient's causal role significance independently of her prior moral agency. As T.M. Scanlon put the point: 'being a means in this sense – being causally necessary – has no intrinsic moral significance...'<sup>7</sup>

I propose to explain why a patient's causal role in bringing about a good, *pace* Scanlon, *does* have moral significance. But I will not do so by defending the MP itself. Rather, I will articulate and defend a new principle, which I call the Restricting Claims Principle (RCP).<sup>8</sup> The RCP provides a moral framework for making sense of why causal role matters. This offers an escape from the dilemma the MP faces. I am not claiming that there is no other way to escape this dilemma. Despite the well-known objections rehearsed above, there are still many defenders of one or the other interpretation of the MP.<sup>9</sup> I will not argue against them further here.<sup>10</sup> I claim only that there is good reason to worry that the subjective interpretation is wrong and that the moral significance of causal role is, at best, mysterious, and therefore that there is reason enough to search for a different principle that will nonetheless account for the same intuitions – more or less; I discuss one problematic case where the RCP and MP might be thought to diverge in Section III. This is an important task not only for the intrinsic value of having a better account of some robust moral intuitions, but also because failure to provide a plausible account of these intuitions invites people to theorize that they are mere prejudices.<sup>11</sup> That position, if mistakenly accepted as true, could lead to policies that are profoundly immoral.

I proceed as follows. In Section I, I describe and illustrate the RCP, distinguish it from the MP, and describe how it can be grounded in basic liberal, moral principles. And in the last substantive section, I examine whether the RCP can really account for, as opposed to merely mapping, the intuitions in the cases discussed in the rest of the paper.

### I. INTRODUCTION TO THE RCP

### A. *The Basic Idea Behind the RCP with Illustrations*

The RCP operates within a rights framework. To be clear about my usage, I treat rights as claims that must be respected. The RCP distinguishes two kinds of patient-claims: restricting and non-restricting claims. Here is a first pass at how that distinction works: restricting claims, if respected as rights, would restrict an agent from doing what she could otherwise permissibly do for herself or others (most of my examples will focus on others) if the claimants were absent; non-restricting claims, if respected as rights, would not in that way restrict an agent. The RCP holds that, all else equal, restricting claims are substantially weaker than non-restricting ones.

For the objective version, see Frances Kamm, *Intricate Ethics; Rights, Responsibilities and Permissible Harm* (New York: Oxford University Press, 2007), esp. pp. 138–164.

How are we to understand what it means for a person to be absent? Are we to imagine that he had never been born? Had died? Had not gotten out of bed that morning? Had wandered out of the room a few minutes ago? Different answers might yield very different worlds, with different moral options, yet many of those differences would seem to be irrelevant to assessing the moral permissibility of, for example, using another as a human guinea pig, without his consent. The way out of this problem is to look to the closest possible world in which the person is unaffected by what the agent can choose to do.<sup>12</sup> What we want to know is what difference a claimant's being present, with a claim that had to be treated by the agent as a right, would make to the lives of others, all else being held, as much as possible, the same.<sup>13</sup>

An illustration will help. Consider the notorious 'Trolley Problem', i.e., the problem of explaining why it is permissible to kill one person, without his consent, for the sake of five other people in the first but not the second of the following two cases (named for the means the agent would use).<sup>14</sup>

*Trolley Switch:* a bystander at a switch can throw the switch and thereby turn a trolley that is hurtling down a hill out of control away from five innocent people onto one innocent person on a side track (the 'side-track man'), thereby killing the side-track man and saving five.

*Massive Man:* a bystander at a switch can throw the switch and thereby cause a massive man to topple into the path of a trolley that is hurtling down a hill out of control, thereby killing him and saving five.

That might ground a certain indebtedness of the others to the one and change the ultimate balance of claims. I put these complications to the side, but for an interesting discussion of them, see Øverland's 'Moral Obstacles', Section V.

<sup>13</sup> As will become clear in Section III, we also want to know what difference a person's property being present makes.

<sup>14</sup> The assertion that it is permissible to turn a trolley (or tram) from five onto one was first made by Philippa Foot in 'The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect', reprinted in P. Foot, *Virtues and Vices* (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1978), p. 23. But the second of the two cases and the label, 'The Trolley Problem', were introduced by Judith Jarvis Thomson in 'Killing, Letting Die, and the Trolley Problem', *The Monist* 59 (1976): 204–217.

Most people believe that it is permissible for the bystander to turn the trolley, but not to topple the massive man in the trolley's path.<sup>15</sup> One obvious difference between the two cases is that she would use the massive man as means of saving the five, while she would kill the side-track man as a side effect of saving them. The RCP captures that difference as follows: The massive man's claim is non-restricting because respecting his claim not to be used as a means without his consent as a right would not restrict the bystander from doing what she could otherwise, if he were absent, permissibly do to save the five. Either there is something else she could permissibly do, such as turn the trolley onto another track, in which case respecting his claim not to be killed would not bar her from saving the five, or there is nothing else she could permissibly do, in which case, again, respecting his claim as a right would not restrict her relative to the baseline of his being absent. In contrast, the side-track man's claim is restricting because, if she had to respect it as a right, she would not be permitted to do what she could otherwise, if he were absent, permissibly do for the five, namely turn the trolley onto the other track.

Note that the connection between the massive man having a non-restricting claim and potentially serving as a means to save the five, and the side-track man have a restricting claim and potentially being harmed as a side-effect of saving the five is not accidental. It follows from the fact that a patient would serve as a means of achieving some good that his right not to be so used would *not* restrict an agent relative to his absence. Likewise, it follows from the fact that a patient would be harmed as a side effect that his right not to be so harmed *would* restrict an agent relative to his absence. What the RCP seeks to do is explain why a patient's causal role has moral significance – something left mysterious in the objective interpretation of the MP – by connecting it to the kind of claim the patient has.

So why should this distinction between types of claim carry moral weight? I will provide a more thorough explanation shortly, but the basic idea is that only restricting claims press to make others worse off than they would be if the claimant were absent. In that sense they impose something like negative externalities on others. This is not the case for non-restricting claims, which can be enjoyed without making anyone else worse off than if the claimant were absent. As a reflection of this contrast, the RCP holds that restricting claims must be put in the balance, more or less on a par with competing restricting claims that protect more or less equivalent interests, while non-restricting claims have a kind of priority over, and are therefore appropriately thought of as qualitatively weightier than, competing, otherwise equivalent, restricting claims.

#### *B. The Distinction Between the RCP and the MP*

Having described and illustrated the basic idea of the RCP, I want now to highlight two ways it is importantly different from the MP. First, they differ in terms of the relationships they focus on. The MP describes a moral fact about a dyadic relationship between an agent and a patient. There is a certain kind of action, using another merely as a means, that it takes to be morally extremely problematic. The RCP broadens the basic frame of relevant considerations to a global balance of claims on an agent. What matters for the permissibility of an agent's acts is the balance of the competing patient-claims on her, and her own agent-claims. There is still a dyadic relationship between an agent and each patient with a claim on her. But which rights arise in those relationships depends on a larger balance of competing claims.

Second, there is a difference with regard to the baseline from which the two principles move. The MP describes a high hurdle that supposedly makes it especially difficult to justify actions that involve using another merely as a means. In contrast, the RCP takes what an agent can do to those with non-restricting claims as a moral baseline, and holds that it is *easier* to justify causing or allowing harms to those with restricting claims. The ultimate effect on what an agent may do is more or less the same, but switching the baseline proves to be significant for understanding why the justification for the RCP has deep, plausible roots in our ideas about rights.<sup>16</sup>

#### C. Some Initial Clarifications

I now want to make three points to further clarify how the RCP works. First, the claims of *all* patients with claims on an agent have to be assessed in terms of whether they are restricting or not. Illustrating with the cases in the *Trolley Problem*, the claims of the five must be assessed too, and it turns out that they are restricting. Why? Because if the bystander had to respect their claims to be saved as rights, that would restrict her from doing what she could otherwise, if they were not present, permissibly do for the one. In *Trolley Switch* she would be restricted from refraining from turning the trolley in his direction. And in *Massive Man* she would be restricted from refraining from toppling him in the trolley's path. True, the side-track man's claim, if respected as a right, would limit what she could do *for* others, while the claims of the five would limit what she could *not* do *to* others. But they are all restricting claims in the sense that they all have the potential, if treated as rights, to affect the welfare of others, compared to a background in which those with the claims in question are absent.

Second, the distinction between restricting and non-restricting claims is logically independent of that between positive and negative claims.<sup>17</sup> *Trolley Switch* and *Massive Man* can be used to illustrate most of the possible combinations needed to establish this point. As I just pointed out, the claims of the five in both cases and of the side-track man in *Trolley Switch* are all be restricting, but the five's claims are positive while the side-track man's is negative. *Massive Man* provides an example of a negative claim that is non-restricting. To finish the set of possible combinations, we need a case with a positive claim that is non-restricting. For that, consider a version of one of the four archetypal cases with which I opened the paper, one that I call: *Possible Organ Donor:* Doctor A could save the possible organ donor (POD) by giving him a drug, but she could also allow him to die and then harvest his organs to save others.

<sup>16</sup> Some have thought that the truth of the MP is entailed by our having inviolable rights. See, for example, Thomas Nagel, 'Personal Rights and Public Space', in *Concealment and Exposure* (Oxford University Press, 2002), p. 37. But the idea that our rights are inviolable is consistent with our rights allowing us to be sacrificed fairly readily for a greater good. One needs an argument to get from the general idea that rights are inviolable to the MP or to any other principle.

<sup>17</sup> Failing to see this was one of the mistakes I made in 'Doing, Allowing, and Disabling'.

The POD's claim on the drug is a positive claim for help, but it is also non-restricting because giving him the drug would not prevent Doctor A from doing what she could otherwise permissibly do, in his absence, to save the five, i.e., nothing.<sup>18</sup> In contrast, the claims of the five, that Doctor A allow the POD to die so she can then take his organs, are restricting because respecting them as a right would prevent her from doing what she could otherwise permissibly do for him, namely give him the drug. The fact that his claim is non-restricting, while their claims are restricting, explains the intuitive judgment that it would be impermissible for her to withhold the drug from him, even if doing so would allow them to gain access to his organs and thereby allow her to save their lives.<sup>19</sup>

Third, the RCP concerns the strength of claims only under particular justifications.<sup>20</sup> For illustration of this point, consider the example of a bomber who drops bombs on a target area that contains both noncombatants and legitimate military targets. Suppose she is wondering whether she can drop her bombs on that target area. One justification for doing so invokes the good that she can bring about by killing the noncombatants (e.g., undermining the morale of the enemy). Their claims under that justification are non-restricting because respecting them as rights would leave her no more restricted in her ability to win the war than if they were not there. However another justification for dropping bombs on that target area appeals to the good that would come from hitting an independent, legitimate military target (e.g., a munitions factory in the area). The claims of the noncombatants under that justification are restricting because respecting them as rights would restrict her from doing what she could permissibly do in their absence, namely drop bombs on a legitimate military target.

<sup>18</sup> This case does raise a complication, however. The counter-factual cannot simply be that the patient is unaffected by what the agent can choose to do, for in that case the POD would die, and then presumably his organs could be used to save the five. But the five are ultimately seeking his organs. The relevant counter-factual, therefore, is that A would not be able to deliver his organs to them. Against that background, his having a right that she give him the drug leaves them no worse off.

<sup>19</sup> Recognition of the need to treat such positive claims as akin to the claims of the massive man goes back at least to Foot's introduction of this example in 'Morality, Action and Outcome', in T. Honderich (ed.), *Morality and Objectivity* (Boston: Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1985), p. 25.

This example shows that a patient or set of patients may have a right or rights that would block one particular justification for an act but not another. They have a right that an agent perform or not perform an act if and only if (a) no justification for her doing the opposite succeeds, and (b) their claims are at least partly responsible for this being true. Whether an agent may perform an act for which a successful justification exists while acting for an illicit reason – one that tracks a failed justification – is an interesting question, but one beyond the scope of this paper.

#### *D. Grounding the RCP in ‘Negative Externalities’ and the Idea of an Independent Life*

I want now to say a bit more about what grounds the RCP. I said above that only restricting claims impose something like negative externalities on others. I will now explain what I mean by this, connecting it to what I take to be the deep roots of liberal, moral thought.

As I understand the liberal tradition, going back at least to Kant, the idea that each is fundamentally free to lead her own life – that she is not to be treated as or directed to think of herself as a mere cog in some larger scheme – is one of the basic principles.<sup>21</sup> I call this the autonomy principle. This is not quite Kant’s idea of autonomy, as the emphasis is not on using one’s own reason. But it is close to Kant’s idea of autonomy in two important respects: (a) that we are all ends in ourselves, in the sense that we can each rightfully see our own lives as especially valuable, and are permitted therefore to prefer our own happiness to that of others<sup>22</sup>; and (b) that we should recognize a limit on our pursuit of our own happiness, namely morality, which is the condition on the worthiness to be happy. This second respect is especially telling, as it presupposes that each person is morally entitled to be particularly concerned about her own happiness, but only as long as she does so in ways that pay due respect to others as equal moral beings.

---

<sup>21</sup> The other two principles are (a) the welfare principle: that human welfare matters (in Kant reflected in the highest good, which is that people be happy in proportion to their virtue); and (b) the equality principle: that each is fundamentally equal to all others in terms of her status as a rights-bearer (in Kant reflected in the idea that we are all equally members of the kingdom of ends). Of course, not all liberal theorists treat these three as equally important. John Stuart Mill, for example, gave priority to human welfare and equality, using them to derive the protection for liberty (a close proxy for the autonomy principle). But his derivation is distinctly unconvincing.

<sup>22</sup> I acknowledge that this is quite different from the view taken by many Kantians, that we are ends in ourselves in the sense that we confer value on things by our choices. But I find that idea metaphysically peculiar. At the same time, I think the meaning I offer is (also) present in Kant’s work.

The autonomy principle is normally taken to be concerned only with agency, for only agents exercise choice, which is the paradigmatic autonomous function. But I suggest that we should see the autonomy principle as applying to people both as agents and as patients. It can be extended to people as patients by adding to the idea that people have their own life to *lead* the thought that they also have their own life to *enjoy in peace*. This is a perfectly natural extension. We can start by saying that insofar as an agent is living independently of others, that is insofar as she has not done and is not doing anything to make others worse off (and otherwise has no special responsibilities for them – such as the obligations parents have towards their children, promisors have to promisees, etc.), she should not have to serve the welfare of others at great cost to her. We can then add, in the same spirit, that insofar as a patient enjoys his welfare independently of others, that is insofar as he has no claims that push to make others worse off (and otherwise has no special responsibilities for them), he should not be forced to endure a great sacrifice of his welfare for them. From both the agent and the patient sides of life, one can claim the *fundamental* right to be left alone to lead and enjoy one's own life.<sup>23</sup> Even as a patient, one should not be forced to endure losses much greater than the kinds of small sacrifices for others that can be demanded of agents as a matter of positive duty.<sup>24</sup>

This explains the baseline from which the RCP works. The question is why restricting claims should be treated as less weighty than otherwise equivalent non-restricting claims. The answer is that restricting claims are the patient analog of agential claims to be free to do things that impose costs on others. Just as one cannot expect to have the right, as an agent, to do whatever one wants, no matter how it harms others, so one cannot expect to have rights as a patient no matter how much one's having such rights makes others worse off than they would be if one were absent.

<sup>23</sup> My notion of a fundamental right is meant to track Raz's notion of a core, as opposed to a derivative, right. Joseph Raz, 'The Nature of Rights', in Joseph Raz (ed.), *The Morality of Freedom* (New York: Oxford University Press, 1986), pp. 168–170.

<sup>24</sup> Tadros makes the same connection between the agent and patient perspective. *The Ends of Harm*, pp. 129–131. The connection is not as perfect as he suggests, however; a point I return to at the end of Section III.

The analogy with negative externalities is meant to help here. In classical economic theory, negative externalities arise when an agent engages in some activity – e.g., producing goods for a market – and does not bear all the costs of her production herself. I use the concept of a negative externality, however, in a moral, not an economic sense. Morally speaking, the idea is that it ought to be impermissible to perform an act that imposes *excessive* non-consensual harms on others – however the idea of ‘excessive’ should ultimately be cashed out in a rights framework.<sup>25</sup> The RCP's distinctive twist on this moralized take on negative externalities is to extend the idea from the actions of agents to the claims of patients. Insofar as a person is a patient, he does not *do* anything; he is simply acted upon or left unaffected. But a patient's merely being present can have an effect analogous to an agent's choosing to do something. If a patient's being present with a claim on an agent makes others worse off than if he were not present, then his being present imposes something like a negative externality on them. Since he does not *do* anything – in the cases I consider, he does not even choose to be present – there is no choice to deem impermissible. But his being present with a claim that makes others worse off than if he were not present should nonetheless not be allowed to have an excessively negative impact on the welfare of others. The only way to achieve that result is for the strength of his claim to reflect its impact on others so that it is not excessive.<sup>26</sup>

We can illustrate this idea, and thereby help to unpack it, by considering the side-track man's claim in *Trolley Switch*. His claim not to be killed presses to restrict what the bystander could do for the five, and therefore it presses in the direction of making them worse off than they otherwise would be in his absence. More concretely, his claim not to be killed presses to change their status with respect to the bystander in two ways. First, if he were not present, then, assuming that it would not be too taxing on her to turn the trolley away from them, their normative status would be that of having rights against her to be saved. His being present with a claim not to be killed changes their status so that at best they are still people whom she *may* save. This change in their status makes them worse off. It is not, however, the kind of change we ought to worry much about, because of the symmetry in the case. The side-track man can claim the same thing: had they not been present he clearly would have had a right against the bystander not to be killed by a trolley.

If it does, then it would change the status of the five in a second, more questionable way, so that they *may not* be saved (given the means available to the bystander). In that case, rather than competing for her concern on a par with the side-track man, their interests would simply lose out to his. In that sense, his being present on the side-track would impose something like a negative externality on the welfare of the five in a more problematic way. Not only would the status of the five swing all the way to the other side of the spectrum – from must be saved to must not be saved – the symmetry of the case would now be lost. But it is unclear why that should be. Why shouldn't the claims of the five, instead, cause side-track man's status to go from must not be killed to must be killed? Unless we have some strong, independent moral reason to favor his claim over theirs, we should treat both sets of claims as more or less on a par.

Some may say, at this point, that we *do* have a strong, independent reason for his claim to outweigh theirs, namely that his is negative and theirs are positive. I have no compelling argument against this position. But I will point out that most people think it is permissible to turn the trolley from five onto one, even though not from one onto one. That indicates to me that the intuitive position is that negative claims have some priority over positive claims, but a weak priority that can be outweighed by a greater number of otherwise equivalent positive claims. I also have seen no compelling argument for giving negative restricting claims the kind of priority over positive restricting ones that non-restricting claims have over restricting ones. The suggestion that they do have that sort of priority seems to me to be either a raw intuition or a conflation of the positive/negative dimension with the restricting/non-restricting dimension. Given that according negative claims a strong priority over otherwise equivalent positive claims would lead to more harm than treating them as more or less on a par, I suggest that we accept, at least for the sake of argument, that the priority of negative claims over positive ones is not very strong. If we do, then the fact that the side-track man's claim not to be killed is restricting explains why his claim should be roughly on a par with those of the five with restricting claims to be saved. And that explains why it is permissible to save them, even if doing so results in killing him.

The contrast, again, is the claim of the massive man. He can enjoy his claim not to be killed as a right without making the five any worse off than if he were absent. They, however, cannot enjoy the right to be saved without making him worse off than if they were absent. His being present with a claim not to be killed, therefore, imposes no analogous 'negative externality' on them, while their claims to be saved impose a 'negative externality' on him. Thus his non-restricting claim should not be treated as roughly on a par with their competing restricting claims. His fundamental right to lead and enjoy his own life should give him the right to remain relatively unharmed, even if it means that they must die.

### II. CONCEPTUAL OBJECTIONS AND REPLIES

I consider here three objections to what I have said so far.<sup>27</sup> These are all conceptual objections, as opposed to objections having to do with problems the RCP may seem to have matching common intuitions in particular cases. I discuss case-based objections in Section [III](#).

#### A. *The Numbers Add Up Wrong*

It might be objected that I've misdescribed how the RCP would handle cases in which the numbers add up. The objection runs like this (illustrating with *Trolley Switch*): If we are to suppose that any one of the five have claims that must be respected as rights, then we have to suppose that all of them do, as they are indistinguishable. But if the other four had a right to be rescued, then the claim of the fifth person adds no new restriction on what a bystander could do to avoid harming the side-track man. Therefore his claim is non-restricting. But since that could be said for all of them, it follows that they all have non-restricting claims.

---

<sup>27</sup> In the interest of space, I do not discuss a fourth objection, which is that the absolutist conception of rights that I rely on is confused. Grounds for this objection can be found in Judith Jarvis Thomson, *The Realm of Rights* (Cambridge: Harvard University Press, 1990), Chapters 3 and 4. I believe an adequate reply can be found in Russ Shafer-Landau in 'Specifying Absolute Rights', *Arizona Law Review* 37 (1995): 209–225.

This problem of overdetermination can be handled simply enough by asking how a claim ‘pushes’. If it were to make a difference, what kind of difference would it make? The claim of any one of the five strengthens the normative pressure of their collective claims, making it more likely that the collection of claims will be successfully restricting. This is brought out by imagining that the number on the track straight ahead is only slightly larger than the number on the side-track. In such a case, it is clear that each presents a restricting claim. But the same is true even if the numbers are lopsided. This can be contrasted with a collection of people in a multi-person variation on *Massive Man*. Whether any one of them could stop the trolley by himself, or would add just enough mass to a collection of men so that, as a group, they could stop the trolley, each one still has a claim that, if respected as a right, would not bar the bystander from doing what she otherwise could permissibly have done in his absence – presumably nothing.<sup>28</sup>

#### B. A Claim Can Have ‘Negative Externalities’ and Be Strong

It might be objected that people can have claims that make others worse off, and that that provides no basis for thinking that their claims should be treated as weak. Consider the case of a rich woman who left all of her money to her niece and none to her nephew. Suppose that if she did not have a niece, she would have left all of her money to her nephew. The presence of the niece, therefore, made the nephew worse off than if she did not exist. Yet it would be unreasonable to suggest that the niece's claim to her inheritance is undermined by this fact. Rather, even if fairness were to call on her to share her inheritance, she has a *right* to keep as much of it as she wants.

---

<sup>28</sup> What if a massive man were tied to a boulder, such that the bystander couldn’t topple the boulder without toppling him as well? Then his claim *might* be restricting. Whether it would be restricting would depend upon his claim over the boulder. This is the kind of issue I discuss in Section III. I am grateful to Kai Draper for bringing this possibility to my attention.

I can only partially respond to this objection at the moment; more will be said about property rights in Section III. But the key conceptual point is that the RCP is not meant to attack property notions. And in a property-respecting context, we should note that neither the nephew nor the niece had a right to their aunt's property; the aunt was at liberty to give it to either of them, to both, or to neither. It is true that if the niece's claim to inherit were respected as a right, that would have made the nephew worse off, and the same is true in the reverse. In that sense, they both had restricting claims to inherit. But that is irrelevant because neither had a claim that impacted the aunt's freedom to dispose of her property as she wished. Moreover, as long as the transfer of property from the aunt to the niece was morally and legally legitimate, the property is now the niece's to dispose of as she pleases. Thus the case is completely unlike the *Trolley Problem* cases where the claims of the patients substantially determine what the agent can do.

#### C. *The Baseline Is Wrong*

One might object that there is no reason to take absence as the right baseline when asking what difference it makes if a person's claim counts as a right. One could just as well use a baseline in which the person lacks the claim in question. If we use that baseline, however, then the massive man's claim would be restricting. This is because the bystander could permissibly use his body to stop the trolley on that baseline, but if she had to respect his claim as a right, then she couldn't use his body in that way. In other words, if we use that baseline, his claim not to be harmed presses to restrict what she could otherwise permissibly do with his body, just as the side-track man's claim not to be harmed presses to restrict what she could otherwise permissibly do with the trolley switch.<sup>29</sup> This objection brings out an important feature of the RCP, namely that it depends on the thought that patients have prior claims over their bodies, claims that press to restrict agents from *using* their bodies. The worry is that this means that the RCP cannot ‘transcend’ the MP, nor can it help us to get past the basic dilemma confronting the MP. Instead, it suggests that the RCP trades on the MP, leaving us still wondering what is so special about using someone’s body, as opposed to merely harming it or allowing it to be harmed.

---

<sup>29</sup> I am grateful to Michael Otsuka for pushing me, more than once, to develop this point.

The way around this problem is to embrace the thought that what an agent can use is morally significant. The world an agent confronts is fundamentally divided up into things that she can presumptively use – things that she owns, things others own but that they have given her permission to use, things that are in the public domain, etc. – and things that belong to others and are presumptively beyond her freedom to use, at least if harm will result to the owners. This fact follows from the pervasive presence of property notions, a moral feature of the world that, as just noted, the RCP takes as a given. Agents should understand that their baseline freedom to use things to achieve their ends depends on whether the things they want to use are presumptively available for them to use or not. And it should then be no surprise that it is hard for an agent to justify using another’s body – and his property in general; a point to which I return in Section III – if he will suffer serious harm as a result. But saying that much should not be confused with endorsing the MP. Something else needs to be said to explain why claims not to be harmed or left to suffer when one’s body is *not* being used as a means are not equally strong.

This is the key point: the RCP does not trade on the MP to reach the conclusion that claims not to be harmed or left to suffer as a side-effect are weaker than claims not to be harmed or left to suffer as a means. It reaches that conclusion by recognizing (a) that once a property respecting baseline is in place some claims not to be used are restricting and others are non-restricting, and (b) that restricting claims should be weaker than non-restricting ones. They should be weaker because they have the effect of pushing to make others worse off than they would be if the agent were permitted to use the means normally, in the baseline condition, available to her.

To be clear, I am not suggesting that property rights are at stake only when an agent *uses* a patient's property, including his body. Property rights can restrict not only use of another's property, but causing harm to another's property. That is why we *need* the RCP. Otherwise, we might start with the baseline thought that an agent may not, without another's consent, use his body and thereby kill him, and move from there to the claim that she may not, without his consent, cause harm to his body as a side-effect of pursuing some good and thereby kill him. What blocks this move is the RCP.

With this we now can see why I said above that the different baselines from which the MP and the RCP 'move' prove to be important for understanding why the latter can explain what the former leaves mysterious. The MP is typically brought into play in contemporary moral, political and legal philosophy as a kind of side-constraint, preventing the sorts of trade-offs that are otherwise thought acceptable. But the MP runs into a dilemma when it comes to explaining why claims not to be harmed or left to suffer, when used as a means, should be particularly strong. The RCP treats claims not to be treated as a means and thereby harmed or left to suffer as the baseline. It gets that baseline from the idea that the world is normatively divided up by property rights. But it then explains why claims not to be harmed or left to suffer as a side-effect are relatively weak. It is because they push to restrict what an agent could otherwise, using the baseline of things normally available to her, permissibly do for others.

### III. HARD CASES AND REFINEMENTS

In this section, I consider how the RCP can handle some difficult cases. The first case pushes for clarification regarding how the RCP would handle conflicting non-restricting claims. It may also drive a wedge between the RCP and the MP. The second demonstrates the robustness of the theory in terms of providing substantive, even counter-intuitive, answers to normative questions. The third introduces an important refinement already mentioned, namely that the relevant baseline for the RCP includes the property as well as the bodies of patients. The fourth brings out an asymmetry between agent claims not to have to treat themselves as mere tools of the greater good and non-restricting patient claims not to be harmed for the sake of the greater good.

### A. The Loop Trolley

The *Loop Trolley* builds on *Trolley Switch*, adding one significant change: the tracks do not continue to diverge; rather they circle back to form a loop. In Judith Jarvis Thomson’s original formulation we are to imagine:

that the five on the straight track are thin, but thick enough so that although all five will be killed if the trolley goes straight, the bodies of the five will stop it, and it will therefore not reach the one. On the other hand, the one on the right-hand track is massive, so massive that his body will by itself stop the trolley, and the trolley will therefore not reach the five.<sup>30</sup>

Many people think this case (Fig. 1)<sup>31</sup> is more like *Trolley Switch* than *Massive Man*.<sup>32</sup> That is, many people think it is permissible for the bystander to turn the trolley away from the five and onto the one. I agree. But it is *prima facie* puzzling that it should be permissible to do so. The bystander would use the massive man as a means to save the five in *Loop Trolley* just as much as in *Massive Man*. Thus it would seem that his claim not to be killed is non-restricting and must be respected. My first task, then, is to explain how the RCP would handle the case.

Fig. 1.

<sup>31</sup> This is a reproduction of Thomson’s original figure, except that I rotate the figure 180 degrees because it is more intuitive to me to represent the higher point as being nearer the top of the page.

<sup>32</sup> One study indicated that people actually split about 50–50 on whether turning the trolley is permissible in this case. Mark Hauser, *Moral Minds* (Ecco: New York, 2006), pp. 128–129. My informal survey of philosophers shows that they are more prone to accept Thomson’s judgment. And there are at least some reasons, having to do with experience with hypotheticals, to think that the judgments of philosophers should be preferred. See Thomas Grundmann, ‘Some Hope for Intuitions: A Reply to Weinberg’, *Philosophical Psychology* 23 (2010), pp. 500–502.

We can start with the observation that, despite the asymmetries entailed by the trolley heading towards the five and not the one, and there being five on one side of the loop and one on the other, the situation is, in an important way, symmetrical: all parties have, I will argue, *non-restricting* claims. The one's claim not to be killed is non-restricting because without him there is nothing that the bystander could do to save the five. I believe that we should also treat the five as having non-restricting claims, but it will take a little extra work to show why. Their situation is not quite like that of the one because we cannot say that without them she could not save the one – he does not need her to save him as things stand.<sup>33</sup> Nonetheless, he does face the threat of the trolley, and he relies on the five to save him. If they were not present, he would die. It follows that if she had to respect their claims to be saved as a right, he would be no worse off than if they were not present.

To find that their claims are non-restricting, we have to modify my initial presentation of the concept of restricting and non-restricting claims. I originally framed it in terms of how the claims would affect what an agent could permissibly do for others. Since there is nothing that he needs her to do for him, this formulation will not capture what seems morally relevant in this case. The revised version states that restricting claims, if respected as rights, would restrict an agent from doing what she could otherwise permissibly do for herself or others if the claimants were absent, or would require her to take an action that would make others worse off than if the claimants were absent; non-restricting claims, if respected as rights, would not in that way restrict an agent or require her to take an action that would cause others to be worse off than if the claimants were absent. This revised version is less elegant, and I spell it out only here, but it captures more completely the thought, articulated above, that the essence of non-restricting claims is that they, unlike restricting claims, can be respected without causing anyone else to be worse off than if the claimant were absent. And it is clear, on this version, that the five have non-restricting claims.

---

<sup>33</sup> I am grateful to Jeff McMahan for pointing this out.

As we saw above, both positive and negative claims can be restricting or non-restricting. Since both sets of claims are non-restricting, both are at full strength. This makes them effectively like the claims in *Trolley Switch*, insofar as the claims on both sides are of the same restricting or non-restricting type. If the bystander can permissibly choose to turn the trolley in *Trolley Switch*, then it seems that she can permissibly do the same in *Loop Trolley*.

One objection to this line of analysis is that when justifying the RCP I said that insofar as someone has a non-restricting claim he should not have to make a great sacrifice of his welfare for the sake of others, but here I am suggesting that the massive man may be killed for the sake of the five, and the five may be allowed to die for the sake of the massive man. This seems inconsistent.

My response is that while these claims are non-restricting, they do not reflect the sort of independence of the claimant normally found with non-restricting claims. Both sides in *Loop Trolley* are in an interdependent relationship that undermines the significance of their having non-restricting claims. This is not inconsistent with the RCP. Rather, it draws on the RCP's normative underpinnings. The deep idea justifying the RCP is that a person who has a non-restricting claim has a claim that does not press to make others worse off than if he were not there. In this sense, he enjoys his welfare independently of them. Given that each has his own life to lead and enjoy, he can appeal to his fundamental right not have to make a great sacrifice for them. But the massive man and the five in *Loop Trolley* do not enjoy their welfare independently of each other. Each side depends on the other for his or their lives. Given their interdependence, it again seems fair to treat their claims as roughly on a par, as they would be if both sides had restricting claims.

As indicated above, I think this is a case with respect to which the RCP comes to a somewhat different conclusion than the MP. The MP may be able to take the symmetry in the case into account just as well as the RCP.<sup>34</sup> But the MP seems to present the bystander with a tragic dilemma. She faces a choice of performing one of two acts, both of which are impermissible. They are both impermissible because the MP is not in the business of making comparisons; it says, at least when the harm at stake is substantial, that certain actions simply may not be performed.<sup>35</sup> But that is not how most people judge the *Loop Trolley*. It does not seem to present the bystander with a dilemma in which whatever she does, she wrongs someone. It seems to present her with a choice very much like the choice in *Trolley Switch*, in which whatever she does, she acts permissibly. The RCP, because it bases permissibility on a balance of all claims, rather than an assessment of dyadic relationships, can avoid implying that this case should feel more dilemmatic than *Trolley Switch*.

### B. Variations on the Loop Trolley

One implication of the prior account of *Loop Trolley* is that if the loop is *not* symmetrical then what is permissible should be different. The RCP's implications in one such case seem to conflict with common (or not-uncommon) intuitions. But I argue that the RCP is better supported than any conflicting raw intuitions in these cases. To see why, consider these two variations, depicted in Figs. 2 and 3.

In Fig. 2 the bystander would need to turn the trolley onto the side loop where it would hit the massive man to save the five, but she does not need to hit the five to save him. In fact, absent her choosing to turn the trolley onto him, he is not threatened, and his claim on her is simply a claim not to be killed. It seems to me that this case is straightforwardly equivalent to the original *Massive Man* case, and as a result she may not turn the trolley onto the massive man.<sup>36</sup>

But the pressure on the bystander to turn away from the five should be stronger in this case than in *Trolley Switch* because the five now have non-restricting claims to be saved. $$(1) P \gg RP$$

What about the relationship between RN and RP? It is clearly different because it seems permissible to turn the trolley in *Trolley Switch* if those with restricting positive claims outnumber those with restricting negative claims. But to reflect the fundamental right to lead and enjoy one's own life, negative claims must have some priority over positive claims. The reason is this: if one person finds himself in a situation in which he is already harmed or threatened with harm (and has a positive claim for help), that is first and foremost *his* problem. Others who have no special connection with him may owe him some aid, but morality may not treat his luck as pooled with that of others who have no special relationship with him and are not responsible for his plight. The fact that they all have non-restricting claims does not completely erase the moral relevance of some claims being positive and others being negative. Therefore, if there were only one person on the track straight ahead and one on the side-track, it would be impermissible to turn the trolley.<sup>38</sup> This priority, one that can be outweighed if the numbers are uneven, is reflected in this statement:

### C. Property and Property-Like Cases

It is now time to address a family of cases that the RCP would get wrong if it reflected only a patient's causal role. My point in this subsection is not to question the moral relevance of causation, but to pay attention to *what* has to play a causal role. I will argue here that it is not only the patient's body that matters for the RCP, but her property, and maybe even things over which she has a property-like claim.<sup>41</sup> Thus the normative baseline for the RCP is not simply what the agent would be able and permitted to do if the patient were not present, but what she would be able and permitted to do if the patient, and in some ways if his property, or even things over which he has a property-like claim, were not present or available to be used by her.<sup>42</sup>

<sup>41</sup> Jonathan Quong articulates a similar position in 'Killing in Self-Defense', *Ethics* 119 (2009): 507–537, 526–530.

For a case in which an agent may not use another's property, consider:

*Drug Owner:* To save the lives of five people, Doctor A needs to give them a certain drug. But all the readily available supply of that drug is owned by one person, Patient B. Moreover, B is not simply hoarding the drugs; he bought that much of the drug because he needs all of it to live.

It seems to me clearly impermissible for A to take B's drugs. And the reason, I suggest, is that, as noted above, the world an agent confronts is fundamentally divided up into things that she can presumptively use, and things that belong to others and are presumptively beyond her freedom to use, at least if harm will result to the owners. An agent should take the baseline of things she is presumptively *not* free to use to include not only the bodies of others, but the property of others. In this spirit I want to embrace Thomson's thought 'that people's bodies are their First Property, whereas everything else that they own – their houses, typewriters, and shoes – is their Second Property'.<sup>43</sup> Second Property is different from First Property in some important respects. Second Property is conventional in the sense that it is clearly defined and usable only in a legitimate legal order, and legal orders can differ in how they assign and regulate property rights. In contrast, First Property has a kind of natural law priority that legal orders are bound to respect. Additionally, First Property is not alienable in the way that Second Property is. But both frame the sorts of resources that an agent should take herself to have at her disposal.

To see how this applies in *Drug Owner*, start by noticing that A's proposed use of B's drugs is not the kind of use of another's property that is normally permissible – it is not, for example, like walking on another's property to ring his doorbell. The baseline world for A's choice, then, is a world in which she does not have the option to take B's drugs. Treating B's claim to her drugs as a right does not make the five worse off than they would be in the baseline world. Given some qualifications to be introduced immediately below, it follows that B's claim to keep her drugs is non-restricting.

<sup>42</sup> The RCP is not unique in terms of being a moral distinction that rests on a physical distinction, where the physical distinction itself is used in a way that reflects broader moral categories. The distinction between harming and allowing another to suffer a harm also works this way. See Kai Draper, 'Rights and the Doctrine of Doing and Allowing', *Philosophy and Public Affairs* 33 (2005): 253–280.

<sup>43</sup> *The Realm of Rights*, p. 226.

It is important to be clear that I am not saying that an agent may never use another's property without his consent. There are three types of cases in which an agent may do so: (1) when the good to be achieved is large and harm to the owner is small; (2) when the property claim yields to other considerations, reflecting the way that Second Property rights are only a useful social convention; and (3) when, despite the fact that the agent would cause a serious harm to the owner, the harm is not directly connected to the interest protected by the property or property-like claim. I will explain each idea in turn.

We already encountered the first type of case in Section I, when I introduced the fundamental right to enjoy one's own life. I analogized an agent's claim not to have to serve the greater good with a patient's fundamental right to have his non-restricting claims treated as rights. The corollary of that analogy is that just as an agent might have to take some steps to serve the greater good, as long as the sacrifice to her is not too big, so a patient might have to accept relatively small harms to goods protected by non-restricting claims for the sake of the greater good. For example, just as the massive man might have to exert himself somewhat to save the five, he might have to endure slight harms, such as suffering a bruise, for the sake of the five.<sup>44</sup> And we can now extend that point to loss of property. A cabin owner cannot complain that his rights are violated if a hiker in need breaks into his cabin to survive a storm.<sup>45</sup> And a while a drug owner may not be asked to give up his whole supply of drugs if doing so would cost him his life, he may be asked to give up some supply of drugs to save the lives of others if the cost to him would be, say, some days of discomfort.

<sup>44</sup> The relationship between the sacrifices that can be demanded of agents and that can be imposed on patients with non-restricting claims will be discussed in the next subsection.

<sup>45</sup> This example comes from Joel Feinberg, 'Voluntary Euthanasia and the Inalienable Right to Life', *Philosophy and Public Affairs* 7 (1978), p. 102.

The second type of case reflects the fact that Second Property, or property, is conventionally defined and regulated. Accordingly, we might want to say that a just set of regulations would not make the drug owner's property rights so absolute that he can keep his drugs no matter how great the need of others. We might want to say that the conventions regarding ownership of drugs should cause the owner's claim to exclude others to yield if the collective needs of others are great enough. This would distinguish the drug owner from the potential organ donor, as I don't think we want to say that the latter's claim over his organs would ever yield to the collective needs of others. But this difference would only open up in extreme cases. In the five-to-one cases we have been considering, it seems to me that both cases should be handled the same: the non-restricting claims of the one trump the competing positive claims of the five.

The third type of case is best explained with an illustration. Consider the following variation on *Trolley Switch*:

*Trolley Switch Tool*<sup>46</sup>: In order to throw the switch, the bystander needs to use a tool. She sees one lying nearby that she knows belongs to the side-track man. If she doesn't use his tool, she can't save the five, but if she uses it, she will kill him.

Intuitively, this case is more like *Trolley Switch* than *Massive Man*. But the question is, why doesn't the fact that the side-track man owns the tool give him the same right not to be harmed as the drug owner? I think the most plausible answer is that the harm of being hit by the trolley is not directly connected to the interest we normally take to be protected by a property claim over an object like a tool. That interest is the interest in having access to the tool when it is wanted and excluding others from use. But given that the tool is an ordinary object – let us suppose it cost \$50 at a hardware store – that interest is the kind of small interest that could be outweighed by the interest of the five in accordance with the first exception. Moreover, we readily conceive of that interest as distinct from his interest in his life. As one would normally imagine the case, he did not buy the tool to protect his life, the way the drug owner did – if he did, and if the bystander's use would deprive him of that benefit, then my judgment is that the case is no longer like *Trolley Switch*. Nor is his property interest dependent on his not being harmed by the use another might make of it – an interest whose relevance we will come to next, when dealing with property-like claims. His interest is more mundane, and distinct from the interest he has in not being hit by a trolley. As it turns out, the interests come together in this case. But the connection is so contingent, so unconnected to his basic property interest in his tool, that it seems fair to keep the interests separate. Accordingly, he has a non-restricting but easily outweighed claim to exclude the bystander from using the tool, and a restricting claim in not being hit by a trolley that is distinct from his property interest in the tool and outweighed by the positive claims of the five.

I turn now to the issue of property-like claims. Towards that end, consider:

*Rescue Ahead:* Doctor A is on a train rushing down the line to save five who are in imminent danger of death. In the distance ahead she sees someone trapped on the track (the ‘trapped-ahead man’). Unless she stops the train, the trapped-ahead man will be killed. If she stops the train, however, the rescue mission will be aborted and the five will die.<sup>47</sup>

Many people think that she may not allow the train to continue. If they are right, then the RCP might seem to be in trouble. For the trapped-ahead man’s claim seems to be restricting, just like the side-track man’s claim in *Trolley Switch*. A does not need him to rescue the others; she could just as well save them if he were not present. His claim not to be hit should therefore be just as easily outweighed by the claims of five to be saved as the side-track man’s.<sup>48</sup>

The solution the RCP can offer rests on the fact that his claim to the track on which he sits is a property-like claim. To be sure, he does not own the track – he can’t sell it, or even choose to stay put given the option to move – so there is no straightforward application of property notions to his case. Nonetheless, his claim over the track functions *like* a property claim. Assuming he cannot move on his own, and is not to blame for being stuck on the track, he can claim: ‘Unless and until I can move or be moved, I have a right to occupy this track which preempts other uses of the track’. If we grant him this kind of priority over the use of the track, then it is perfectly appropriate to say that A’s use of it is beyond the baseline of things she can presumptively use. It follows that treating the trapped-ahead man’s claim to the track as a right would not make the five any worse off than if he *and that stretch of track* were not physically present or accessible to A. This would explain why A must respect his claim to not to be killed as a non-restricting claim.

I want to end this sub-section by discussing one more case that shows not how the RCP must work, but a range of ways that it *can* work. Consider:

*Massive Man on the Side Track.* The trolley is hurtling down the hill at five, and the bystander can divert it onto a side-track where it will kill only one. The reason she can turn it onto the side-track is that the one sitting on the track is a massive man who has depressed the track. If he were not there, the switch would not work, and she would be unable to save the five.

This case can be assessed in two ways. On the one hand, it can be analogized with *Massive Man*. If he were absent, she would not be able to save the five, and thus his claim not to be hit should count as non-restricting.<sup>50</sup> On the other hand, it can be analogized with *Trolley Switch*. The massive man on the side-track has no claim with regard to the fact that he makes the switch work; it is simply a given of the situation. The only thing the agent can do is take advantage of that fact or not. If she had to choose whether to put him or keep him in that position, then the harm he would suffer as a result would have to be taken into account when weighing his claim not to be so used, and his claim would be like that of the massive man who is toppled into the path of the trolley. But given that she would take advantage of a fact that is true regardless of what she chooses, she should regard his claim as concerning not the fact that he plays a role in making the switch work, but only the harm that would befall him if she uses the switch. In other words, his claim should be taken to be restricting. I do not mean to imply that there is no way to determine which of these is the better reading of the case. But the RCP itself does not clearly point to one reading over the other.<sup>51</sup>

### *D. An Asymmetry Between the Agent and the Patient Perspective*

In developing the RCP, I have relied on an analogy between an agent's claim to lead her own life, without treating herself as a tool of the general welfare, and a patient's claim to enjoy his own life in peace, without being harmed for the general welfare. Both agents and patients can claim the benefit of this fundamental right only insofar as they are leading or enjoying their lives independently of others. They may have to make large sacrifices, or endure large harms, but only if they are in special relationships with others, or are doing or have done things that make others worse off, or have claims that press to do the same. Here, however, I want to draw attention to one way in which the analogy is not complete. To see the gap between the agent and the patient point of view, consider this variation on *Trolley Switch Tool*<sup>52</sup>:

*Heirloom Tool:* A bystander sees a trolley hurtling down the hill towards five innocent people. She sees a switch that would allow her to divert the trolley onto an *empty track* where no one would be harmed. She tries to throw the switch, but it is stuck. She realizes, based on prior experience with similar switches, that the only way to make it work is to hit it with a hard, heavy object. The only object available is an heirloom vase owned by someone else. She knows that the vase was made by the owner's grandfather, and that he counts it as his prize possession. If she hits the switch with the vase, the vase will shatter.

<sup>51</sup> This is not the only sort of intermediate or hybrid case that can be developed, for which the RCP's answer is not clear. But artificial clarity in a moral theory is not a virtue.

<sup>52</sup> I am grateful to Mark Greene for helping me to see the need to address this gap.

It seems intuitively clear to me that the bystander may proceed to use and destroy the owner's vase to save five people. But it also seems at least plausible to me that the owner would *not* be required to sacrifice the vase himself. We might think that he *should* be willing to sacrifice the vase for the sake of five, but that is not the same as saying that he *must* sacrifice it. Assuming that he would be permitted not to sacrifice it, the question is: Why should the owner, in the role of agent, have the right not to sacrifice his vase, but in the role of patient lack the right to bar the bystander from sacrificing his vase?

The answer, I believe, rests in an asymmetry in the notion of autonomy. As I noted in Section I, the dimension of choice applies to us only as agents, and this gives agents an extra degree of concern with autonomy. What this extra concern with autonomy opens up for agents is the right to do wrong. This right has, unfortunately, ever since Jeremy Waldron's seminal article, been taken to refer to a right not to suffer interference that would guarantee that one does not do wrong.<sup>53</sup> That interpretation of the right is oddly and wrongly *patient* centered. It treats the right as a claim on others, a claim against their agency. It misses the idea, there in Waldron, that there are ways we can permissibly 'fall short of the standard required by some ideal or principle which is conceived as a constraint independent of [the] moral rights [of others or of justice more broadly]'.<sup>54</sup>

To unpack that last point, we can start with the thought that if one is to take rights seriously, then one has to accept that there are some principles that one must respect as an agent. Chief among those is the prohibition on violating the rights of others or of committing any injustice. Insofar as we frame for ourselves the Hohfeldian question: 'Do I have the moral privilege to do X?' we should acknowledge that the answer is no if another has a right that we not do so, or if doing so would be unjust. But what if it would be unkind, uncivil, selfish, or the like? We may know that such actions fall short of appropriate moral ideals or principles, and yet want to hang onto the thought that we have a privilege to perform them.

<sup>53</sup> See 'A Right To Do Wrong', reprinted as Chapter 3 in *Liberal Rights* (Cambridge University Press, 1993). See also, e.g., Ori Herstein, 'Defending the Right To Do Wrong', *Law and Philosophy* 31 (2012): 343–365; and Gerhard Øverland, 'The Right to Do Wrong', *Law and Philosophy* 26 (2007): 377–404.

<sup>54</sup> 'A Right To Do Wrong', p. 67. I insert injustice as a broader category because there may be certain injustices, having to do with taking unfair advantage of the rules by which others, for the most part, live, which violate the rights of no one in particular.

Having one's own life to lead suggests that one should have such a privilege to choose to indulge in vice. Vice is primarily the concern of the person who has it; it is the moral quality of her life that is primarily at issue. This freedom to choose it, then, must be not just freedom from external interference – though it is that as a secondary matter – but a freedom from the inner voice of morality telling one that certain choices are simply impermissible.

We can now apply those ideas to *Heirloom Tool*. Intuitively, the owner's non-restricting claim to exclude the agent from using his vase does not amount to a right in this context. The bystander can justifiably take his vase and destroy it to save the five. But if the owner does not have a right to deny the agent the destructive use of his vase, why would he be permitted to hang onto it, rather than be required to use it himself to save the five? The answer is that his right to be vicious gives him an extra agent-claim, one that affects the balance of claims on him. It gives him a right of self-indulgence, a right not to take positive steps to cause the loss to himself. It is not that the steps are costly in themselves; he would be required to take similar steps if the loss to him were smaller. But his right of self-indulgence augments his claim to keep his vase so that, as an agent, he has a privilege to choose not to take steps to sacrifice it.

To be clear, if the cost to him were high enough – say the loss of the tool would prevent him from being able to practice his chosen profession for years to come – then the bystander would be obliged not to use and destroy the tool, even to save five others. My aim here is *not* to say that non-restricting property claims can trump positive claims to be saved from death only if the owner's life depends on his claim being respected. It is only to explain how there could be a middle ground in which the right to do wrong, which applies only to agents, creates a gap between what a patient has a right not to suffer and what an agent has a right to refuse to do.

## IV. A METHODOLOGICAL CONCERN ABOUT THE CONNECTION BETWEEN INTUITIONS AND THEORY

I have tried to argue for the RCP by arguing that we have good reason to accept (a) an expanded understanding the autonomy principle, taking it to apply it not only to agents but also to patients; (b) a baseline of means that an agent is presumptively free to use, against which certain patient-claims count as restricting and others as non-restricting; and (c) that restricting claims are weaker than non-restricting claims, all else equal. But I have also argued that the RCP can account for a set of fairly robust moral intuitions, that are normally accounted for by the MP, and that it can do so just as well, if not better, than the MP. What I want to address here is a challenge to the connection between these two parts of the argument.

The challenge presupposes that we have reason for skepticism about the epistemic reliability of moral intuitions, which, of course, we do. There is evidence that intuitions often reflect parochial cultural prejudices, vary depending on the mood or emotional state of the person offering the intuition, and are subject to framing effects.<sup>55</sup> Moreover, they seem quite likely to reflect the theoretical commitments one has already made, so that they are likely to be not so much an independent test of any theory one holds, but a reflection of that theory which, if inconsistent with another theory, shows no more than that the two theories differ.<sup>56</sup>

For all that, I believe that they do still have some moral-epistemic value. They *may* represent an inchoate form of moral insight that *may* outstrip any extant moral theory, and as a result they may, if theory and intuitions do not match, usefully prod moral theorists into developing richer, more nuanced theories, theories that would then have to be tested to see whether they stand up to scrutiny in terms of other basic moral commitments. The intuitions that action is impermissible in the four archetypal cases with which we began seem to have played that critical role *vis-à-vis* utilitarian theories. My goal here has been to try to offer an alternative to the MP that accounts for such cases and stands up better than the MP in terms of our other basic moral commitments.

The problem I now want to address is connected to this potential evidentiary role of intuitions. If their epistemic relevance depends on our being able to account for them as a form of inchoate moral insight, then merely matching them, without showing why they reflect a form of moral insight, is not the same as accounting for them. The match might be nothing but a coincidence. And if it is a coincidence, then there is no reason to trust that the intuitions have any moral epistemic value.<sup>57</sup>

<sup>55</sup> See, e.g., Kwame Anthony Appiah, *Experiments in Ethics* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2008), pp. 81, and 85–87. See also Peter Unger, ‘Causing and Preventing Serious Harm’, *Philosophical Studies* 65 (1992): 227–255 (illustrating how to frame intermediate cases which move from Trolley Switch to Massive Man in slow steps so that toppling the massive man seems permissible in the end).

<sup>56</sup> For a helpful survey of how thought experiments can produce knowledge, and what to make of the fact that different people react to thought experiments differently, see Joseph Shieber, ‘On the Nature of Thought Experiments and a Core Motivation of Experimental Philosophy’, *Philosophical Psychology* 23 (2010): 547–564.

Making the worry more concrete, it is plausible that our intuitions reflect the MP, because it is a familiar and simple moral principle. But the RCP is neither familiar nor so simple, especially given the details I had to introduce in Section III. If our intuitions do not reflect the RCP's underlying ideas at some level, then this match between the RCP and our intuitions might be just coincidence. If so, then the ideas in the RCP cannot account for whatever insight these intuitions reflect, and we face another dilemma. Either the MP is true, and it accounts for these intuitions as reflecting a moral insight, or the MP is false, in which case these intuitions have not been shown to reflect any moral insight and it might be reasonable to discount them as mere prejudice or confusion. Moreover, if the MP is true, then the RCP may be interesting, but not morally important. And if the MP is false and the related intuitions are ungrounded, then the RCP is in the dubious position of explaining *nothing* of moral significance. In sum, if the RCP cannot actually account for our moral intuitions, then it is either insignificant or much less well-grounded than the preceding sections suggest.

Is there, then, any reason to think that our intuitions reflect this more complex and novel principle? I think the answer is yes. Despite its novelty and complexity, I think the RCP is actually an intuitive doctrine. Consider that among the common-sense explanations people give for their intuitions in the *Trolley Problem* cases is that the massive man is not 'involved' in the situation in the same way that the side-track man is. I suggest that this reflects an inchoate awareness that in some sense the side-track man's claim competes with the claims of the five, while the massive man's claim does not. This notion of being or not being involved in the affairs of others is exactly what the RCP explicates in terms of living independently or not, and having claims with or without 'negative externalities'. With regard to cases like *Loop Trolley*, I think it is plausible that the interdependence of the parties – the key to the RCP's handling of the case – is also intuitively appreciated. Lastly, it is plausible that the extension of the RCP to property and property-like notions fits our intuitive sense of the importance of property in setting a baseline for what agents may presumptively use to navigate through the world. And therefore, while I would not go so far as to say that people have been intuitively relying on the RCP all along, I think it is at least plausible that their intuitions have reflected deep principles that the RCP picks up and develops. And this makes it plausible that the RCP *accounts* for those intuitions.

### V. CONCLUSION

A number of philosophers have become skeptical of the MP. There are understandable reasons for this skepticism: both the subjective and the objective interpretation of what it means to use another as a means are difficult to defend. The best thing it has going for it, it seems to me, is that it provides a simple explanation of robust intuitions in a wide range of cases. But mapping intuitions is not sufficient if the underlying principle seems morally unsound.

The RCP shows why the causal role of patients, a salient moral fact for the objective interpretation of the MP, is morally significant. It does so by shifting the focus from why it is particularly hard to justify using another merely as a means to why it is particularly easy to justify harming another as a side-effect. Additionally, it broadens the focus from dyadic relationships between agents and patients to a balance of all competing claims on an agent, taking into account how some patients' claims compete with other patients' claims. It highlights the potential of restricting claims to impose something like negative externalities on others, and adjusts the strength of claims that would do so downwards. At the same time, it holds that when non-restricting claims concern serious harms, they normally register as absolute rights. These rights reflect the deep principle that we all have a fundamental right to lead and enjoy our own lives. Large sacrifices cannot be demanded of us for the sake of others with whom we have no special relationships, and whom we neither as agents nor as patients make worse off than they otherwise, in our absence, would be.

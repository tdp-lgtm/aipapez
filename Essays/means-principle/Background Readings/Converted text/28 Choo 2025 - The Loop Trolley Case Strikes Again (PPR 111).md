# The Loop trolley case strikes again! A threat to the Doctrine of Double Effect and the Mere Means Principle

Frederick Choo

Rutgers University, New Brunswick, New Jersey, USA

### Correspondence

Frederick Choo, Rutgers University, New Brunswick, NJ, USA.  
Email: [fcwyl@hotmail.com](mailto:fcwyl@hotmail.com)

## Abstract

Judith Thomson introduced the Loop variant of the trolley case (*Loop Case*) which poses a problem for two attractive moral principles: the Doctrine of Double Effect (DDE) and the Mere Means Principle (MMP). In the *Loop Case*, it seems permissible to divert the trolley. However, both DDE and MMP entail that diverting is impermissible. Hence, these principles give the wrong verdict. Since then, however, various philosophers have argued that the *Loop Case* does not actually pose a problem for DDE and MMP. One group argues that we should deny that it is permissible to divert in the *Loop Case*. A second group instead develops arguments to show that DDE and MMP do not entail that diverting in the *Loop Case* is impermissible. I argue that none of these responses succeeds. Since both trains of thought fail, I maintain that the *Loop Case* still poses a problem for DDE and MMP—it has made its comeback.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Thomson's (1985) Loop variant of the trolley case (henceforth *Loop Case*) was widely seen as a counterexample to two attractive moral principles: the Doctrine of Double Effect (henceforth DDE) and the Mere Means Principle (henceforth MMP). In the *Loop Case*, it seems permissible to divert the trolley. However, both DDE and MMP entail that diverting is impermissible. Hence, these principles give the wrong verdict.

Since then, there have been two groups of responses. One group of philosophers argues that we should deny that it is permissible to divert in the *Loop Case*.<sup>1</sup> A second group of philosophers instead develops arguments that would allow us to maintain that it is permissible to divert in the *Loop Case* while holding to DDE or MMP.<sup>2</sup>

In this paper, I argue that none of these responses succeeds. Hence, the *Loop Case* still presents a problem for DDE and MMP. The structure of the paper is as follows: Section 2 introduces two trolley cases and elaborates on how MMP and DDE explain our intuitions in these cases. Then, the *Loop Case* is introduced as a problem for MMP and DDE. In Section 3, I consider the first group of responses and argue that they fail. In Section 4, I consider the second group of responses and argue that they fail. Since both trains of thought fail, Section 5 concludes that the *Loop Case* still poses a problem for DDE and MMP—it has made its comeback.

## 2 | TROLLEY CASES, MMP, AND DDE

To begin, consider two trolley cases.<sup>3</sup>

*Standard* (Figure 1). A trolley is going down a track toward five people who will be killed unless something is done. A bystander could pull a lever that will cause the trolley to be diverted onto a second track, and the five would be saved. However, there is a large man on the second track. If the trolley is diverted onto the second track, it will hit and kill the large man and then stop.

*Bridge* (Figure 2). A trolley is going down a track toward five people who will be killed unless something is done. A bystander could pull a lever that will cause a large man to fall off a bridge onto the tracks. The trolley would then hit and kill the large man, which would bring the trolley to a stop. The five would be saved.

Most people intuitively judge that it is permissible for the bystander to pull the lever in *Standard*, but impermissible to pull the lever in *Bridge*.<sup>4</sup> What can explain the difference between these cases? Various non-consequentialists appeal to either MMP or DDE.<sup>5</sup>

Mere Means Principle (MMP): It is impermissible to (harmfully) use someone as a mere means to bring about a greater good. (Bruers & Braeckman, 2014, p. 256)

Doctrine of Double Effect (DDE): It is impermissible to cause harm if one intends the harm as a means of bringing about a greater good, but it can be permissible to cause harm if one merely foresees the harm as a side-effect of bringing about a greater good. (Bruers & Braeckman, 2014, p. 262)

The two principles are similar. The main difference is that DDE has an extra psychological element (intending vs. foreseeing). Despite this difference, they are often used interchangeably as they typically result in the same verdicts.<sup>6</sup> How do we tell if a victim is being used as a means? One common way of determining whether a person is being used as a means is the counterfactual presence test: If the victim were not present, would performing the action (that would have caused harm to the victim if the victim were present) still result in the goal being achieved? If the answer is no, then the presence of the victim is causally necessary for the goal to be achieved, and hence, performing the action would be to use the victim as a means.<sup>7</sup>

<sup>5</sup> Although MMP and DDE are formulated in absolutist terms here, it is important to note that most contemporary deontologists are not absolutists. They do not hold that it is *always* impermissible to use someone as a mere means or to intend the harm as a means. After all, it seems permissible to tickle someone if doing so would save five lives. Instead, most contemporary deontologists are threshold deontologists. They hold that it is *harder to justify* a harm when one is using someone as a mere means, or when one intends the harm as a means. This means that one may permissibly use someone as a mere means, or intend harm to someone as a means, once the bad consequences are severe enough relative to the harm done to the person who is harmed. In this paper, I have left this out because most threshold deontologists judge that the death of the five people is not severe enough to cross the threshold to make pulling the lever permissible in *Bridge*.

<sup>6</sup> MMP and DDE may occasionally depart either due to the nature of the case (see Bruers’s and Braeckman’s (2014) “The loop with an avalanche” case) or based on the psychological state of the agent (e.g., the agent might use someone as a mere means without the relevant intentions simply because they are unaware of certain facts about the case).

Applying this to the trolley case, we can ask: If the large man were not present, would pulling the lever still result in the five being saved? In *Standard*, the answer is yes—the trolley would go on the second track and not hit the five. In *Bridge*, the answer is no—pulling the lever does nothing to stop the trolley from maintaining its path—the trolley will hit the five if the large man were not present. Therefore, the large man is not used as a means to save the five in *Standard*, but the large man is used as a means to save the five in *Bridge*.

The counterfactual presence test is, of course, not the only game in town. A common alternative is to ask whether the victim's presence or participation plays a causal role (in the right way) toward the goal of the action.<sup>8</sup> If yes, then the victim is being used as a means. Applying this to the trolley case, we can ask: Does the trolley hitting the large man causally contribute to saving the five? In *Standard*, the answer is no. In *Bridge*, the answer is yes—it is the hitting of the large man that stops the trolley from hitting the five (i.e., the large man is used as a trolley stopper). Therefore, the large man is used as a means in *Bridge*, but not in *Standard*.

I must note here that the tests above only establish whether a person is used as a means rather than whether the person is used as a *mere* means. This, however, will not matter for our purposes. On one account, whether the large man is used as a *mere* means depends on whether he has *actually* given informed, voluntary consent.<sup>9</sup> Other accounts instead appeal to whether the large man *would give* informed, voluntary consent (i.e., hypothetical consent), or whether the bystander can reasonably believe that the large man has actually given (or would give) informed, voluntary consent.<sup>10</sup> In all the trolley cases under consideration, there is no actual or hypothetical informed, voluntary consent; and the bystander cannot reasonably believe that the large man has given (or would give) informed, voluntary consent. So, if the bystander uses the large man as a means, the bystander would be using the large man as a *mere* means.

Given that pulling the lever would be to use the large man as a *mere* means in *Bridge* but not in *Standard*, MMP can explain why it strikes us as impermissible to pull the lever in *Bridge* even though it is permissible to do so in *Standard*.

Similarly, DDE gives the same results as MMP in these cases. To determine whether an agent who causes harm intends the harm as a means or merely foresees the harm as a side effect, we can again either apply the counterfactual presence test or ask whether the harming of the victim plays the relevant causal role in the goal being brought about.<sup>11</sup> From this, we get the result that a bystander who pulls the lever in order to save the five in *Standard* merely foresees that the large man will be hit as a side effect of saving the five; but a bystander who pulls the lever in order to save the five in *Bridge* intends that the trolley hit the large man as a means of saving the five (i.e., the bystander must intend that the large man play the role of the trolley stopper). Therefore, DDE offers a similar explanation for our differing intuitions between *Bridge* and *Standard*.

Since Thomson's paper was published, various prominent philosophers have expressed that they find the *Permissibility Verdict* intuitive.<sup>13</sup> Other philosophers who write on the *Loop Case* also report (based on anecdotal evidence) that many philosophers they have come across seem to find the *Permissibility Verdict* intuitive.<sup>14</sup> For example, Walen (2014, p. 445, footnote 32) notes that his "informal survey of philosophers shows that they are more prone to accept Thomson's judgment." Otsuka (2008, p. 92) further claims, "The widespread consensus among moral philosophers who have addressed this problem is that it is permissible to divert the trolley." Similarly, Ashford and Tim Mulgan (2018) say that "most philosophers who discuss Thomson's Loop share Scanlon's intuition that switching the trolley is permissible."

Because of the *Loop Case*, many philosophers have dismissed DDE and MMP. McMahan (2009, p. 345) says, "Between 2000 and 2007, when I was one of the editors of *Ethics*, I reviewed a number of submissions by junior philosophers in which [the Doctrine of] Double Effect was relegated to a footnote and dismissed as an exploded view that no reasonable person could take seriously. I saw that as evidence of a decisive shift in philosophical orthodoxy." Similarly, Kaufman (2016, p. 22) reports that when he defends DDE, philosophers at conferences and anonymous reviewers from journals often ask him, "Hasn't *Loop* already disproved DDE?"<sup>15</sup> Hence, the *Loop Case* was (and perhaps still is) widely seen as a counterexample to MMP and DDE.

## 3 | THE PERMISSIBILITY OF DIVERTING IN LOOP

Although the *Loop Case* was widely taken as a counterexample to MMP and DDE, various philosophers have since responded in defense of MMP and DDE. In this section, I deal with the first group of responses. The first group argues that we should reject the *Permissibility Verdict*.<sup>16</sup> There are three types of arguments that fall under this group: (a) some argue that the intuition for the *Permissibility Verdict* is due to an order effect, (b) some hold that the *Permissibility Verdict* does not enjoy widespread intuitive support, and (c) some argue that we should reject the *Permissibility Verdict* because we can see that the large man would be used as a means of saving the five in the *Loop Case*. I respond to each argument in the following sub-sections.

#### 3.1 | Order effects distort our intuitions

Some philosophers argue that our intuitions about the *Loop Case* are distorted by an order effect.<sup>17</sup> For example, Otsuka (2008, pp. 107–110) suggests that the intuition for the *Permissibility Verdict* is due to an order effect. He argues that philosophers find the *Permissibility Verdict* intuitive because we are shown *Standard* before the *Loop Case*, and the *Loop Case* looks similar to *Standard*. He suggests that we might instead intuitively judge that it is impermissible to divert in the *Loop Case* if we are shown *Bridge* first, followed by a *Loop Case* variant that looks more like *Bridge*. Similarly, Nelkin and Rickless (2014, p. 154) suggest that if the *Loop Case* were presented before *Standard*, then people would not find the *Permissibility Verdict* so intuitive.

To test whether people’s intuitions about the *Loop Case* are indeed distorted by an order effect, Liao et al. (2012) conducted a study to test whether people’s judgments about the *Loop Case* varied depending on whether they were shown *Standard* or *Bridge* first. Group A was shown *Bridge*, followed by the *Loop Case*, and then *Standard*. Group B was given the reverse order. Participants were asked whether pulling the lever was permissible in each case and responded based on a 6-point scale (where 6 is “strongly agree”). The results are in Figure 4.

For the *Loop Case*, 44% in Group A leaned toward the *Permissibility Verdict* (mean = 3.10), but 66% in Group B leaned toward the *Permissibility Verdict* (mean = 3.82).<sup>18</sup> Based on the results, it seems that people’s intuitions about the *Loop Case* tend to be similar to their judgments about the prior scenario. This indicates an order effect. Hence, Liao et al. conclude that an order effect distorts people’s intuitive judgments about the *Loop Case*. They claim that this undermines the supposed evidential status of intuitions about the *Loop Case*. Due to an order effect, our intuitions for the *Permissibility Verdict* cannot be relied on.

Does appealing to order effects show that the *Loop Case* fails to present a problem for MMP and DDE? I think not. While I agree that an order effect does affect people’s intuitions about the *Loop Case*, I think it is too hasty to conclude that all intuitive judgments about the *Loop Case* have no evidential value. Three replies are in order.

First, multiple studies have established that when participants are shown both *Standard* and *Bridge*, an asymmetric order effect is present.<sup>19</sup> When shown *Bridge* before *Standard*, most participants judge pulling the lever as impermissible in both scenarios. However, when shown *Standard* before *Bridge*, most participants judge pulling the lever in *Standard* as permissible but pulling the lever in *Bridge* as impermissible. Such results reveal an asymmetric transfer effect.<sup>20</sup> When shown *Bridge* before *Standard*, participants transfer their judgment of impermissibility from *Bridge* to *Standard*. However, when shown *Standard* before *Bridge*, participants do not transfer their judgments of permissibility from *Standard* to *Bridge*—their judgments about both cases seem unaffected since the results are similar to the judgments of those who are only shown either *Standard* or *Bridge* in isolation.

Based on such studies, Alex Wiegmann and colleagues conclude that when subjects are presented with similar moral dilemmas:

- a. If people would judge an action as impermissible when a scenario is considered in isolation, their judgments are unaffected by order effects, but
- b. If people would judge an action as permissible when a scenario is considered in isolation, their judgment is susceptible to a transfer effect if they are first presented with a similar scenario where they judge an action as impermissible (Wiegmann et al., 2012; Wiegmann & Waldmann, 2014).

Given the asymmetric transfer effect, I think we should only find the judgments of those who are shown *Bridge* before *Standard* as suspect.

Hence, I suggest that when it comes to Liao et al.’s study, we should similarly hold that Group A’s judgments are suspect but not Group B’s judgments because of the asymmetric transfer effect. Notice that Group A leaned toward judging action as impermissible in all three scenarios, with close mean scores. This again suggests that being presented with *Bridge* first results in a transfer effect—people carry the impermissibility judgment from *Bridge* to the subsequent scenarios. So, Group A’s judgments are suspect. In contrast, Group B, who were shown *Standard* first, varied in their judgments for the three scenarios, indicating that people did not carry the permissibility judgment from *Standard* to subsequent scenarios. Given the asymmetric transfer effect observed from the other studies and Liao et al.’s own results, we should not find group B’s judgments for the *Permissibility Verdict* suspect. Hence, group B’s intuitive judgments will still have evidential value.

Second, we can also look at cases where no scenario is shown to subjects prior to the *Loop Case*. There are three such studies. In the study of Liao et al. (2012), they collected responses from Group C, who were only shown the *Loop Case*. The majority in group C leaned toward the *Permissibility Verdict* (mean = 4.19). In another study by Waldmann and Dieterich (2007), a majority of the participants also leaned toward the *Permissibility Verdict*, with the mean at 4.74 on a 6-point scale. This result is very similar to their results for *Standard* (mean = 4.85) and differed significantly from *Bridge* (mean = 3.73). In another study by Greene et al. (2009), a majority of the participants also leaned toward the *Permissibility Verdict*, with the mean at 5.89 on a 9-point scale. Greene (2013, pp. 220–221) reports that 81% of participants hold to the *Permissibility Verdict*, which is similar to *Standard* where 87% of participants say it is permissible to divert. All these studies show that the *Permissibility Verdict* enjoys widespread intuitive support when no order effect is present. In all of these cases, the widespread intuitive support for the *Loop Case* is rather similar to the widespread intuitive support for *Standard*.

Third, in the case of philosophers, we are typically exposed to *Standard*, followed by *Bridge* (and then MMP or DDE), and then the *Loop Case*. In such a context, philosophers would have two contrasting cases to compare to the *Loop Case*. So, the temporal priority of the cases should have no effect on their intuitions about the *Loop Case*. If anything, we should expect that being exposed to MMP or DDE as a plausible way of accounting for our intuitions in *Standard* and *Bridge* should prime us to think that it would be impermissible to divert in the *Loop Case* since the large man would be used as a means of saving the five. Yet, as mentioned, many philosophers seem to accept the *Permissibility Verdict*.<sup>21</sup> Therefore, such intuitive judgments will still have evidential value.

For these three reasons, I think the appeal to order effects as a means to undermine the intuitive support for the *Permissibility Verdict* fails. We have reason to maintain that Group B's intuitive judgments still have some evidential value due to the asymmetrical transfer effect, we can rely on the results of studies where subjects are not shown any case prior to the *Loop Case*, and I have argued that philosopher's intuitions are unlikely to be affected by an order effect since they are shown both *Standard* and *Bridge* prior to the *Loop Case*. Therefore, intuitive support for the *Permissibility Verdict* remains.

#### 3.2 | Divided intuitions in the *Loop Case*

Instead of appealing to order effects, several philosophers have offered a different response to the *Loop Case* by citing the study of Hauser et al. (2007). The study of Hauser et al. (2007) reports that only 56% of their participants judge diverting as permissible in first-trial responses to the *Loop Case*.<sup>22</sup> Given this, the *Permissibility Verdict* seems to lack widespread intuitive support. Hence, one might doubt that the *Loop Case* is a counterexample to MMP and DDE.

In response, I think it is problematic to simply appeal to Hauser et al.'s study because it contradicts the results from other studies.<sup>23</sup> We have seen in Sub-section 3.1 that Liao et al.'s study, Waldmann's and Dieterich's study, and Greene et al.'s study all concur that a majority hold to the *Permissibility Verdict*. This also aligns with the reports that many philosophers find the *Permissibility Verdict* intuitive.

Besides the fact that all the other studies contradict Hauser et al.'s study, I think that there are three additional reasons to treat Hauser et al.'s study with skepticism. First, a framing effect may be present in Hauser et al.'s study due to how the *Loop Case* was described. Waldmann and Dieterich (2007, pp. 251–252), and Greene et al. (2009, p. 369) suggest that their results contradict Hauser et al., because Hauser et al.'s study problematically compares the large man to a heavy object. Waldmann and Dieterich argue that the way Hauser et al. framed the case highlights that the large man is used as a stopping device and makes his cruel death more imaginable. This would cause more people to judge that diverting is impermissible.

Second, I think there are some important differences between Hauser et al.'s (2007, pp. 18–19) version of the *Loop Case* and how most other philosophers have described the *Loop Case*. For example, in Hauser et al.'s version, the hitting of the large man merely slows down the trolley and gives the five men time to escape on their own. So, in their case, if the trolley is diverted onto the second track, it would still travel on the extra bit of track back toward the five people. Such differences are significant enough to allow more factors into play and give us a different result from the *Loop Case*.

<sup>21</sup> I think this is surprising if an order effect is present. I think an order effect is more likely to cause philosophers to judge that diverting is impermissible. This is because being exposed to *Bridge* and DDE right before the *Loop Case* will likely make them sensitive to the fact that the large man is used as a mere means.

<sup>22</sup> To note, Otsuka (2008, p. 110), Bruers and Braeckman (2014), Kaufman (2016, p. 26), Bruers (2016a), and Bruers (2016b, p. 418) cite other papers or books by Hauser (and co-authors). However, the results discussed in those papers or books are based on Hauser et al. (2007).

<sup>23</sup> One might also be suspicious of experiments in which Hauser is involved in since he is infamous for having conducted research misconduct, including fabricating and falsifying data. As far as I am aware, however, investigations have not shown that this study is affected.

Third, Hauser et al.’s study did not provide any visual diagrams—it was just a paragraph of text. This might make it harder for people to visualize the case as a *Loop Case*, which may in turn affect the accuracy of their responses. For these reasons, I think that we should favor the other studies, which all concur that the *Permissibility Verdict* enjoys widespread intuitive support.

One might not find the above reasons cumulatively strong. One might point out that Hauser et al.’s study has a vastly larger sample size, and a more diverse subject population compared to all the other studies. Hence, we should consider Hauser et al.’s results to be the most accurate.

In response, even if we take Hauser et al.’s results as the most accurate, I think having 56% of the participants find the *Permissibility Verdict* intuitive is still significantly problematic for DDE and MMP. If DDE or MMP is true, then why do 56% of people have intuitions that contradict what DDE and MMP entail in the *Loop Case*? As Kaufman (2016, pp. 28–29) (who defends DDE) and Bruers (2016b, p. 419) (who defends MMP) each point out, defenders of DDE and MMP need to explain why half of the participants find diverting permissible *contra* what DDE and MMP seem to entail. Furthermore, proponents of DDE and MMP will still need to deal with the fact that many philosophers find the *Permissibility Verdict* intuitive. Hence, even if we set aside the other studies, a mere appeal to Hauser et al.’s study cannot save DDE and MMP.

#### 3.3 | The large man is a mere means

Perhaps there is another way to undermine the *Permissibility Verdict* without appealing to order effects or Hauser et al.’s study. Several philosophers have argued that since we can see that the large man is used as a mere means (and that a bystander must intend the harm to the large man as a means to save the five), we can see that the *Loop Case* is more similar to *Bridge* than to *Standard*, and hence, we should reject the *Permissibility Verdict*.<sup>24</sup> Otsuka and Bruers each provide slightly more argumentation.

Otsuka (2008, pp. 107–110) argues that since the large man is used as a mere means in the *Loop Case* but not in *Standard*, DDE allows us to morally differentiate the *Loop Case* from *Standard*.<sup>25</sup> In contrast, we lack a principle to morally differentiate the *Loop Case* from *Bridge*. Since DDE is highly plausible and seems to explain a variety of cases, we should take the *Loop Case* to be similar to *Bridge*, and hence reject the *Permissibility Verdict*. Otsuka suggests that the *Permissibility Verdict* is a moral illusion that is generated because the *Loop Case* and *Standard* look so similar.

Similarly, Bruers (2016b, pp. 419–420) argues that the *Permissibility Verdict* is a moral illusion. People find the *Permissibility Verdict* intuitive only because they fail to recognize that the large man is used as a mere means. Bruers suggests that this failure happens either because the *Loop Case* looks similar to *Standard*, or because when we move the threat in the *Loop Case*, rather than the victim (as in *Bridge*), our judgment that the large man would be used as a mere means is blurred. Bruers argues that if people recognize and focus on the fact that the large man is used as a means in the *Loop Case*, just as in *Bridge*, they would not find the *Permissibility Verdict* intuitive.

<sup>24</sup> See Otsuka (2008, pp. 107–110), McMahan (2009, pp. 358–359), Wedgwood (2011, p. 467), FitzPatrick (2012, p. 189), Kerstein (2013, p. 124), and Bruers (2016a, 2016b). To note, Wedgwood (2011, p. 467) does not actually say whether it is permissible to divert. He merely claims that there is a stronger reason against diverting since the large man would be used as a mere means. However, I think Wedgwood should hold that if it is impermissible to pull the lever in *Bridge*, then it would be impermissible to divert in the *Loop Case* since both have the same consequences and the large man is used as a mere means in both.

<sup>25</sup> To note, Otsuka characterizes the large man as being *harmfully* used as a means, rather than being used as a *mere* means.

I think these responses fail. First, one can recognize the *Loop Case* as a counterexample to DDE/MMP only if they do recognize that the large man is used as a means of saving the five, just as in *Bridge*. So, philosophers who take the *Loop Case* as a counterexample must recognize that the large man in the *Loop Case* is used as a mere means just like in *Bridge*. Yet, they still find the *Permissibility Verdict* intuitive. Hence, their intuitive judgments are neither due to a failure to recognize that the large man is used as a mere means nor a failure to recognize that the *Loop Case* is similar to *Bridge* in this way.

Second, many philosophers are initially attracted to DDE/MMP as a plausible explanation of our intuitions in *Standard* and *Bridge*. However, once they are introduced to the *Loop Case* as a counterexample, they come around to thinking that MMP and DDE are problematic and abandon these principles. This suggests that their intuition for the *Permissibility Verdict* is much stronger than their intuitions for DDE/MMP. To convince these philosophers, one cannot simply suggest that they reject their intuitions about the *Loop Case* because it is clear that the large man is used as a mere means, and DDE/MMP has intuitive plausibility.

Let me take stock. Thus far, I have examined three arguments for rejecting the *Permissibility Verdict*. In response to the worry that order effects distort our intuitions, I have argued that the asymmetric transfer effect does not give us reason to doubt the *Permissibility Verdict*, that there is widespread intuitive support for the *Permissibility Verdict* when no order effect is present, and that such order effects do not distort philosophers' intuitions.

In response to the claim that intuitions are divided in the *Loop Case*, I raised some worries for Hauser et al.'s study and pointed out that other studies concur that a majority hold to the *Permissibility Verdict*. Also, even if we take Hauser et al.'s study as the most accurate and hold that intuitions are split roughly 50–50, that would still be a problem for DDE and MMP.

In response to the call to reject the *Permissibility Verdict* because the large man is used as a mere means just like in *Bridge*, I argued that philosophers' intuitions are not due to a failure to recognize that the large man is used as a mere means just like in *Bridge*. Also, such a response is dialectically ineffective because many philosophers have stronger intuitions for the *Permissibility Verdict* than for DDE/MMP.

## 4 | CAN DDE AND MMP GET US THE *Permissibility Verdict* IN THE *Loop Case*?

In this section, I turn to the second group of responses. Kleingeld (2020), Kaufman (2016), and Kamm (2007) each advance arguments that would allow us to maintain the *Permissibility Verdict* while holding to DDE/MMP. If successful, then the *Loop Case* fails as a counterexample. In the following sub-sections, I consider and respond to each of their arguments.

#### 4.1 | Kleingeld: MMP and a bystander's own practical reasoning

The first argument to consider is by Kleingeld. Kleingeld (2020) defends MMP by arguing that MMP is compatible with the *Permissibility Verdict*. Kleingeld starts by arguing that there are two ways to understand what it is to use another person as a mere means. First, "we can describe the action from an external narrator's perspective, focusing on the bystander's observable behavior and another person's causal contribution to the realization of the bystander's presumed end" (Kleingeld, 2020, p. 212). This is how philosophers who discuss the trolley cases typically assess whether the large man is used as a mere means. They hold that if the large man's presence is necessary in order to save the five, then the large man is used as a mere means.

Kleingeld (2020, p. 212), however, points to a second way which she attributes to Immanuel Kant. She says that an alternative is to describe the bystander's action based on the bystander's own practical reasoning—what their ends are, what they take as a means, and how they reason. Kleingeld suggests that when it comes to MMP, the relevant point of reference is the bystander's own practical reasoning instead of an external narrator's perspective.

Kleingeld (2020, pp. 218–222) then argues that in the *Loop Case*, it is possible to construe the bystander's reasoning in two different ways. First, imagine Tili, who reasons that if she must choose between one and five lives, she would save five even if it involves using the large man as a mere means. Tili would pull the lever in both *Bridge* and the *Loop Case*.

Second, imagine Manuela, who reasons that if she must choose between one and five lives, she would save five, provided that she does not use the large man as a mere means. So, Manuela would not pull the lever in *Bridge*. When faced with the *Loop Case*, we can imagine Manuela reasoning: "either I let the trolley continue towards the five, which will save one life, or I divert it towards the heavy man, which will kill him but save five lives" (Kleingeld, 2020, p. 220). She then chooses to divert. Kleingeld (2020, p. 212) emphasizes that

[Manuela] will achieve her end by diverting the trolley to the side where only one man will die, and this is why she diverts it. Her reasoning includes the morally required proviso, and at no point does the heavy man enter into her reasoning as a means. It is not that she neglects to take note of important facts about the case: she knows that his body will stop the trolley and that this will kill him. But this prediction as such does not include any instrumental role for him.

Given her reasoning, Manuela does not use the large man as a mere means in her reasoning. MMP entails that she can permissibly divert the trolley.

Kleingeld (2020, p. 223) then argues that we can show that it is permissible to divert in the *Loop Case* by combining two familiar Kantian principles: "(1) A morally permissible action remains permissible even when it is performed on the basis of a morally impermissible action principle; and (2) if an action can be performed only on the basis of morally impermissible action principles, then the action is impermissible." Since Manuela uses a permissible action principle and can permissibly divert the trolley in the *Loop Case*, diverting is permissible. Hence, MMP can accommodate the *Permissibility Verdict*.

#### 4.2 | Addressing Kleingeld's solution

Kleingeld's solution seems problematic to me. If Manuela can reason the way she does in the *Loop Case*, then it seems to me that she can reason the same way in *Bridge*. Imagine that when faced with *Bridge*, Manuela reasons: "either I do nothing which will save one life, or I pull the lever which would kill one but save five lives." Manuela knows that she will achieve her end by pulling the lever which would cause only the large man to die, and this is why she pulls the lever. At no point does the large man enter into her reasoning as a mere means. Again, it is not that she neglects to take note of important facts about the case: She knows that his body will stop the trolley and that this will kill him. However, this prediction does not include any instrumental role for her. Given her reasoning, Manuela permissibly diverts the trolley. Since Manuela uses a permissible action principle and can permissibly pull the lever in *Bridge*, diverting is permissible in *Bridge*. Thus, if Manuel can reason like that, Kleingeld's solution yields the wrong verdict in *Bridge*.

Kleingeld (2020, pp. 216, 255) denies that Manuela can reason this way in *Bridge*. She claims that pulling the lever in *Bridge* necessarily involves the bystander using the large man as a mere means. This is because the bystander's reason for pulling the lever must be to use the large man's body to stop the trolley in order to reach her end of saving the five.

But here we must ask, why must the bystander's reason for pulling the lever be to use the large man as a mere means to save the five? The answer seems to be that the large man's presence is necessary in order to save the five. It would be pointless to pull the lever if the trolley would not hit the large man and stop. The only way to save the five is to cause the trolley to stop by hitting the large man.

Notice, however, that such an answer would apply to the *Loop Case* as well. In the *Loop Case*, the large man's presence is necessary to save the five. It would be pointless to pull the lever if the trolley would not hit the large man and stop. The only way to save the five is to cause the trolley to stop by hitting the large man. The bystander's goal of saving the five is not satisfied merely by diverting; it is satisfied only if the trolley hits the large man and stops. So, if we hold that any bystander who pulls the lever in *Bridge* must include an instrumental role for the large man because the large man's presence is necessary in order to stop the trolley from hitting the five, then the same must be said in the *Loop Case*. In other words, if we think Manuela cannot reason the way she does above in *Bridge*, then she cannot do so in the *Loop Case* either. Therefore, I submit that Kleingeld's solution fails.

#### 4.3 | Kaufman: DDE and reasoning based on one causal path

Let me now turn to Kaufman's argument. Kaufman (2016) defends DDE against the *Loop Case* by arguing that whether DDE entails the *Permissibility Verdict* depends on how we construe the bystander's reasoning. Kaufman (2016, p. 29) starts by arguing that it is plausible to interpret the *Loop Case* as having two separate causal chains that could lead to the death of the five. The first causal path is the first track, and the large man's death is merely foreseen with respect to this causal path.<sup>26</sup> The second causal path is the second track, and the large man's death is necessary in order to eliminate the threat posed by the trolley from this causal path.

Kaufman (2016, p. 30) then argues that what verdict DDE yields depends on what reasoning we attribute to the bystander. Some of us will think in terms of the second causal path and take the bystander's reason for diverting as causing the trolley to hit the large man as a means of saving the five. Since the bystander intends to cause harm to the large man as a means of saving the five, DDE gives the verdict that diverting is impermissible.

However, some of us will instead think in terms of the first causal path and take the bystander's reason for diverting as trying to save the five by eliminating the threat coming via the first causal path. The bystander who diverts would not be intending to cause harm to the large man as a means of achieving this goal. Although the bystander foresees that the large man's death is necessary to eliminate the threat coming via the second causal path, morality does not require one to change their original decision. Given this reasoning, DDE entails the *Permissibility Verdict*. Therefore, Kaufman concludes that DDE by itself does not give any verdict—it depends on how we construe the bystander's reasoning.

<sup>26</sup> Kaufman's statement seems strange. If the trolley goes down the first causal path, the large man does not die. So, the large man's death cannot be foreseen with respect to the threat going down the first causal path. What Kaufman must mean then is that if we only think about saving the five from being hit via the first causal path, then the large man's death is merely foreseen with respect to the diversion of the trolley via the first causal path and onto the second one.

Kaufman thinks that his solution has the virtue of being able to explain why intuitions are split 50–50 among people according to Hauser et al.'s study. It is because half of the participants are thinking about the second causal path (and so they think of the case as a decision to cause harm to the large man as a means to save the five), but the other half are thinking about the first causal path (and so they think of the case as a decision to save the five without causing harm to the large man as a means of saving the five).

#### 4.4 | Addressing Kaufman's solution

I think Kaufman's solution fails. First, to get DDE to give us the *Permissibility Verdict*, we have to construe the bystander's *only* goal as saving the five *from being hit via the first causal path*. But this seems implausible. We should not construe the bystander as being concerned only about saving the five from the trolley via one particular causal path. We should take the bystander as concerned with saving the five *simpliciter*. Since the trolley must hit the large man in order to save the five *simpliciter*, the bystander must intend to cause harm to the large man as a means of saving the five.

Second, consider the way that Kaufman takes the bystander to reason to get DDE to give the *Permissibility Verdict*. The bystander first sees that causing harm to the large man is not a means to prevent the trolley from hitting the five via the first causal path. Given this, the bystander decides to divert. Then, the bystander sees that causing harm to the large man would be using him to stop the trolley from hitting the five via the second causal path. However, the bystander thinks that morality does not require one to change their original decision. This seems deeply problematic to me. It seems wrong to think that morality would not require one to change their original decision. In the “original decision,” the bystander does not consider all the morally relevant features since the bystander is solely concerned with the first causal path. When a person makes a decision based only on some relevant features of a case and then comes to recognize additional morally relevant features about the case, morality often requires them to change their original decision. In the *Loop Case*, since causing harm to the large man is the means to eliminate the threat via the second causal path, DDE predicts that this additional fact would be morally relevant. So, Kaufman himself should accept that morality requires the bystander to change their original decision.

Third, it seems implausible to suggest that most people who hold to the *Permissibility Verdict* are thinking of diverting as a decision to save the five *from being hit via the first causal path*, while most people who judge that it is impermissible to divert are thinking of diverting as a decision to cause harm to the large man as a means to save the five *from being hit via the second causal path*. It seems implausible to hold that most people think about the case only in terms of one causal path. Surely, most of us recognize that there are two causal paths, and we take both into consideration when forming our judgments.

Fourth, it is simply false that people who hold to the *Permissibility Verdict* are thinking of the case as a decision to save the five without causing harm to the large man as a means. As I argued in Sub-section 3.3, philosophers who think the *Loop Case* is a counterexample to DDE/MMP must hold to the *Permissibility Verdict*, and yet also hold that the bystander intends to cause harm to the large man as a means of achieving their goal. If they thought of the case as a decision to save the five without causing harm to the large man as a means, then they cannot hold that DDE/MMP entails the wrong verdict. Therefore, I conclude that Kaufman's solution fails.

#### 4.5 | Kamm: Doctrine of Triple Effect

Let me now move to a third proposal by Kamm. To address the *Loop Case*, Kamm (2007) argues that DDE needs to be supplemented with her Doctrine of Triple Effect (DTE). She argues that DTE entails the *Permissibility Verdict*. To advance her argument, Kamm (2007, p. 95) distinguishes between acting *because* it will cause an effect and acting *in order to* cause an effect. The former merely involves “foreseeing,” but the latter involves “intending.” She demonstrates the distinction with the following case.

*Party.* Suppose that Adam is deciding whether to throw a party to have fun with his friends. The fact that throwing a party will result in having fun with friends is a sufficient reason for throwing a party as long as there are no countervailing reasons. However, Adam can see that throwing the party would cause a mess for him to clean up alone and he is unwilling to do so. He would throw the party only if he did not need to clean up alone. Thankfully, Adam foresees that throwing the party also causes his friends to feel indebted and so his friends would help to clean up. So, Adam decides to throw the party. (Kamm, 2007, pp. 95, 102)

Adam throws the party *in order to* have fun and *because* doing so would cause his friends to help clean up. Adam does not throw the party *in order to* cause his friends to clean up—the goal of throwing the party is simply to have fun; the goal is not to cause friends to clean his house.

*Party* shows that one may act (e.g., throw a party) *in order to* achieve a goal (e.g., have fun), while the act causes both a potential defeater of the goal (e.g., having a mess to clean up alone) and a defeater-defeater (e.g., feelings of indebtedness which result in friends helping to clean up). One acts *because* their action would bring about the defeater-defeater, but one does not act *in order to* bring about the defeater-defeater (Kamm, 2007, p. 102).

To apply the “because vs in-order-to” distinction to the *Loop Case*, Kamm (2007, pp. 94–95, 118) argues that we should view the *Loop Case* as having two different problems. The first problem is that the trolley will hit the five from the first track. For Kamm, the goal of diverting is to eliminate the first problem. Diverting, however, causes a second potential problem (a defeater)—the trolley hitting the five from the second track. Thankfully, diverting also causes the trolley to hit the large man and stop (a defeater-defeater). So, Kamm thinks that we can similarly apply the “because vs in-order-to” distinction. When the bystander redirects the trolley, they do so *in order to* prevent the trolley from hitting the five people from the first track, and *because* the trolley will hit the large man (but not *in order to* hit the large man).

One objection to Kamm's solution is that it seems implausible to think that a bystander's *only* goal is to prevent the trolley from hitting the five *from the first track*. Surely, their goal must be to save the five *simpliciter*, and hence, the bystander must intend that the trolley hit the large man. Both Otsuka and Liao have raised such an objection. Otsuka (2008, pp. 105–106) argues that there is only one threat in the *Loop Case*. To illustrate, Otsuka raises a case similar to *Bridge*.

*Illuminated Trolley Case.* An unilluminated trolley is going down a track and will kill five people. However, a bystander could pull a lever which would get a spotlight to illuminate the trolley. The light will reflect off the trolley and cause a large man to fall off a bridge onto the track. The trolley would then hit and kill the large man, and then stop. The five would be saved.

It would be implausible to hold that a bystander who pulls the lever only has the goal of preventing an unilluminated trolley from hitting the five, and they do so because pulling the lever causes both a new potential defeater (an illuminated trolley hitting the five) and a defeater–defeater (the trolley hitting the large man and stopping). This is implausible because illuminating the trolley does not eliminate the threat posed by the trolley. Intuitively, an unilluminated and an illuminated trolley heading toward the five are one and the same problem. The only reason for illuminating the trolley is to eliminate the threat posed by the trolley by causing the trolley to stop by hitting the large man. Similarly, in the *Loop Case*, the trolley poses the same threat to the five regardless of the track it comes from. There is only one problem. Since the only reason for diverting is to eliminate the threat by causing the trolley to stop by hitting the large man, Otsuka (2008, pp. 100, 105–106) contends that the bystander must have intended that the trolley hit the large man as a means of saving the five.<sup>27</sup>

Liao (2009, p. 228) similarly argues that it is implausible to hold that the bystander’s only goal in the *Loop Case* is to prevent the trolley from hitting the five from the first track. The reason to prevent the trolley from hitting the five people from the first track would be the same reason to prevent the trolley from hitting the five people from the second track. Therefore, the bystander’s goal must be to prevent the trolley from hitting the five *simpliciter*.

I think both Otsuka and Liao are on the right track. Eliminating the first problem seems like an instrumental goal. The final (i.e., non-instrumental) goal is to save the five *simpliciter*. After all, the bystander is construed as a moral agent who is concerned about the five lives. So, even if we describe the *Loop Case* as having two different problems, eliminating each problem would merely be an instrumental goal. The bystander has one final goal which is to save the five *simpliciter*.

This objection, however, is not enough to show that Kamm’s solution fails. Kamm (2007, pp. 91–92, 104–108, 188) does say that we can think of the bystander in the *Loop Case* as having one final goal—to save the five. Getting the trolley to hit the large man is necessary to achieve this goal. But she thinks that even though the bystander intends to achieve this final goal in acting, they need not intend all of the means necessary to achieve their final goal.

Kamm argues that achieving the one final goal of saving the five requires the elimination of the *two* problems. The bystander can just intend to eliminate the first problem as a means of achieving the final goal, and then foresee that their act both causes a second potential problem (a defeater) and causes the trolley to hit the large man and stop (a defeater–defeater). Given the “because vs in-order-to” distinction, the bystander diverts *because* the defeater–defeater would arise; the bystander does not divert *in order to* cause the defeater–defeater. Therefore, the bystander need not intend all of the means to save the five. The bystander merely intends to eliminate the first problem as a means of achieving the final goal. The bystander foresees and conditions their action on the fact that the second problem generated will also be eliminated by the same act as a side effect.

<sup>27</sup> Although I agree that there is only one final goal—the save the five, I worry about Otsuka’s argument. In *Illuminated Trolley Case*, there is only one causal path for the threat to hit the five. However, there are two causal paths for the threat to hit the five in the *Loop Case*. Hence, it may make sense to talk about two different problems in the *Loop Case* but not in the *Illuminated Trolley Case*.

Kamm's DTE hence distinguishes between:

- (i) Acting with the intention to cause harm as a means of achieving a goal,
- (ii) Acting while merely foreseeing that one's actions will cause harm as a side-effect of achieving a goal,
- (iii) Acting because one's actions will cause harm as a means of achieving a goal, though one does not intend to cause the harm as a means of achieving a goal.

On DTE, although (i) is impermissible, (ii) and (iii) are both permissible (Kamm, 2007, pp. 91–119). Therefore, DTE can yield the verdict that it is permissible to divert in the *Loop Case*.

#### 4.6 | Addressing Kamm's solution

Although I agree that the “because vs in-order-to” distinction works in *Party*, I think it cannot be applied to the *Loop Case*. I think that the cases are dissimilar. First, in *Party*, there are two different final goals. One goal is to have fun with friends at a party. Another goal is not to clean up alone after the party. In the *Loop Case*, there is one final goal which is to save the five.

Second, consider how the defeater functions in both cases. For *Party*, Kamm herself notes that the defeater does not prevent the goal from being achieved if the action is taken—If Adam throws the party, he would have fun with his friends at the party even though he has to clean up the mess later. What the defeater does is that it merely prevents the fact that one's action will achieve the goal of having fun from being a sufficient reason for action (Kamm, 2007, p. 102). In the *Loop Case*, however, the defeater prevents the goal from being achieved—the trolley traveling down the extra track would prevent the bystander from achieving the goal of saving the five.

Third, consider how the defeater–defeater functions. In *Party*, Adam causing his friends to feel indebted and clean-up is not a means of achieving the goal of having fun with his friends. It is the party that causes him to have fun with friends, not the friends cleaning up after that causes him to have fun with friends. So, the defeater–defeater is not a means to achieving the goal of having fun with friends. This is why it is plausible to think that Adam does not throw the party *in order to* cause his friends to feel indebted and clean up. However, in the *Loop Case*, causing the trolley to stop by hitting the large man is a means of achieving the goal of saving the five. So, the defeater–defeater is a means to achieving the goal. This is why I think we should hold that the bystander diverts *in order to* cause the trolley to stop by hitting the large man as a means of saving the five.

Since the number of goals and the way that the defeater and the defeater–defeater functions in *Party* and the *Loop Case* are different, we have good reason to think that the “because vs in-order-to” distinction captured in *Party* cannot be applied to the *Loop Case*. In fact, for this reason, I could not represent *Party* in a diagram that looks like the *Loop Case*.<sup>28</sup> We can instead modify *Party* to make it more similar to the *Loop Case* by making sure that there is one final goal, and that the defeater and defeater-defeater function similarly. In addition, to make the case even more like the *Loop Case*, whoever is being used should disbenefit and should not have a choice in the matter.<sup>29</sup> Here is a case that I think is relevantly similar to the *Loop Case*.

In addition, my case also has additional details to make it even more like the *Loop Case*. In both *Party Variant* and the *Loop Case*, the parties being used are unwilling to be used and they disbenefit. In contrast, the friends in *Party* seem to be willing to help clean up, and they benefit (as they would have fun at the party).<sup>33</sup> So, my *Party Variant* is a much more analogous case.

It seems to me wrong to think that I throw the party *in order to* avoid the no-party route as a means to prevent a bad birthday, and *because* my neighbors will help clean up. Instead, it seems that I throw the party (in order for my wife to enjoy a party and) *in order to* cause my neighbors to clean up as a means of achieving my final goal of preventing a bad birthday for my wife. I intend to use them as a mere means to achieve my final goal. Therefore, the “because vs in-order-to” distinction does not apply to *Party Variant*. Given that *Party Variant* can be represented the same way as the *Loop Case*, we have good reason to think that the “because vs in-order-to” distinction does not apply to the *Loop Case* either. Hence, I submit that Kamm’s solution fails.

## 5 | CONCLUSION

Does the *Loop Case* pose a problem for DDE and MMP? As we have seen, various philosophers have thought not. One group of philosophers argues that we should deny the *Permissibility Verdict* either (a) because order effects distort intuitions, (b) because intuitions are divided according to Hauser et al.’s study, or (c) because the large man is used as a means in the *Loop Case*. A second group of philosophers advances different arguments which would allow us to maintain that diverting is permissible in the *Loop Case* while holding to MMP/DDE.

However, I have argued that none of these arguments succeed. We have good reason to maintain the *Permissibility Verdict*, and to think that DDE and MMP cannot accommodate the *Permissibility Verdict*. I conclude that the *Loop Case* still poses a problem for DDE and MMP—the *Loop Case* strikes again!

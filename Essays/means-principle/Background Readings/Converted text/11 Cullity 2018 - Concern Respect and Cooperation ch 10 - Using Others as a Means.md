#### Abstract

This chapter offers an account of the moral importance of not using others as a means: why, when, and how this matters. In Kant, a formulation of this thought is treated as *the* foundation for morality. On the view presented here, its significance is derivative. We each have a right of self-ownership, whose main derivational source is the equal respectworthiness of every self-expressing agent. This right functions as a context-underminer: the fact that my use of you will promote a personal end of my own fails to count towards infringing it. But other reasons can generate justified infringements. The chapter concludes with applications, including to the Trolley Problem.

Marshal Zhukov, the hero of the Soviet army's victories in 1942–5, had a blunt solution to the problem of clearing enemy minefields to prepare the way for his offensives. He ordered the weakest part of his infantry to be marched across them, with the NKVD following. The men forced to do this included conscripts and 'punishment battalions' containing political prisoners.<sup>1</sup> There seems to be something distinctively morally objectionable about this: it goes beyond the usual ways in which military commanders have treated their troops as 'cannon fodder'. It is as though, on running out of ammunition, he had loaded his guns with unwanted soldiers and fired their bodies at the enemy instead.

To identify the objection to Zhukov's action, it is natural to reach for a Kantian phrase: he used his soldiers as nothing more than a means to his own ends. In doing so, his treatment of them had the same feature that philosophers illustrate with artificial examples of people being pushed in the path of runaway trolleys, involuntary organ donors, and the like.<sup>2</sup> This feature can be found across a broad spectrum of cases that vary greatly in seriousness. Some are more seriously wrong than Zhukov's actions: Nero's use of human torches to light his games, for example. But there are also less serious cases. Suppose that when the paper towels have run out in the airport bathroom you wipe your hands on my arm, or that in the immigration queue you use my back as a desk to fill out your customs form, or that while I am standing in conversation on campus you lean your bike against me while you pick up your dropped keys. In cases like those, your behaviour might literally be harmless—I might not even find it annoying, only ridiculous.<sup>3</sup> However, it could still be wrong. To many of us, one of the main ways in which the morality of respect appears irreducible to the morality of concern is that we can be required (at least sometimes) to respect a person's entitlement to refuse to be used for someone else's benefit. However, the military example shows that we will need to do some work to identify the exact complaint. There is a sense in which all soldiers are used as a means by their commanders. And most of Zhukov's soldiers presumably shared his end of preventing their homeland from being conquered by the Nazis.

p. 196

In this chapter, I show how the substantive moral theory I have presented helps us to think about the moral importance of not using others as a means: why, when, and how this matters. In Kant, a formulation of this thought is treated as *the* foundation for ↳ morality.<sup>4</sup> On my view, its significance is derivative. I explain the derivation in what follows, showing how the theoretical resources supplied in Parts I and II can help us to think about when and why using others as a means is justifiable, and when it is not. The structure of the chapter is similar to Chapter 9. Again, the emphasis will be on explaining the grounding, scope, and force of a right we all possess: this time, a right of self-ownership.

### 10.1 Four Approaches to Using as a Means

The objectionable feature shared by the actions belonging to the spectrum just described cannot simply be that one person uses another as nothing more than a means to her own ends. An artist does not wrong the members of a crowd when, struck by the visual pattern it forms, she photographs it and exhibits the result without their permission. A surveyor does not wrong me when she takes advantage of where I happen to be standing to take a sighting.

To examples like these, the reply is typically made that such actions do not treat me *merely* as a means. The photographer is not disposed to treat me in *whatever* way would further her ends, irrespective of my own.<sup>5</sup> But suppose that is false, and she does have that nasty disposition. Then her attitude towards me is callous and disrespectful; but she still has not wronged me in taking my photograph as part of a crowd.<sup>6</sup> Her attitude in regarding me merely as a means may be bad, but acting with a bad attitude need not be wrong.<sup>7</sup>

So: which ways of using others as a means are wrong, and why? I think the right way to answer the first of these questions is via the second. We can start by trying to explain why some ways of using others as a means are morally problematic, and then use that to guide our understanding of which ways of doing so are problematic.

Among the attempts to explain this, four main thoughts stand out. The first is that it involves treating someone as a thing, not a person.<sup>8</sup> Zhukov was treating his soldiers as a kind of expendable substitute for mine-clearing machines when he sent them through the minefields—as a mere replaceable resource.

The second thought is that it is wrong in a similar way to the violation of property rights. My body is mine, not yours, to control. For you to regard it as a resource available for your use in furthering your ends violates my fundamental entitlement to its control. I possess my body in a far more direct and fundamental way than I possess any piece of owned property. That makes it a deeper violation of my rights if you put it to your own use without my consent.<sup>9</sup>

Third, the source of the disrespect might be thought to consist in the subordination of one person's agency to another. In using me as a means to your ends, you force me to serve as an extension of *your* agency, serving ends that are yours but not mine. So the fundamental offence is an offence against the relationship of equality that should exist between independent agents. In using me, you are failing to recognize me as your equal, with ends and priorities of my own.<sup>10</sup>

p. 197 A fourth thought is emphasized by Thomas Nagel.<sup>11</sup> Harming one person as a deliberate means to some further good end is morally problematic because of the way in which the agency of the person perpetrating the harm is directed at attaining an immediate end that is bad. According to this line of thought, even if this is chosen as a means to producing some further good, the way in which the perpetrator's agency is directed or guided is still morally corrupted. It is 'guided by evil', to use Nagel's phrase. Zhukov's aims are only achieved if his first wave of soldiers detonate the mines; his decision-making is directed towards achieving this. If, for some reason, this does not happen on one occasion, he will need to learn from that and change his approach to achieve the desired result—order the ranks to be packed closer together, perhaps. This makes his military tactics importantly different from those which, while they may result in many deaths and injuries, do not have as their guiding aim the maiming of one's own soldiers.<sup>12</sup>

I now want to explain how the account I have given of the morality of respect, its structure, and its relationship to the other parts of morality offers to make sense of thoughts such as these. In fact, I think that 'using others merely as a means' can express a complaint with a variety of different sources. It can be a complaint about violating a person's right of self-ownership, violating some other right, exploiting someone's intimacy, expressively treating another as a non-agent, or failing to respect the preciousness of a person. However, the central cases are those that invite the first of these complaints: they violate a right of self-ownership. Our main task will be to explain that.

### 10.2 The Right of Self-Ownership ---

Suppose we change the scene in the airport bathroom. I have taken off my jacket and put it on top of my bag. Now when you wipe your hands on it you cannot be said to be wiping them on me. But you are still acting wrongly. You are infringing my property rights, and that is still obviously a disrespectful way of treating me.<sup>13</sup>

My property rights are the rights I have over the use of things which are not persons or parts of persons. To spell out the content of those rights is to specify the ways in which the control of my property is subject to my own determination. They can include first-order claims and privileges with respect to the use (and sometimes destruction) of a thing, and can also include higher-order powers and immunities with respect to the revision of those use-rights and their transfer to others. Applying the earlier treatment of rights again here, the content of these rights is determined by all the reasons that bear on whichever package of higher- and lower-order freedoms we should recognize as morally protected:<sup>a</sup> this leaves scope for property rights of broader and narrower scope with respect to different goods (some that we have a right to destroy, others not, and so on). To specify the boundaries of a property right you possess is to specify the limits of an authority you have—an authority over the use of what is yours.

p. 198 Property rights, so understood, are one of two main kinds of ownership right. The other is the right of *self-ownership*.<sup>14</sup> This also constitutes a morally protected authority, but this time with respect to the use of oneself.<sup>15</sup> Its content, too, amounts to a set of first-order claims and privileges, and higher-order powers and immunities. The content and grounding of these protected freedoms is what we will be going on to investigate; but the view I shall be defending has the conventional implication that the relevant powers are much more tightly restricted than those associated with many property rights: our consent can give others rights to use us in ways they would not otherwise be entitled to do, but we lack the power to alienate the claims and privileges of self-ownership completely, transferring the ownership of ourselves to others.<sup>b</sup>

Ownership rights of either of these two kinds are infringed when others make unauthorized use of what I own. But they can also be infringed when others damage or destroy what I own, without making use of it. I could infringe your property rights through vandalism rather than theft; so, too, I could infringe your right of self-ownership by attacking and paralysing you, thereby depriving you of the use of your body, without making any use of it myself.<sup>16</sup>

In saying that the right of self-ownership demarcates one’s authority over the use of ‘oneself’, I mean to cover three distinguishable objects. It includes a right over the use of one’s body, which is violated in the examples mentioned so far, from Nero to the bike-leaner. It includes a right over the use of one’s agency, which would be violated by someone enslaving me to perform non-manual labour. And it also includes a right over the use of one’s judgement, which is violated in the following kind of case.<sup>17</sup> Suppose you employ me to sell a product that you manufacture, but conceal from me a flaw in the product, taking advantage of my ignorance so that I tell untruths to the customers and thereby sell more of your products than I otherwise would. This would be another way of wrongly using me as a means.<sup>18</sup> Notice that it is a case of wrongful use by omission.

The right of self-ownership should be distinguished from the right to self-determination. The latter is a (bounded) authority over the direction of my life, which excludes this from the domain of others’ agency. It restricts the *ends* that others are free to adopt—such as the promotion of my welfare. The right of self-ownership restricts the *means* that others are free to make use of in furthering their ends, without restricting those ends themselves. When you use me as a means, you are not directing my life for me; you are using me as a resource in directing your life.

The right of self-ownership, then, is the authority one has over the use of one’s body, agency, or judgement. This authority is also bounded. My approach to explaining its boundaries will follow the pattern adopted in treating the right of self-determination in the previous chapter. There, I offered an account of what determines p. 199 the boundaries of the right, rather than a summary description of those boundaries. The ⌞ same approach will be taken to the right of self-ownership. We should start, as before, by explaining where the determinative justification of this right comes from.

#### A responsive derivation

The right of self-ownership, as just described, is more than just a right not to be used by others. It is also a right not to be deprived of the use of one’s body, agency, or judgement by being treated in uselessly destructive ways. It should be obvious how the theory described earlier generates reasons supporting a right not to be subjected to deprivations of this kind. Such deprivations are both harmful and disruptive, so there are reasons of both concern and respect for us to recognize a morally protected freedom from being treated in those ways. But beyond this, I am claiming that our right of self-ownership also includes an authority over others’ use of us, whether harmful (as in cases like Zhukov and Nero) or not (as in the other examples I began with). What is the determinative justification for a right of *this* form?

The reasons supporting this right have more than one derivational source. However, the place to start is with an important class of reasons deriving from the morality of respect. The derivation is responsive: it follows the same general form as the derivation of norms of courtesy described earlier.<sup>c</sup> By following conventions of courtesy, we can communicate our recognition that others have the status of self-expressing equals and of persons whose welfare matters. Courteous ways of treating other people are called for as a way of expressively recognizing those two fundamental forms of moral status—of saying, ‘We are respectworthy and concern-worthy equals.’

Something similar is true of our recognition of the morally protected freedoms that constitute the right of self-ownership. I began with examples of morally objectionable use: why should we recognize a morally protected freedom from being made use of in those ways? One answer is: this is another important way of communicating our recognition of each other’s status as self-expressing agents who call equally for respect. It is a way of saying to each other that none of us is anyone else’s subordinate. If you force me to serve your ends rather than my own, different ends, you thereby compel me to serve as an extension of your own agency. You treat me not as an equally respectworthy, independent agent but as a way of extending your own agency to reach your ends despite their not being mine: in that way you make me subservient to you.<sup>19</sup> The reasons we have to express our recognition of each other's equal respectworthiness are reasons to shape the distribution of freedom that we recognize as morally protected in a way that assigns to each of us an entitlement not to be made subservient in this way.<sup>20</sup>

p. 200 Using the earlier classification, this is a derivation of recognitional responsiveness, of type (c).<sup>d</sup> In a derivation of this kind, the derived norm governs a recognitional response to the existence of the grounding norm. Here, one kind of moral standing is derived from another, more fundamental one. Fundamentally, we have the standing of ↳ self-expressing agents who are equally subject to norms of respect. Our responsively derived reasons to communicate our recognition of that equal standing are reasons to recognize as morally protected a distribution of freedom in which we are unavailable to be subordinated to another person's agency. If these reasons are decisive, we have that right; so our being bearers of that right is a responsively derived moral standing.

This is not to say that our possession of this right depends on whether the freedoms in question actually *are* recognized as morally protected. Our having the right is its being the case that there are decisive reasons for this recognition. The standing you have as the bearer of a right of self-ownership is not something that depends on anyone's conferring it on you: it is its being the case that the associated freedoms *should* be recognized as morally protected. My claim is that the determinative question of why we have this standing is answered by deriving it from another, more fundamental standing we have as self-expressing agents who call to be respected.

Alongside the reasons of respect that support our right of self-ownership, there are associated reasons of concern. When this right is respected, that secures for us an important form of freedom, which is typically good for us.<sup>21</sup> We also have an important dignitary interest in being acknowledged as respectworthy, and the recognition of each other as bearers of this right furthers our welfare in that way too.

Does this demonstrate that there are rights of self-ownership of this form, refuting those who deny it? No; I do not claim that, since alongside the reasons that favour the attribution of such rights, there are also reasons against. There are reasons of concern not to recognize as morally protected a freedom from being harmfully used to prevent several others from each suffering similar harm. So if we think that there is a right not to be used in that way, we are committed to holding that the reasons I cite in favour of its attribution outweigh the reasons against. I do not claim to have provided a case, independent of the plausibility of the view that there is such a right, for that judgement about the relative strengths of the reasons for and against its attribution. Instead, my claim is that this is a sensible explanation of the plausibility of that view. To the extent that we do find it plausible that there can be a moral constraint against some ways of using a person as a means of promoting others' welfare, it can be explained in the way I just did. To this, I add a second claim. Since that explanation acknowledges these competing reasons, it can make sense of why there is persistent controversy over the existence of such a constraint. And its ability to explain this controversy—as a disagreement about the relative strengths of the reasons for and against the attribution of the right in question—is itself a source of support for the plausibility of the moral theory that supplies this explanation.<sup>e</sup>

#### Ownership rights as context-underminers

p. 201 According to some writers, all rights function as context-underminers.<sup>22</sup> I do not go that far; but having argued that this is true of the right to self-determination, I now ↪ make the same claim about ownership rights. They function as context-underminers too. As usual, I split the task of justifying this claim into two. The first task is to identify its determinative justification. In the next section, I provide a further argument that this judgement is warranted.

As I see it, ownership rights and rights to self-determination both function as context-underminers, but there is an important difference. Your right to self-determination is a domain-restricting right: it restricts the ends I am free to adopt, excluding the conduct of your life from the domain of my agency. Your ownership rights, in contrast, do not restrict the ends I am free to adopt, but rather the means I am free to make use of in furthering my ends. So there is not the same domain-based explanation of how they function as context-underminers. Instead, the explanation is meaning-based: it is based on the meaning that attaches to something's being yours or mine.

As applied to property rights, the point is this. Once I accept that a piece of property really is *yours*, I cannot sensibly count considerations of my own personal advantage as legitimate reasons for using it in the service of my own ends. The point of the recognition of property rights, after all, is to demarcate boundaries between what is and is not available for my discretionary use. To recognize an object as *your property* is to recognize that it is unavailable to serve my own advantage. This is not to say that I could not justifiably take someone else's property to save my own life. But the reason for doing so cannot come from the mere fact that it would be more convenient for me to use it.<sup>23</sup>

I think that this gives us another case of meaning-based context-undermining. When you acquire ownership of a piece of property, that changes the meaning of another person's action of using that property without your permission: it is now taking what is *yours*—stealing. This meaning is contrast-relational. For *X* to be yours is for it to be unavailable to serve my personal ends—ends that I happen to have, but that you lack decisive reasons to serve. So when *X* is yours and *E* is a personal end of mine, the fact that *using X in this way will serve end E* is disqualified from being a reason for my action of using it.<sup>24</sup> The contrast-relational meaning of the action prevents this fact from being a reason for it. Considerations of personal convenience cannot sensibly qualify as reasons for actions of stealing.

The corresponding points apply to the right of *self-ownership*. The ownership relation we bear to our bodies, agency, and judgement is acquired not through transfer but by growing up.<sup>25</sup> But again, for these things to be *yours*—for them to fall within the scope of your right of self-ownership—is for them to be unavailable for the service of ends that others happen to have but that you lack decisive reasons to serve. That is to say, actions of taking what is *yours* for my own use have a contrast-relational meaning here as well, like actions of stealing your property. So in the same way as before, the fact that using you will further my personal ends—ends I happen to have, but you lack decisive reasons to serve—is disqualified from being a reason for actions with that contrast-relational meaning.<sup>26</sup> The reasons I have to pursue my personal ends, since they do not make the same claim on you, fail to count in favour of actions that use *you* to achieve them.<sup>27</sup> By respecting each other as the bearers of a right that functions in this way, we ↪ recognize each other as equal bearers of an entitlement to exercise our own agency, without having it subordinated to anyone else.

p. 202 Thus, there is a meaning-based case for treating rights of self-ownership as context-underminers. In calling this a 'meaning'-based case, I am not making a claim about the semantics of the phrase '*X* is yours', but rather about the normative significance of *X*'s *being* owned by you. The claim is not that someone who thinks that his own convenience gives him a reason to use other people's property misunderstands the words 'your property'—any more than someone who fails to appreciate the normative significance of the communal provision of facilities for disabled people is making a semantic mistake about the phrase 'wheelchair access'.<sup>28</sup> The mistake is a normative mistake, not a semantic one: a mistake about the normative significance of the difference between what is yours and mine.

### 10.3 Against a Persistence View of Usefulness

That gives us a determinative justification for the claim that the right to self-ownership functions as a context-underminer. However, more needs to be said to provide a warrant for this judgement. For again, there is a rival Persistence View to consider. This time, it takes the following form. When a person has an ownership right, the fact that an action would infringe that right always counts against the action, but if it would further an end I have reasons to attain, that always counts in its favour. Saying this is consistent with accepting that something is going wrong if I deliberate about reasons of personal convenience in thinking about whether to help myself to others' property or to use them as means to my own ends. If the reasons of convenience are obviously outweighed, I should not deliberate about them. But that only gets us as far as deliberative exclusion, not undermining. Why not say that?

My response to this challenge has the same form as the one I gave in defending the claims I made about meaning-based context-undermining in Chapter 6. There, I defended my interpretation of the train trip example with a balance-tipping argument.<sup>f</sup> When the Persistence View insists that Bill's enjoyment is a persisting reason, it becomes unable to explain why that reason cannot be sensibly invoked in deliberation when all the other reasons fail to determine a verdict. An argument of the same form is available here as well.

To see this, start with a case of wrongly using another person as a means that is at the less serious end of the spectrum. You are in the library, crouching down to reach a book on the bottom shelf; I take the opportunity to step on your back to reach a book on the top shelf. Now modify the example so that something much more important is at stake instead. A bomb will go off unless I press a switch high on the wall, straight away. Obviously, I should step on your back if it is my only means of doing that: my action would not then be wrong.<sup>29</sup>

p. 203 There are two rival explanations of the difference between these cases. On the Persistence View, the relevant difference simply comes from the magnitude of what is at stake. The convenience to me in the first case is a weak reason, outweighed by your right; the reason to save others from the bomb in the second case is strong enough to prevail. On my view, in contrast, what is significant is whether I am using you in the pursuit of my own personal ends, or of ends that you yourself have decisive reasons to serve. In the first case, the fact that it will be convenient for me fails to count in favour of using you; but in the second, saving other people's lives is an end you are morally required to share, so the fact that using you will serve *that* end is not disqualified from being a reason to do so, and the reason is strong enough to justify my action.

So we have two cases, and two rival explanations of the difference between them. Both views read the second case in the same way: there is a reason not to use you as a footstool, but a much stronger reason to do so. But now suppose we weaken the latter reason. By stepping on you to push the button, what I will achieve is the relief of someone else's pain. Whether that justifies my action surely depends on the duration and intensity of the pain: if it will prevent long-lasting agony, it is justified, but not if it will only prevent fleeting irritation. Given this, there will be intermediate cases. We can therefore adjust the pain in our example until we get a case of the following sort: the pain is not great enough for there to be a decisive reason to use you in this way, nor small enough for there to be a decisive reason against. Now it is hard to see what I should do. But let us next ask this: what further features could we add to the case that could help me to decide? Suppose we add that stepping on you, as well as preventing the other person's pain, will also help me to reach a book I need. Surely that makes no difference. Having specified that the other considerations fail to determine what I should do, this cannot now be invoked to tip the balance. And the same is true of the promotion of any other personal end for which my reasons are not shared by you. If stepping on you will further some pet project of mine, or help me keep a promise of mine that has nothing to do with you, or help me fulfil some personal responsibility that you do not share, I cannot invoke such considerations as reasons that tip the balance in favour of using you.

The argument here has the same structure as the balance-tipping argument used in connection with the train trip example. The Persistence View tries to explain why a consideration should be excluded from deliberation by saying that it is relatively unimportant, compared to the right I would be violating. But this fails to explain why it cannot be invoked in a case in which the balance of other considerations favours neither action. It is true that such cases can plausibly be seen as cases of incommensurateness rather than strict equality—cases in which the addition of a small extra reason can still fail to be decisive. However, that does not explain why it is enough simply to know what *kind* of consideration is being invoked in order to know that it cannot be relevant to justifying the action of using you in this way—we do not have to make any assessment of how much support it gives to the action. No matter how great the convenience to me, that goes no way towards justifying stepping on you; but if there is ↪ only a small change to the reason against disrespectful use or the reason in favour of alleviating the other person’s pain, that would need to be attentively considered. The best explanation of this is that the consideration of convenience is not just comparatively small: it is irrelevant.

p. 204

I conclude that this is another case in which a Persistence View is unsatisfactory. We should instead accept that the right of self-ownership does indeed function as a context-underminer, restricting the considerations that can qualify as reasons for infringing it.

### 10.4 Boundaries of the Right of Self-Ownership

An account has been given of how the right of self-ownership is grounded, and why we should think it functions as a context-underminer. Next, we should examine its boundaries.

The boundaries of the right of self-ownership are determined by the answers to this question:

- (1) Is the distribution of morally protected freedom that we should recognize, all things considered, one that protects your self-ownership with respect to use *U*?

For any substitution for *U* which yields ‘No’ as the answer to this question, you lack a right against being used in that way.

For a general summary description of the boundaries of the right, we would therefore need to summarize the difference between the uses that yield positive and negative answers to question (1). I am not going to attempt that: I doubt that there is any such general description that is both true and useful.<sup>8</sup> Answers to (1), after all, depend on all of the reasons that derive from all of morality’s foundations, by all the available derivational routes. The determination of the shape of the boundaries of our right of self-ownership, like any other right, is therefore as complex—and as open to disagreement, in assessing the significance of different contributors—as the underlying structure of morality. So we must be content with some indicative explanations of the ways in which these boundaries can be restricted. Here are three representative examples.

p. 205

Suppose that, monitoring traffic flows on a TV screen, I see that there are injured people beside a deserted road, and that by closing another road I can divert the traffic to make it likelier that they will receive help. In doing that, I would not violate the redirected drivers’ rights: we should not recognize a morally protected freedom against being used in *this* innocuous way. Why not? Because there are good reasons of concern against it, and no comparably strong expressive reason of respect for doing so. That provides us with a relevant substitution for *U*. When *U* is being used in a way that ↪ reasonably distributes a small cost in order to produce a substantial benefit to someone else, without significant expressive disrespect, it is plausible that the answer to question (1) is ‘No’. So there is no right against being used in this way.

Nor is a right infringed by the photographer or surveyor in the earlier examples. Here, *U* is *being used in a way that serves others' self-expression without intruding on your ability to lead your own life*. This is another substitution for which the answer to question (1) is plausibly 'No'—this time, for reasons of respect. The reasons we have to respect each other's innocuous self-expression are reasons not to extend a right against being used in those ways.

For a third example, we can turn to reasons of cooperation. When *U* is *being used in a way that pools a fairly resolved burden to serve a collective action that is worthwhile (all things, including the associated burden, considered)*, we can also have cases for which it is plausible to answer 'No' to question (1). I think this means that there are forms of compulsory national service that use a person without any infringement of her rights.

I say that in these three cases, answering 'No' to question (1) is plausible, and point to reasons of concern, respect, and cooperation in support of this. However, it is also true that in each case there is an expressive reason of respect for recognizing a morally protected freedom not to be subjected to opportunistic use. So my claim is that these are cases in which it is plausible to see the reasons for restricting the right of self-ownership as stronger than the reasons to resist the restriction.<sup>h</sup> However, sometimes it is possible to give a sharper explanation of the limits of the right. The content of a person's self-expression can undermine its respectworthiness, in a way that removes any such expressive reason of respect. Here is an illustration of that.

#### **Content-undermining and the right of self-ownership**

Suppose you are part of a gang that is attacking me: in self-defence, I grab you and use your body to shield myself against the others. Surely in *this* situation you cannot complain that I have infringed your right not to be treated in this way. That would be like a complaint of theft if I grabbed the stick of one attacker to ward off the blows of the others. Here, *U* is *being used in a way that proportionately protects the agent who uses you against your attempt to violate his rights*. Again, it is plausible that this gives us a substitution for which the answer to question (1) is 'No'. However, this is not a case where there are reasons for and against our recognizing your morally protected freedom from being used in this way, with the reasons against outweighing the reasons in favour. Instead, we have a case of content-undermining. Here, the self-expression of the attacker is misdirected in a way that subjects it to oppositional content-undermining. So there are no reasons of respect for extending the protection of a right over *this* kind of self-expression. When I use you in my own defence I am forcing you to serve my end ↴ rather than your own; but the end I am forcing you to serve is the very opposition that your misdirected self-expression calls for.

It is tempting to describe this as a case in which, through misusing your agency, you forfeit your right of self-ownership. But that would be misleading. It is not as though once you attack me I am entitled to enslave you outright, using you to serve ends of mine that have nothing to do with my own defence. The explanation of content-undermining just supplied does not yield any such indiscriminate entitlement. It explains why there is no reason of respect against subordinating your end of attacking me to mine of defending myself; but it does not similarly remove the reasons of respect that still count against subordinating you to other, unrelated ends of mine. So you retain a right not to be used by me in other ways. It therefore seems more accurate to think of this as a circumscription of the right of self-ownership that you possess, rather than an account of how the right can be forfeited.

In Chapter 9, we saw that we also need to invoke relations of context-undermining to explain some of the restrictions on the right to self-determination.<sup>i</sup> However, that seems unnecessary here. There are of course many ways in which the contours of the right of self-ownership are context-sensitive. But we can explain these without invoking context-undermining: they can all be explained as cases in which entering a context amounts to an act of implicit or explicit consent through which one waives a right that would otherwise be possessed (exercising the higher-order power that is part of the authority of self-ownership). For example, the managers of an organization can use its 'human resources' in ways that conflict with the ends of those who are used, without infringing their rights. The acceptance of an employment contract has the structure of a conditional waiver: it amounts to agreeing that the organization can use me for its reasonable ends, in exchange for remuneration. And many competitive contexts (for example, sporting ones) involve an implicit agreement to allow others to try to use you in the ways permitted by the rules of the competition.<sup>30</sup>

### 10.5 Justified Infringements

Thus, some uses of a person do not infringe the right of self-ownership. Others do so permissibly.

The possibility of permissible infringements is obvious in relation to rights of ownership over property. If you have left your towel on the beach while you go for a run, then when I need to use it as a tourniquet to help a shark-bite victim I should take it, whether you have given me permission or not. However, the emergency does not stop the towel from being yours, so if I ruin it I owe you a new one. Here, the structure of the situation is this. The shark-bite victim's needs provide decisive agent-neutral reasons to help him. I am best placed to supply the help, but currently lack resources of my own for doing so. So I should make use of your resources, p. 207and then offer to restore ↳ your loss. (If you are a decent person, you will presumably waive your entitlement. But you do retain that entitlement, and if you do not waive it I must respect it.)

There are justified infringements of the right of self-ownership with the same structure. Suppose that, in order to prevent something terrible from happening, a button needs to be pushed, or a panel shielded from sunlight. I cannot communicate with you, but I can cause you to be knocked against the button, or temporarily paralysed in a position that shades the panel. Then I should do that. But, again, my justification has not extinguished your right. I should still treat you as someone who *has* the authority I am justified in contravening. So I should try to make amends for the way I have used you: I owe you an apology, and if you are bruised in the first case or sunburned in the second, it is my responsibility to ensure that you are looked after.

There is room for justified infringements of the right of self-ownership, on the account I am offering, because of the difference between the right-shaping question:

(1) Is the distribution of morally protected freedom that we should recognize, all things considered, one that protects your self-ownership with respect to use *U*?

and the right-infringement question:

(2) Are there good reasons for infringing the rights generated by positive answers to (1)?

In cases like the ones just described, the answers to both questions are plausibly 'Yes'. There remain good reasons of respect for recognizing you as the bearer of a morally protected freedom not to be treated as a resource in the service of ends you do not share. Should we see you as having the standing of someone who is morally protected, in the sense that using you carries with it a requirement of redress? Yes. But here, the reasons for using you are strong enough to yield a positive answer to (2). So I should subject you to this use, and thereby render myself liable to the requirement of redress.<sup>31</sup>

This is consistent with the earlier explanation of the context-undermining function of ownership rights. There is a conception of *X*'s being *yours*, I argued, on which *X*'s usefulness in serving my personal ends—ends of mine that you lack decisive reasons to serve—cannot sensibly qualify as a reason for my using *X*. Our attribution of ownership rights according to this conception is supported by important reasons of respect. If so, my personal ends cannot provide reasons that support a positive answer to (2): respecting you as a bearer of this right precludes it. But the agent-neutral reasons I have to use your resources for the non-personal ends which you yourself have decisive reasons to serve are not subject to undermining in this way. So they can provide us with cases in which there is a justification for me to treat your resources, including your body, as a necessary stand-in for my own, and then make good your loss.<sup>32</sup> In doing so, I can respect you as a bearer of the right, accepting the requirement of redress that it imposes on me, while responding to the important reasons that justify my treating you in this way.

p. 208 This does not mean that whenever my reasons for pursuing an end also apply decisively to you, I can justifiably use you to serve it. It means that there is something that counts in favour of doing so; but there is also always something that counts against—namely, your right. So I must assess whether the reason to respect your right is outweighed by the reason to promote that important end. When it is not outweighed, we can have a case in which I should respect your right, although you act wrongly in exercising it. For example, when someone asks you for directions and you rudely refuse, you are acting wrongly; but I am not justified in grabbing your arm and making you point the way for him.

### 10.6 Other Objections to Using Others as a Means

So far, I have focused on one way in which using others as a means can be wrong: this can violate the right of self-ownership. I have made four main claims about this right: (i) it has boundaries that are shaped by underlying reasons of concern, respect, and cooperation; (ii) it functions as a context-underminer; (iii) it can permissibly be infringed, when the reasons that are *not* undermined are strong enough; and (iv) it can be wrongly exercised.

However, the violation of this right does not exhaust the ways in which it can be wrong to use someone else as a means. There are at least four others which the theory developed in the previous chapters equips us to explain.

#### Other rights-violations

Journalists who invade the privacy of ordinary people to sell newspapers wrongly use them as a means, but not by violating their right of self-ownership. Rather, this violates another right, the right to privacy. Our recognition of this right derives its justification from reasons of both concern and respect. To be deprived of privacy is typically both psychologically harmful and restrictive of self-expression (since it deprives you of the ability to perform a whole class of actions—the private ones). When, for these reasons, we possess this right, it assigns to us a domain which is protected from intrusion; so, as before, the responses it properly evokes are responses of respect.

#### The exploitation of intimacy

In *The Golden Notebook*, Doris Lessing describes the protagonist Anna's shock when she discovers that her lover Nelson has been using his affair with her—and thus using her—to humiliate his wife. Norvin Richards cites this as a leading example in his own account of one person's wrongful use of another.<sup>33</sup> I agree with him that Anna is wronged, but I think the wrongness of Nelson's action has a different source from the other cases we have been examining. Here, it seems less helpful to characterize the wrongness of his action as the violation of a right. The moral complaint is not that he fails to respect a standing that Anna possesses in virtue of a morally bounded allocation ↴ of *freedom*. Rather, the reasons to which Nelson is failing to respond are reasons of concern. For Anna, her intimacy properly belongs to a relationship in which she is valued more than instrumentally; so this makes it bad for her to be treated this way. Nelson's treatment of her is wrong because it is callous, rather than because it violates her rights.<sup>34</sup>

p. 209

#### Expressive reasons of respect

It can also be wrong to treat others as mere means after their deaths. The Nazis’ use of slave labour and killings in the death factories were one kind of desecration; but their methodical exploitation of the corpses as a material resource was another.<sup>35</sup> The wrongness of using a person’s body in *that* way is not well accounted for as a violation of the right of self-ownership—a failure to respect the dead person’s authority over the use of herself.<sup>36</sup> But instead, we can appeal directly to the expressive reasons of respect that support the recognition of that right. The Nazi economy of death was structured around a systematic expressive denial of the respectworthiness of its victims. The mistreatment of the victims’ bodies after their deaths was a continuation of the denial while they were alive of their moral status as self-expressing equals. To have this status is to be subject to norms of respect: the mistreatment was (among other things) an expressive refusal to acknowledge this status. The reason against the mistreatment of a dead body is a reason to recognize the status that comes from the application of the fundamental norms of respect to all self-expressing agents. The failure to take adequate account of this reason makes such action gravely wrong, independently of whether it violates a right of self-ownership.

#### The preciousness of human beings

I see that as one of the things Primo Levi is expressing when he describes the Holocaust as an assault on his ‘humanity’.<sup>37</sup> It was a systematic denial of his equal considerability as a self-expressing human being, and the claim that gives us all to being addressed by each other as equals. However, Levi’s phrase also points towards another, simpler objection to the Nazis’ treatment of the dead. It was a flagrant violation of the fourth of the foundations I allowed for morality, in its failure of proper reverence for the preciousness of human life.

### 10.7 Implications ---

p. 210 In outline, the account of the wrongful use of others as a means that we have arrived at is this. You have a property-like right of self-ownership that amounts to an authority over the use of your own body, agency, and judgement. Its main determinative justification comes from the reasons we have to express our recognition of the equal respectworthiness of every self-expressing agent. The extension of this right is determined by all the reasons we have bearing on the boundaries of the morally protected freedom ↳ that we should recognize as constituting this authority. The right of self-ownership functions as a context-underminer, in a meaning-based way: the fact that my use of you will promote a personal end of my own fails to count in favour of infringing this right. But the agent-neutral reasons supporting other ends can generate justified infringements. Beyond this, however, there are at least four further sources of reasons against using someone as a means, which can make it wrong to do so.

I conclude by briefly discussing some noteworthy implications.

###### Intention

This account combines the first three of the thoughts surveyed at the beginning of the chapter.<sup>j</sup> It bears out the thought that when I wrongly treat you as a means, I am treating you as a thing, not an agent: I treat you as an extension of my own agency, rather than someone whose own independent agency calls equally for respect. It bears out the complaint that your body is yours, not mine, to control: its explanation of the moral constraints against using others as a means emphasizes the violation of rights of self-ownership. And it bears out the complaint that using you involves a subordination of your agency to mine: this forms the core of the case for the attribution of those rights.

However, it does not include the fourth thought: Nagel's complaint about being 'guided by evil'. That is one way of articulating the central idea behind the 'doctrine of double effect'. There are different versions of this doctrine, but what they have in common is the claim that it is more morally objectionable (other things being equal) to harm someone by intending one's harmful impact on him than it is to produce the same harm as a foreseen but unintended side effect of one's action. The account I have given of the wrongful use of others as a means is not a version of that doctrine. The objectionable use of others need not be guided by the aim of inflicting on them what is bad for them. It is guided by many aims—as many as there are personal ends—without being constrained by a respect for others' entitlement to pursue their own ends. And if that is evil, this is a conclusion to be reached by producing some further explanation of the source of the objection to it, as I have done.

It is true that using someone for a purpose includes acting with a specific intention: one's interaction with the other person is guided towards serving the purpose for which one is using him.<sup>38</sup> However, this does not mean that when one wrongly uses another as a means, the wrongness of one's action always derives from the intention with which one acts.<sup>39</sup> I identified several reasons why it can be wrong to use someone as a means to ends she does not share; and while some derive from the intention of the user, others do not.

p. 211 In particular, the latter is true of the main class of cases I have been discussing: those in which this is wrong because it violates the right of self-ownership. Many rights can be violated irrespective of the intention with which one acts, and the right of self-ownership is one of them.<sup>40</sup> Violations of this right are wrong because they are failures ↴ of respect for your authority over the use of your own body, agency, or judgement, irrespective of the intention with which that failure occurs. One way of violating your right of self-ownership is by using you to serve my personal ends, and in those cases what makes my action an instance of *use* is the intention with which I have acted; but what makes it *wrong* is that it violates your right.<sup>41</sup> Your right functions as a context-underminer, so facts about your usefulness in serving my personal ends are disqualified from being reasons to infringe it. This means that facts about my intended use of you are disqualified from being reasons that could make my action right; but they are not part of what makes it wrong.

However, we saw that the violation of the right of self-ownership is not the only way in which using others as a means can be wrong. This can also be wrong because it is expressively disrespectful. In using you, I can be failing to treat you as my respectworthy equal, and this can be true because of the intention with which I have acted towards you, independently of the violation of any right.<sup>42</sup>

###### Zhukov

We can now return to the opening example: Zhukov's mine-clearers. To apply our account to this case, we must first ask the right-shaping question:

- (1) Is the distribution of morally protected freedom that we should recognize, all things considered, one that protects your self-ownership with respect to use *U*?

As we have seen, the answer to this question can be affected by your explicit or implicit agreement, and by reasons of cooperation. In volunteering to join the army, a soldier waives claims he would otherwise have against being made to serve the ends of his commanders (his waiver being the exercise of a higher-order power over those claims); and there are circumstances of national emergency in which conscription would not violate his rights. However, that does not mean that *all* of the protections constituting the right of self-ownership are thereby alienated—justified military service is not enslavement. Question (1) can still be asked of the uses to which a commander you ought to serve may put you. And when the use in question is stepping on mines to clear a path for other soldiers, the reasons of respect and concern we have for recognizing each other as self-owners support the retention of a right not to be used in this way.

We must then ask the right-infringement question:

(2) Are there good reasons for infringing the rights generated by positive answers to (1)?

The answer to this has to be ‘No’. Zhukov’s soldiers were not morally required to clear minefields for him by stepping on mines. So their right not to be used in this way acts as a context-underminer, preventing their usefulness for this end from justifying the infringement. Therefore, Zhukov wronged them.<sup>43</sup>

p. 212 It is true that pointlessly squandering soldiers’ lives wrongs them too: the right not to be treated in *that* way is not surrendered when one joins the army either. So in ↳ what way was the wrongness of Zhukov’s action distinctive? The answer is not that instrumental use always adds to the wrongness of harmful actions.<sup>44</sup> Were Zhukov to have just capriciously ordered his troops to be shot, that would be no less wrong than it was for him to order them to be marched across a minefield. The action’s not being supported by an adequate reason is what makes it wrong, so gratuitous killing is no less wrong than instrumental killing. Instead, the answer comes from the way in which the right of self-ownership disqualifies some facts from being available to justify an action. When an assault has a low probability of success, its military objectives can still go some way towards justifying it. But that is not true of Zhukov’s objectives in using his soldiers as he did. Zhukov’s failure was not just a failure to take the cost to them adequately into account. It was a failure to recognize their standing as bearers of a right that rendered them unavailable to be used in this way.

###### **The Trolley Problem**

The Trolley Problem is the problem of explaining the moral difference between the Diversion Case, in which a bystander diverts a runaway trolley from a main track on which five people will be killed to a sidetrack on which it kills one, and the Push Case, in which a bystander pushes a fat man onto the track to block a trolley from killing five. Our account of wrongful use supplies a plausible explanation of this difference.

In the Push Case, the fat man is not morally required to jump in front of the trolley to save the others. Therefore, when you push him onto the track in order to save them, you are using him for an end he lacks decisive reasons to serve. His usefulness in serving your personal end is unavailable as a reason for infringing his right of self-ownership: it is subject to context-undermining. So you lack a justification for infringing his right, and your action is wrong. In the Diversion Case, by contrast, the man on the sidetrack is not being used as a means of serving the bystander’s personal end. So the fact that five lives will be saved by diverting the trolley is not subject to context-undermining, and counts in favour of doing so. This is why Diverting is easier to justify than Pushing.

From here, three views about Diverting remain open. One is that, although there is more to be said for it morally than Pushing, it is also morally impermissible.<sup>45</sup> The second is that it is permissible, since the sidetrack man has no right against being killed in a situation of this kind, where a threat is being diverted away from a larger number of people. But I find a third view more plausible: diverting does infringe the sidetrack man’s right not to be killed, but justifiably so.<sup>46</sup> As we saw, the right of self-ownership can include a claim against being treated in ways that deprive me of the use of my body, agency, and judgement, as well as a claim against having them used by others. So the question arises: Do the moral boundaries that constitute this right extend to cover the kind of deprivation the sidetrack man suffers? I think so: killing the sidetrack man should be recognized as carrying with it a requirement of redress. As the bystander, you should kill the one rather than allowing the five to be killed; but in doing so, you would then owe it to *him* to look after his dependants. The p. 213 reasons we have to recognize each other’s importance are reasons to treat them as having a morally ↳ protected standing that includes this requirement. So I think we should see this as an infringement of his right, but one that is justified. His usefulness in serving the bystander’s personal ends could not count in favour of infringing his right; but since he is not being used in that way the reason for diverting the trolley is not undermined.

These claims are subject to the qualification I made earlier.<sup>k</sup> Since the extension of rights depend on facts about the balance of reasons bearing on the different possible ways of locating their boundaries, the judgements I make about them can be contested. But my main point is this: even though, as I see it, Diversion is plausibly seen as a case of right-infringement, it is an infringement which the saving of five lives can count towards justifying. Pushing is different. The usefulness of the fat man as a means of serving the bystander's end of saving the five is not available as a justification for doing that to him.

This is not to say that killing someone as a means of saving others is absolutely prohibited. There are two ways in which this could be permissible. First, the answer to the right-shaping question:

- (1) Is the distribution of morally protected freedom that we should recognize, all things considered, one that protects your self-ownership with respect to use *U*?

can be 'No', even when *U* will kill you. This could be true in a lethal version of the earlier self-defence example, where I use one attacker's body to defend myself against others. When *U* is *being used in a way that proportionately protects the agent who uses you against your attempt to violate his rights*, the answer to (1) is plausibly 'No'. If so, the attacker lacks a right against being killed as a means.

The other possibility is a case in which killing someone as a means justifiably infringes his right of self-ownership. We could have a case of this kind if two things were true. First, the end for which this is done would need to be so important that the victim was morally required to sacrifice his own life for it: it would then be an end he has decisive reasons to serve. And second, it would also need to be impossible for you to give up your own life instead: only then would you be justified in substituting him for yourself. If those conditions were met, your situation would be one where, lacking the resources to perform the action that must be performed for decisive agent-neutral reasons, you would need to use his life in place of yours. And since the right, although justifiably infringed, would not be lost, that would carry with it a requirement of redress, creating an obligation to provide for his dependants.

###### A spectrum

This type of response to the Trolley Problem is sometimes rejected on the strength of the Loop Case—a variant that combines the first two cases by making the sidetrack loop back around in the direction of the five people, and positioning the fat man on the sidetrack where his body will stop the trolley (Figure 10.1). (The trolley will kill the five ↳ and then stop if it is not diverted.) Some writers take it to be intuitively obvious that diverting the trolley remains permissible here, and infer that the impermissibility of the intervention in the Push Case cannot derive from your use of him as a means.<sup>47</sup> I myself do not find it intuitively obvious what we should say about the Loop Case—nor about many of the other variants that have been devised.<sup>48</sup> I think that to arrive at a confident judgement about such cases, we have to reflect on which features of them might be morally significant and why. And when we do that, it seems to me satisfactory to say that adding 'that extra bit of track' in the Loop Case has indeed made a significant moral difference, because now when you divert the trolley the point of doing so, as in the Push Case, is to use the fat man's body to stop the trolley, which is an end he lacks decisive reasons to serve. So, as in the Push Case, his usefulness in serving your personal ends is unavailable as a justification for infringing his right of self-ownership by using him in this way.<sup>49</sup> A further question is invited, though. Why is this less obvious than in the Push Case—why does the moral objection seem less strong? Here, too, our account can help. It sees the case for a right of self-ownership that is violated by using people in these ways as depending on the reasons we have to recognize each other as unavailable to be subordinated to the agency of others. If subordinating use is something that comes in degrees, then we should expect to find a spectrum of cases which vary in seriousness. And we do find this. At one end of the spectrum, there is the Push Case. A somewhat less intrusive form of use is found in the Swap Case, in which a single track has the fat man positioned behind the five (who will be killed by the trolley as it stops), with the bystander’s only options being to leave the situation as it is, or to shift the fat man in front of the five (Figure 10.2). Here, the counterfactual structure differs from the Push Case, since the fat man would die if the five were not present (and it would be morally permissible for the five to leave, exposing him to the threat, if they could). The use involved in the Loop Case is less intrusive again: this has a different causal structure, in which saving the five requires moving the trolley rather than the fat man. The cases on this spectrum involve progressively less intrusive forms of use, and therefore progressively less pronounced ways of subordinating someone’s agency to your own. If we continue along this spectrum, we eventually arrive at the Warning Case (Figure 10.3). The fat man is sitting on the track, oblivious to the oncoming trolley. You could save him by shouting a warning, but five people on the track behind him will then die. Your purpose in not shouting the warning would be to serve the end of saving the five; so this is a case of use by omission. However, I think we have now arrived at a case in which using the fat man in this way does not violate his right of self-ownership.<sup>50</sup> When *U* is *not helping you to avoid a threat, in order to protect a greater number of others from an equally serious threat*, the answer to the right-shaping question is plausibly ‘No’. The reasons of concern and respect for a right that protects us against being subordinated to the agency of others do not provide a compelling case for extending the boundaries of that right to cover an entitlement to be assisted to preserve one’s own life at the cost of other people’s.<sup>51</sup>

What this suggests is that there is a spectrum of progressively less intrusive forms of use, the moral objection to them is progressively weaker, and eventually it yields cases in which the use is morally permissible. An account of the moral significance of using others as a means might be thought to owe us a sharply defined set of necessary and sufficient conditions for the kinds of ‘subordinating use’ against which the right of self-ownership protects us. But I think that is a mistake. If the boundaries of the right are determined by reasons of recognitional responsiveness—reasons to communicate our recognition of a more fundamental moral status—then they will inherit the lack of sharpness of those reasons and the communicative practices they support. So we have to be content with identifying the underlying factors that determine where cases of instrumental use lie on this spectrum. A sharp description of the dividing line between the permissible and impermissible cases is not to be had.

###### p. 216 **Consent**

Finally, what if you consent to being treated as a means? In the main, this is straightforward. The right not to be used as a means is subject, like other rights, to the possibility of waiver, and it is easy to see why. By making our use of you subject to your permission, we can thereby respect your equal status as an agent. So the reasons that support the recognition of our rights of both self-ownership and self-determination are reasons to include this power as part of the authority that constitutes these rights.<sup>52</sup>

However, consider this final example. During the Iran–Iraq war of 1980–88, Iranian *Basiji* volunteered for martyrdom by marching across minefields to clear the way for the troops behind them.<sup>53</sup> (They were equipped with plastic keys before being sent to their deaths, to symbolize their entry directly into heaven.) Many of these suicidal mine-clearers were children, whose consent was irrelevant: their right to self-determination did not include the power to waive their right of self-ownership in this way.<sup>54</sup> And of the adults, no doubt some were manipulated or coerced in ways that compromised the validity of their consent. But what about the others—the wholehearted would-be martyrs? Surely, these suicidal men were acting in opposition to the ends they *should* have had; but there seems no reason to believe that they were all being made to serve ends that were not their own.

Of those men, we should deny that their right of self-ownership was violated. But this leaves open three other possible objections to taking advantage of their willingness to be used in this way.<sup>55</sup> One points to the obvious fact that they were being encouraged to do something that not only ended, but wasted, their lives: so the fact that this was bad for them was a strong reason not to treat them in that way, even if it violated no right of theirs.<sup>56</sup> A second is that their actions violently destroyed valuable human lives, which, like all precious things, call to be protected, not destroyed. And finally, there is this point. Suppose my commander knows nothing of my willingness to serve him, and does not care about that. He will get me to walk through the minefield whether I want to or not. Then his treatment of me still expresses his disrespect towards me, despite my consent. This is not just blameworthy, in not caring about my rights; it also actually treats me in an expressively disrespectful way. It is like the case of breaking a promise to someone who happens not to be inconvenienced by it.<sup>1</sup> He is treating me as though I did not have the right I have waived—as someone whose opinion about how I am to be treated does not matter. So his action is not just blameworthy, but wrong.<sup>57</sup>

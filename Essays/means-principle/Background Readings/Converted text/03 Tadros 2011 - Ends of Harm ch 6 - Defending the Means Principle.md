## 6

# Defending the *Means Principle*

The idea of general deterrence is that some people are punished for their wrongs in order that other people will be deterred from committing similar wrongs. We punish offenders not only in order to deter *them* from committing wrongs against other people, but also in order to deter other people from committing wrongs against other people. The offender is punished with the intention of making it clear to other people what the consequences of offending will be.

An argument for general deterrence justifies punishment in respect of its instrumental rather than its intrinsic value. General deterrence aims at a reduction in the rate of offending, which is considered to be a good consequence that punishment will have. I have already rejected two views of punishment: the retributivist and the communicative views. If I am right in rejecting these views, justifying punishment through its intrinsic value is unappealing. We are better concentrating on the instrumental benefits of punishment. Furthermore, the moral significance of the value sought through punishment must be sufficiently significant to justify the costs of a plausible criminal justice system. Protection from offending is, I believe, the only serious candidate.

General deterrence is often associated with, and defended as part of, a comprehensive consequentialist theory of punishment. But as I have already suggested in chapter 2, although endorsing general deterrence implies that punishment is of instrumental rather than intrinsic value, we have no reason to align general deterrence with consequentialism. All non-consequentialist theories will recognize that there are things that are of instrumental value. The question is whether any particular pursuit of the good can be justified in the light of the best set of non-consequentialist principles and considerations.

The main question is whether general deterrence conflicts with non-consequentialist constraints on the pursuit of the good. These constraints are to be understood in the light of some deeper considerations that ground them. Furthermore, those considerations must be understood in the light of a more general set of ideas about how to understand wrongness. I will have something brief to say about this last question, but the main aim of this chapter is to begin to outline one important constraint on the pursuit of the good and to show where its source lies.

From the description above, we can see that the idea of general deterrence is that some people are harmed in order to benefit other people. Offenders are harmed for the benefit of members of society who are, through the example of punishment, protected from being offended against. Punishment is justified in that harming some people increases the security of others. The people who are harmed are in no way responsible for any threats that are negated or reduced through their being harmed. We punish offenders in order to reduce the rate of threats created by *other people* against *other potential victims* with whom the offender may have no relationship whatsoever.

This seems to suggest that the idea of general deterrence conflicts with a familiar non-consequentialist constraint on the pursuit of the good: the constraint against harming some people as a means to the good of others. Those who endorse this constraint endorse the *means principle*. The general idea of the *means principle* is that whilst it may be permissible to pursue the good where this will have, as one of its side effects, some lesser harm to others, it is not permissible to pursue the good where others will be used as a means to achieve that good. There are various ways to make this principle more specific, and I will outline my preferred view in the next chapter.

But whether we should endorse the *means principle*, as a general principle, under *any* specification is highly controversial. It is typically rejected by consequentialists, of course, who suggest that it is permissible, or perhaps even required, for us to cause harm as a means to a greater good. But some non-consequentialists also think that there is no general constraint on harming some people as a means to achieve a greater good.<sup>1</sup> As we will see, the *means principle* has highly intuitive implications.<sup>2</sup> But whether the principle is capable of adequate justification has been debated. Many people think that we cannot defend it either in the light of contractualist reasoning, or as an independent principle constraining our actions. We have reason to care about not being harmed, and in being protected from being harmed, these people argue, but no reason to care about how we are harmed, whether as a means or as a side effect.<sup>3</sup>

I will not be able to provide a knockdown argument for the *means principle*, and to my knowledge no one has managed to do that. I will be able to show that its implications are attractive. Furthermore, unlike the idea of desert, there is no obvious alternative way to explain our considered judgements about these implications. There is no principle that has been outlined that has anywhere near the intuitive plausibility of the *means principle*. Finally, whilst a knockdown argument for the principle is elusive, we can provide some kind of defence of the principle in theory. It is somewhat vague, but perhaps not all of our deepest moral convictions are very sharp or capable of being justified in a more satisfying way.

## I. Some Answers to the Trolley Problem

I will begin by considering some cases that are familiar to moral and legal philosophers: variations on the Trolley Problem and related cases. These cases are helpful in sharpening our perception of what is at stake in this debate. They have stimulated different moral principles and ideas that are said to be at the heart of a non-consequentialist morality.

Here is a version of the well-known Trolley Problem<sup>4</sup> (for those who don't know, by the way, a trolley, in some parts of the USA, is a tram. Supermarket trolleys are nowhere near as dangerous). It invites a comparison between two cases.

*Trolley Driver.* The driver of a trolley is hurtling down a track and he realizes that the brakes have failed. Five people are on the track in front of him, and if he does nothing the trolley will run into them and kill them. However, he can turn the trolley onto another track. If he does so, the trolley will run into one person, killing them.

*Bridge.* The driver of a trolley is hurtling down a track and the brakes have failed. Five people are on the track in front of the trolley. However, there are two people on the bridge between the trolley and the five. One of those is a large person, large enough to stop the trolley if he falls in front of it. The other is a very strong small person. The only way for the strong small person to prevent the trolley hitting the five is by knocking the large person off the bridge so that he will fall in front of it, killing him.

The problem is that it seems permissible for the driver to turn the trolley in *Trolley Driver* but wrong for the strong small person to push the large person off the bridge in *Bridge*. In both cases, there would be a net saving of four lives. So what's the difference between the cases?

One answer to this problem, the answer that I favour, is grounded in the *means principle*. It is permissible to kill the one person in *Trolley Driver* because doing so produces a significantly greater good (a net saving of four lives). That is also true in *Bridge*. But in *Bridge* only using one person as a means can save the five. It is wrong to kill one person to save five if the one person is used as a means, but not if the death of the one is a side effect of the good-producing action.

<sup>4</sup> I've cleaned this up a bit from the original problem developed by Philippa Foot in 'The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of Double Effect' in *Virtue and Vices* (Oxford: OUP, 2002).

There are different versions of this principle. One version, which is sometimes known as the Doctrine of Double Effect, claims that the way that we should understand this principle is that it is wrong to intend to kill one person to save five but it is not wrong to save five, foreseeing that the act of saving the five will result in the death of the one. Another version, which Frances Kamm has called the Doctrine of Productive Purity, claims that we should focus on the causal relations between the death of the one person and the death of the five. In *Bridge*, the killing of the one itself causes the five to be saved. In *Trolley Driver*, in contrast, the five people are saved by an act which *also causes* the death of the one.

The difference between these two views is that the first claims that the permissibility of an act turns on the motivation with which it is done. Intentions matter to permissibility. The second view claims that mind-independent facts, such as causal relations, are all that matter to permissibility. In the next chapter I will consider these two views and defend a version of the view that motives are relevant to permissibility. At present I will simply refer to the *means principle* as the relevant principle, without distinguishing clearly between these two interpretations of it.

Another answer to the Trolley Problem draws on the idea that it is worse to kill than to let die. In *Trolley Driver*, if the driver does nothing he will *kill* the five. If he turns the trolley he will kill the one. And it is better to kill one than to kill five. In *Bridge*, in contrast, if the small strong person does nothing he lets five die, and it is better to let five die than to kill one. In that case, there might or might not be a significant role for subjective features of the conduct to play, depending on whether it is appropriate to describe an action as a killing where there is no intention involved in one's action. If not, then we may have to add to *Trolley Driver* that the driver started the trolley up. Doing nothing would then involve killing the five.

In this chapter I will show that two more general objections to the *means principle* fail. That will also allow us to understand the principle more deeply. The first, suggested by Philippa Foot, and recently defended by Judith Jarvis Thomson, claims that the principle that there is a stronger constraint on harming than allowing a person to be harmed can provide a complete answer to the Trolley Problem. It is common to think that it is worse when I cause some harm when compared with failing to prevent that harm. We might think that what distinguishes *Trolley Driver* from *Bridge* is that the trolley driver will cause harm to the five people if he does not divert the trolley. Hence he is permitted to divert the trolley to the track with the one person on it. Acting to kill one person is not as bad as acting to kill five. In section II I will show that this idea has very strongly counter-intuitive implications in a range of cases and that Thomson's defence of it fails.

The second, suggested by Shelly Kagan<sup>5</sup> and John Gardner,<sup>6</sup> claims that the *means principle* implies that there are agent-relative restrictions on action that are difficult to motivate. Agent-relative restrictions on action justify moral constraints by reflecting on what it will mean for the agent doing the action that he acts in a certain way. It might be thought that intending to harm a person, for example, corrupts me. My motivations are especially mine, and I ought to be concerned to maintain my own moral purity.

<sup>5</sup> *The Limits of Morality* (Oxford: OUP, 1989).

<sup>6</sup> 'Complicity and Causality' in *Offences and Defences: Selected Essays in the Philosophy of Criminal Law* (Oxford: OUP, 2007).

Agent-relative constraints are very difficult to motivate. They seem to encourage agents to focus in the wrong way: inwardly on their own moral purity rather than outwardly on the good or harm that they do to others. Kagan and Gardner seem to think that the *means principle* is valid only if there are such constraints.<sup>7</sup> Drawing on Frances Kamm's work, I will show that this is not true.<sup>8</sup> The *means principle* need not be defended on agent-relative grounds. It may be grounded in the moral status that each person has. We each have such a status only if we endorse certain constraints on harming some as a means to the good of others. Hence my defence of the *means principle* will be thoroughly agent neutral. This will provide a deeper account of what is at stake in accepting or rejecting the *means principle*. That will divert us in section III.

This will provide a platform for an investigation into the limits of the *means principle*. It is often suggested that the *means principle* cannot be an absolute principle. It is surely sometimes permissible to harm a person as a means to the greater good. But, some claim, any restrictions on the scope of the principle are inevitably ad hoc. Either the restriction should make us inviolable against being harmed as a means or we should conclude that there is nothing special about being harmed as a means. I demonstrate that this is false. There is a principled way in which we can set limits on the *means principle*. We do so by determining the scope of our duties. It is often permissible to harm a person as a means to a greater good, I claim, if that person would themselves have a duty to pursue that good even if doing so would harm them to that degree. I begin to show that this idea is plausible in this chapter and consider it in more depth in later chapters.

## II. The Limited Power of the Doctrine of Acts and Omissions

Perhaps the most important alternative to the *means principle* as an explanation of the Trolley Problem has been the doctrine of actions and omissions. If endorsing this principle alone had attractive implications we would have good reason to prefer it to the *means principle*. For it is independently attractive to think that there is an important difference between harming another person and letting another person be harmed. Ordinary morality certainly condemns wrongful actions to a much greater degree than wrongful omissions.<sup>9</sup> And it may seem easier to motivate the doctrine of acts and omissions than the *means principle*.<sup>10</sup>

<sup>7</sup> The idea that limits on consequentialism are necessarily agent relative is also implied in D Parfit *Reasons and Persons* (Oxford: OUP, 1984) 27.

<sup>8</sup> John Broome also notes that constraints need not be understood in agent-relative terms. See *Weighing Goods* (Oxford: Blackwell 1991) 9–10.

One way to illuminate this idea is to consider the costs that a person must bear to justify failing to save another person when compared with killing another person. Suppose that a stranger is drowning in a lake. If the only way to save them is for me to lose a leg, it is common to think that I am not required to save them. Suppose now that I am driving a motorbike that has gone out of control. I am about to hit a truck that will cause me to lose my leg. The only way to prevent this from happening is to divert the bike into you, but if I do that I will kill you. In that case, it is common to think that I may not divert my bike into you. If this is true, the costs that I have to bear to avoid harming you are greater than the costs that I must bear to prevent you from being harmed. And that suggests that it is worse to harm than to fail to save.<sup>11</sup>

But although the distinction between acts and omissions can explain the cases with which we began, relying on this principle alone has troublesome implications in other cases. One problem is that it does not explain cases where it seems permissible to turn the trolley even though failing to do so would be an omission rather than an action. For example, in a variation of *Trolley Driver* suppose that it is a bystander rather than the trolley driver who has the option to turn the trolley. Let's call the case *Bystander*. In that case, the bystander would be failing to save the five if he does nothing. Hence, he would be omitting rather than acting. And yet it seems wrong to think that the bystander is in a different position from the driver.

Many people share the intuition that it is permissible for the bystander to turn the trolley. But perhaps we should reject that intuition. Recently, Judith Jarvis Thomson has suggested an argument to the effect that it is wrong for the bystander, unlike the driver, to turn the trolley.<sup>12</sup> In her attempt to drive us to that conclusion she considers:

*Bystander's Three Options.* A trolley is hurtling down the track towards five people. A bystander could turn the trolley onto a second track, but there is one person on that track who will be killed if he does that. He could also turn the trolley onto a third track, but *he* is on that track and he will be killed if he does that.

<sup>9</sup> There may be differences between the action/omission distinction and the doing/allowing distinction. These need not distract us here.

<sup>10</sup> Although it is not as easy as one might think. For discussion, see V Tadros *Criminal Responsibility* (Oxford: OUP, 2005) ch.7.

<sup>11</sup> For a more fully developed version of an account of the doctrine of actions and omissions based on the costs that agents must bear, see F M Kamm *Morality, Mortality* vol.2: *Rights, Duties, and Status* (Oxford: OUP, 1996) ch.4.

<sup>12</sup> 'Turning the Trolley' (2008) 36 *Philosophy and Public Affairs* 359.

At the beginning of this section I suggested that a person does not have to bear very significant costs to save others from harm. This suggests that the bystander would not be required to divert the trolley onto his own track. But, Thomson suggests, the person on the second track is in just the same position as the bystander except that she doesn't have control over the trolley. The bystander, we might think, cannot impose a cost on the person on the second track if he would be unwilling to bear that cost himself.

Thomson's argument for this view is as follows. In turning the trolley away from the five, the bystander wishes to do a good deed—to save the five. But if he wishes to do it, he must bear the cost. He cannot pass the cost of his good deed on to another person. The bystander is not *required* to divert the trolley onto his own track. But if he would not do that, he may not divert it onto the track with one stranger on it. From this, Thomson suggests, we should conclude that it is wrong for bystanders to divert harms away from a greater number of people to a smaller number of people in general. If the bystander *would not* bear the cost of turning the trolley onto his own track were that option available to him he may not impose that burden on the one person.

Why does Thomson believe that it is generally wrong to make another person bear the cost of one's good deed? To bolster her view, she draws an analogy with giving to charity. Suppose that I want to give to charity. I can either use my money or I can steal from you and give the money. It would be wrong for me to steal your money and give it to charity, imposing the burden of my good deed on you rather than me.

However, there is a significant difference between this case and *Bystander's Three Options*. In *Bystander's Three Options*, were I to turn the trolley towards the one person I would not use the one as a means to save the five. In contrast, in stealing from you to give to charity I would harm you as part of my plan to do the good. Of course, it may sometimes be permissible to take resources from others for the sake of the good. Let us suppose that general taxation already takes from you the portion of your wealth that you are required to pay for the poor. In taking your property for the sake of charity I would be forcing you to make a contribution to a goal that you have no duty to contribute to, and that would involve harming you as a means. If it is true that it is wrong to harm one person as a means to produce the good, we have an explanation of why I am not permitted to steal from you to give to charity.

More generally, though, we should not draw conclusions about the permissibility of turning the trolley in *Bystander* from what we would do in *Bystander's Three Options*. I am not sure what the bystander is permitted to do in *Bystander's Three Options*. Here is a plausible view. The bystander is permitted not to turn the trolley. If he turns it, however, he must share the cost equally between those who are in a position to bear an equal share. If that is right, if the bystander is to turn the trolley away from the five people, he must not place all of the cost on the stranger on the line, but must share it equally between the stranger and himself. He could do this by flipping a coin.

Suppose that it is also true that, in consequence, he is unwilling to turn the trolley. He is unwilling to bear a 50 per cent chance of the loss of his life to save the five. He is permitted not to turn the trolley, in this case, for were he to act consistently with his other moral obligations, an obligation to turn the trolley would involve deciding to bear a 50 per cent cost to his life for the sake of saving five, and that sacrifices independence to too great a degree.

But the fact that he wouldn't turn the trolley in that case does not render it wrong for him to turn the trolley in *Bystander*. The fact that a person would not be willing to impose a cost on herself in *pursuing a goal* does not entail that she ought not to impose the cost on others *as a side effect*. To see why, consider what it means to say that it is permissible rather than obligatory to act in a certain way. To say that it is permissible provides us with an option to take something as our goal. We may be permitted to do that even though there are some costs associated with doing that thing. When I decide whether to adopt that thing as my goal my decision depends on the importance that it has to me and also on the extent to which the costs of doing it interfere with other things that I take as my goal. The existence of the extra option in *Bystander's Three Options* alters the way in which the costs will fall if the trolley is turned. That makes a difference to whether the bystander will take the saving of the five as his goal. If he decides to turn the trolley he decides to bear a cost in the service of a goal. If the cost is large, he is not required to do so. Were he required to do so he would be required to bear a great cost for the sake of others, threatening his independence.

In contrast, in *Bystander* no one is made to bear a cost in the service of a goal at all. It would be wrong to force the stranger in *Bystander's Three Options* to turn the trolley himself if that would result in the stranger either being killed, or even bearing a 50 per cent chance of being killed. In that case, it would be wrong to harm the stranger in this way *even if* the bystander would be willing to bear that cost in the service of the relevant goal. One cannot force a person to do what they have no duty to do simply because the thing is worthwhile and one would be willing to do it if one could.

The fact that it is wrong to force a person to harm himself obviously does not show that it is wrong to harm a person in a way that does *not* co-opt him into participating in a scheme to service a goal. Take the stranger who complains to the bystander: you would not be willing to use yourself in the service of this goal at this cost. To this, the bystander can reply: well, I am not using you in the service of this goal either, and I would not do so. In other words, Thomson's argument simply assumes that we should treat being required to bear a cost in the service of a goal as equivalent to having a cost imposed on one where that is not done in the service of a goal, but simply as a side effect. The fact that one would be unwilling to *use oneself* for the service of a goal may tell us something about the permissibility of *using others* for the service of a goal. It may be wrong to *use* a person at a cost to them in the service of a goal where one would be unwilling to use oneself at the same cost in the service of that goal, even if it would otherwise be permissible to use the person. I'm not sure whether this is true. But even if it is true, it tells us nothing about the permissibility of turning the trolley in *Bystander*. For no one in *Bystander* is used at all.

The better set of parallel cases that might be used to test the permissibility of harming the stranger in *Bystander*, we might think, compares harming a person as a side effect, as in *Bystander*, and allowing oneself to be harmed as a side effect. We might ask: would the person turning the trolley, were she on the single track, interfere with the decision to turn the trolley if she could do so, even at the cost of the lives of the five? She ought not to turn the trolley in *Bystander* if she would interfere with the turning of the trolley were she in the position of the stranger and she was able to do so. One ought not to impose a cost on a person as a side effect, it might be argued, if one would not be willing to bear that cost as a side effect. And the answer to that question depends on whether one would willingly bear the cost, or whether one would avoid the cost were one able to do so.

Whether this is the right question to ask depends on whether it would be permissible for the stranger to interfere with the turning of the trolley to save his life were he able to do so. For it could be the right question only if the stranger is permitted to interfere with the decision of the bystander to save the five, killing him. In chapter 9 I will argue that he is not, and this renders the comparison just drawn irrelevant. The fact that he would be willing to act wrongly if he was in the same position as the stranger cannot render it wrong to harm the stranger.

Even if this argument is mistaken, and we should reject the permissibility of turning the trolley in *Bystander*, there are further problems with utilizing the principle that it is worse to harm a person than to let them be harmed to resolve cases of this kind. One problem is that there are variations on *Trolley Driver* in which the driver has a choice to harm one person as a means to prevent the harm to the five. And it seems impermissible for the driver to do this. For example, suppose that the trolley driver can stop the trolley, saving the five people, only by pushing one of his passengers under the wheel. Thomson's argument relies on the idea that the trolley driver, were he not to do anything, would kill the five. For that reason he, but not a bystander, is permitted to alter the direction of the trolley. But if there were nothing special about harming a person as a means, we would then expect not only that the trolley driver can divert the path of the trolley to kill the one, but also that he could harm a person as a means to prevent him from killing the five. It follows that it would also be permissible for him to throw the passenger under the wheels, but that is difficult to believe.

And it seems generally wrong to use one person as a means to prevent oneself causing a greater harm. For example, suppose that I wrongfully put a trolley in motion that will kill five further down the track. I have a change of heart and want to save the five. It would be wrong for me to avert the threat by throwing a person in front of the trolley, killing them. This suggests that the constraint on harming a person as a means has independent significance beyond the general prohibition on harming others.

Furthermore, there are other familiar cases in which it seems wrong to fail to save one person where failing to save a person even if the harm that would be caused to that person would lead to a greater good. For example, suppose that one person in hospital is dying and I could easily save him from that fate. However, if I let him die I could use his organs to save five other people. It is difficult to believe that it is permissible for me to allow the person to die for that reason. It seems permissible to divert resources away from one person in order to save five others, but it is not permissible to allow one person to die in order that their body can be used as a means to save the five others.

Perhaps it might be thought that the use of organs or the context of the hospital has special significance in these cases. But the idea generalizes across contexts. Suppose that a child is about to fall in front of a bus. If I give the child a hand I will save her, but then the bus will hit five other children. I must save the one child. Failing to do this would really be no different from throwing a child in front of the bus to save the five. Were I not to do so, when the child's parent asks me why I didn't save her child, all that I could say was that it was necessary to leave her child there in order to stop the bus so that the other five were saved. But that would be to regard the one child as a useful tool for saving the five.

## III. Means and Status

For many non-consequentialists, the *means principle* provides a constraint on acting. But why is there such a constraint? In general we aim to promote the good. We have reason to promote good states of affairs and reasons for refraining from producing bad states of affairs. In the Trolley Problem that I outlined above, the relevant states of affairs that we produce seem to be identical. In *Trolley Driver* and in *Bridge* one person is killed and five people are saved, a net saving of four lives. It seems clear that we are permitted to achieve the net saving of four lives in *Trolley Driver*. Why should we care about how that net saving is produced?

One answer focuses our minds not on the number of lives saved, but on the quality of the actions that are produced through our conduct. In general, we might think that it is morally significant not only that a certain outcome, such as death, is produced. We might care about the quality of the action that produces it. For example, we might think that it is worse that murders occur than accidental deaths.

People who defend this view might claim that we have much stronger reasons to rescue people from murder than we do to rescue people from natural disasters, an issue that we began to explore in the previous chapter. They may think (not very plausibly)<sup>13</sup> that there are much stronger reasons to prevent harmful actions than harmful outcomes.<sup>14</sup> Suppose that I am in a boat. A mile away to my left, a villain is drowning one person. A mile away to my right, two people have had their boat overturned by a shark which is chasing them down. I have time to save either the one person from being drowned or the two from being eaten by the shark. I cannot save all three. People who focus on the quality of actions in cases like this might argue that we have much stronger reason to rescue people from murder than from being eaten by sharks. Hence, I ought to turn left and save the one person from being drowned.

<sup>13</sup> I discuss this view further in chapter 5.

<sup>14</sup> This is John Gardner's view, for, in 'Complicity and Causality' in *Offences and Defences*, Gardner focuses particularly on preventing the wrongdoing of others rather than on preventing harm.

This kind of explanation might be available to solve the Trolley Problem. In *Bridge*, we might say, I murder one person to save five. In *Trolley Driver* I don't murder the five to save the one. This supposes that only killing as a means is sufficient for murder, but killing as a side effect is not.<sup>15</sup> But let us suppose, for the moment, that directly intentional killing is a much worse quality of act than foreseen killing, and hence refer only to the first of these killings as a murder.

This still does not give rise to a uniform constraint on harming a person as a means. Here's why. Suppose that harming as a means is a worse action than harming as a side effect. That suggests that we ought particularly to be concerned with harm as a means. If we are concerned with harm as a means, we ought to be concerned to reduce the instances of harm as a means. But if that is right, we ought sometimes to harm one person as a means if that has as its consequence the prevention of a greater number of harms as a means. In other words, it would be permissible for me to murder one person to prevent five other people from being murdered.

We can make things still more precise than this. Suppose that there are five people, each of whom is about to harm one person to save a further five. If I do nothing five people will be harmed, resulting in the saving of a further 25. If we think that harming one person to save five is particularly bad, then we have very strong reasons to prevent this happening. But what if the only way to prevent the five from doing this is to harm one person? Am I permitted to harm one person to prevent five others from being harmed as a means?

If I do so I will prevent five people from being harmed as a means to save 25 others. I will have harmed one person as a means to achieve this. But overall, only one person will have been harmed as a means to produce the good. If I don't harm the one person, I will not have treated anyone as a means. But I will have allowed five people to be treated as a means. And given that I have a very strong reason to prevent others being treated as a means, I should be pleased that there is a net reduction in the number of people who have been treated as a means. So that is what I should do. And if that is what I should do there is no general constraint against treating people as a means: I am permitted to treat people as a means to prevent other people being treated as a means.

<sup>15</sup> This is an idea which is not accepted in English criminal law, of course. See *R v Woollin* [1999] AC 82 for the latest statement on the *mens rea* of murder.

This kind of argument, if it were valid, would seem to have profound implications for punishing the innocent. Wrongdoers often treat the people they wrong as a means. Many rapists harm their victims as a means to sexual pleasure, for example. Suppose that punishing one innocent person in a way that harms him to the same degree as victims of rape are harmed would prevent five rapes. If the above argument is true we should punish the one innocent person. For in doing that we would minimize the instances in which people are harmed as a means. And there is even more reason to affirm that conclusion. In punishing the innocent person, we would be harming him as a means to the *good* end of preventing rape. In raping, rapists do not treat the person as a means to the good. The sexual pleasure that rapists get from raping, I take it, does not count as a good. So we might even think that it is permissible to punish one or more innocent people to prevent *one* rape. This is not an attractive result.

Another argument is that there are special agent-relative constraints on action.<sup>16</sup> It might be argued that the fact that *I* harm one person as a means ought to have special significance in my reasoning. If I do nothing *I* do not treat anyone as a means. Hence, I remain morally pure. But whilst it is common to think that constraints on action have an agent-relative foundation, there are difficulties with this view. It seems strangely self-regarding to worry about my own moral purity when there are all of these other people around who might be harmed or saved depending on what I will do. Agent-relative constraints might seem to direct the focus of agents in the wrong way: towards oneself and one's moral purity rather than towards others. And that does render them difficult to motivate.<sup>17</sup>

A third possibility, defended by Warren Quinn, Frances Kamm, and Thomas Nagel, is more promising.<sup>18</sup> We might think that we each have a status such that we are not to be harmed as a means for the sake of a greater good. The idea that we should build our moral principles out of our moral status is something that has been regarded as central to non-consequentialist thought since Kant, so it should not be surprising that this kind of solution is attractive to non-consequentialists.

The idea is this. If it were permissible for me to harm you as a means in order to prevent five others from being harmed as a means, *each of us would be available* to be treated as a means—we would each be available to be harmed for the sake of a greater good. So each of us would have the same status as an object—available for use by others to promote the good. Consider two ways in which another person might regard me as 'like an object'. If the person uses me as a means to pursue their goal, I am treated like an object. Objects, but not humans, may be used to pursue goals. Another way is that the person regards me as available for use to pursue goals. Objects are available to be used in this way but persons are not. On the latter idea, it is wrong to use me to pursue your goals. But this is only derivative of the fundamental idea: that I am not available to be used. If I am permissibly used to pursue the goal of preventing others from being used, fewer people are used to pursue goals. But each person is available to be used to pursue goals: namely the goal of preventing others being used to pursue goals. If we permit this, we regard it as important that some are not used to pursue goals. But we imply that everyone is available for use by each other person: to pursue the goal of preventing the use of others.

<sup>16</sup> See T Nagel *The View From Nowhere* (Oxford: OUP, 1986) 175–80.

<sup>17</sup> See S Kagan *The Limits of Morality* ch.2 and J Gardner 'Complicity and Causality' in *Offences and Defences*.

<sup>18</sup> See W Quinn 'Actions, Intentions, and Consequences: The Doctrine of Double Effect' in *Morality and Action* (Cambridge: CUP, 1993), F M Kamm *Morality, Mortality* vol.2 ch.10 and T Nagel 'The Value of Inviolability' in P Bloomfield (ed) *Morality and Self-Interest* (Oxford: OUP, 2008).

If we reject the permissibility of harming one person as a means even to save five others from being harmed as a means to the greater good, our status remains intact. For by failing to prevent the five from treating five others as a means to the greater good we do not *endorse* their acts. The five who are treated thus are wronged. The five wrongdoers may not have *recognized* their moral status. But as the acts of wrongdoers are not endorsed according to the moral principles to which we adhere, the status of the five remains intact.

This conclusion helps to vindicate something that I suggested in the previous chapter: what matters most is *having* a certain moral status. Whether others respect our status matters much less. Those who think it permissible to use a person to prevent more people being used wrongly focus on the quality of the actions involved directly, when the importance of the quality of actions, in this context, is to be derived from the more fundamental idea that our moral status distinguishes us from objects.

This suggestion reveals the paradox in the idea that it is permissible to harm one person as a means in order to prevent five people from being harmed as a means. If we permitted one person to be harmed as a means to save five others from being harmed as a means, we would appear to minimize the number of actions that show a lack of recognition of a person's moral status. But in fact, given that it would be permissible to harm one person as a means, we would no longer *have* the status that we are not to be harmed as a means. After all, we would regard it as permissible to harm one person as a means for the good of others. We would then regard it as unimportant that we maintain such a status. And there would no longer be a reason to focus on means at all, but only on harm. Hence, we would have no reason to harm a person as a means to prevent five people each from being harmed as a means to produce a greater good.

If this is right, at the heart of the moral person is not only the desire to produce or prevent certain actions or outcomes: those that are in accordance with our moral status. We also must show respect for our moral status by endorsing and living by the principles that are consistent with our having that status. Despite the fact that even some who defend this view fail to grasp this point,<sup>19</sup> this idea is thoroughly agent neutral. There is a prohibition on anyone using anyone else as a means. It is only if that general prohibition is valid that we each have the status of persons: that we are not available for use by each other to pursue the good. This has nothing to do with agent-relative constraints, which are constraints that apply to particular individuals in virtue of some feature of them, or because of some quality that they will have if they act in certain ways. The constraint on using others is grounded in the status that each of us has and which grounds prohibitions on actions that apply to all of us.

<sup>19</sup> See T Nagel 'Personal Rights and Public Space' in *Concealment and Exposure and Other Essays* (Oxford: OUP, 2002). Nagel suggests that the idea of status grounds agent-relative constraints, but fails to explain what is agent relative about them.

We see a parallel in the case of rights. A person may violate my rights. But if she does so, that does nothing to erode the right that I have not to be treated in that way. I still *have* such a right even if it has been violated. And I might care about whether I have such rights in the first place. That depends not on what people will do, but on what they are permitted to do. This is also why, *contra* Jean Hampton,<sup>20</sup> we should not regard those people who are victims of crime as having lost moral status, something that we would then need to *repair*. We may need to do something to *affirm* the status of people who have been wronged: wrongdoers may deny that their victims have such a status, and that may cause the victim and others to doubt their status as well. And we may have good reason to ensure that offenders recognize that what they have done is wrong. As we have already seen, this is a legitimate role for the criminal law to play. But wrongdoing causes no loss of status that needs reparation.

The idea that the *means principle* is grounded in moral status also helps to explain our intuitions when it is objects rather than agents that we harm. Suppose that five people are each about to destroy a beautiful rare flower that belongs to no one. They will refrain from doing so if I destroy a sixth beautiful flower that belongs to no one. Because flowers do not have the moral status of agents I believe that it is permissible for me to destroy a flower for the sake of the net gain of four flowers. If we endorse the view that there are agent-relative constraints on doing wrong we ought to come to the perverse conclusion that it is wrong to destroy the flower even at the cost of five other flowers being destroyed.

Of course, those who think that there are much stronger reasons to prevent wrongful actions than harmful outcomes might accept that it is permissible to destroy one flower intentionally to prevent five others being destroyed intentionally. Five destructive actions are prevented by one destructive action in this case. But they seem compelled to accept perverse consequences in other cases. If I can throw one beautiful redwood that belongs to no one in front of a trolley in order to stop the trolley, preventing it from destroying five equally beautiful redwoods that belong to no one, I surely do not act wrongly. But if we take seriously the idea that there are stronger reasons against performing destructive actions than there are to prevent destructive outcomes an implication would be that it is wrong for me to act in this way. After all, were I to do nothing, no redwoods would be destroyed through an action. There would simply be a bad outcome that no one had wrongfully produced.

<sup>20</sup> 'Righting Wrongs: the Goal of Retribution' in *The Intrinsic Worth of Persons: Contractarianism in Moral and Political Philosophy* (Cambridge: CUP, 2007). See, further, the discussion in chapter 4.

The same thing can be said about non-human animals. Intuitively, it is permissible for me to use one deer in a way that will cause it pain in order to prevent five deer each suffering the same degree of pain. Because deer lack the status of humans, there is nothing wrong with using them as a means. It is wrong to cause animals pain, but not because they have the moral status that humans have. This further bolsters the conclusion that we should focus on the way in which principles permitting one person to be harmed as a means to the good would be inconsistent with moral status, rather than focus either on the way in which it would morally corrupt *us* as agents, or on the significance of harmful actions more generally. It is because people have a particular status, as agents rather than objects, that we are not permitted to use them as means.

The fact that there is no constraint on using animals that is equivalent to the constraint on using humans suggests that our moral status is grounded in capacities that are distinctive of humans. The most obvious reason is this: as humans we are capable of determining what ends to set for ourselves. We do so by determining what we will value amongst the range of things that are valuable. Our right to set ends for ourselves can be understood as derived from our independence from each other—the autonomous control that we have over our own lives. Non-human animals lack this status because they lack the relevant capacity—to determine what is valuable and to pick out particular things to value.<sup>21</sup>

When a person is used as a means, she is used in pursuit of a certain goal. But as an independent person, she ought to be able to set goals for herself. It is wrong to compel her to act for the sake of some end that she is permitted to reject for herself. Her permission to reject the goal is grounded in her status as an independent end-setter. Because non-human animals cannot set goals for themselves in this way, there is no special difficulty in using them in the service of a goal. It is often wrong to harm non-human animals. It is not wrong to harm them in the service of a greater good, however. There can be no complaint that they were forced to pursue a goal that they would have been permitted to reject, for they lack the appropriate capacities that render end-setting so morally significant.

## IV. The Limits of the *Means Principle*

Some people think that the *means principle* provides an absolute constraint on harming people. They think that we can *never* harm one person as a means to the good of others. Others think that where harming one person as a means is required to prevent some great disaster, we are faced with a tragic choice where it is inevitable that we must do something wrong; not only something that is *prima facie* wrong but something that is wrong in some more absolute sense. I don't think that these views are very plausible. There are some cases when we are permitted harm one person as a means to avert harm to others.

<sup>21</sup> There are special difficulties that I cannot address here with respect to humans who, due to severe cognitive difficulties, lack the capacity for end setting. It is intuitive that they cannot be used as a means. It may be that their relationship with those who have the relevant capacities grounds the application of the *means principle* to them.

For one thing, a person may be harmed as a means to prevent a *much* greater harm. Suppose that the only way to prevent a nuclear explosion killing one million people is to push one person in front of the trolley that is carrying the nuclear device, killing her. Even if the one person would survive the nuclear blast were it to occur (say because that person is wearing a radiation suit) it is permissible to kill the one person as a means to save one million.

It might be argued that this is true because the death of one million people is a special kind of catastrophe. We are permitted to harm one person as a means to prevent catastrophe, but not to prevent greater harm. But even if this idea has some force, it does not restrict the permission to harm a person as a means. What is also important is the *relationship* between the harm to be prevented and the harm that the person would suffer in being used as a means to prevent it. We can see this by creating an example with reduced levels of harm, both to the person who would be harmed as a means and to the person who would be saved by harming that person. For example, suppose that the only way to prevent one person being run over by a bus is to push a second person into the one. Doing that will cause the second person some bruising, but it will save the life of the one. This is permissible even though the other person is harmed as a means to save the life of the one. As bruising is very trivial compared with the loss of a life, it is permissible to bruise the second person as a means to save the life of the one.

To capture this we might say that the fact that a person will be harmed as a means to the good acts as a multiplier on the significance of the harm in deciding what it is permissible to do. Suppose A will suffer 1000 units of harm unless B is used as a means to prevent that harm. In being used as a means B will suffer one unit of harm. In that case it is permissible to use B as a means. And this is true no matter how big each unit of harm is. But if this is the right view, it is itself in need of explanation.

Furthermore, the idea does not seem applicable in all cases. There may be some cases where it is wrong to use a person in the service of a goal where that person is not harmed at all. For example, it is wrong to have intercourse with a person who is in a coma even if that person's prospects are in no way set back.<sup>22</sup> It is wrong to do so even if some harm is prevented by doing so. It would be wrong to have intercourse with the person in these circumstances even if doing so would somehow cure me of the flu.

<sup>22</sup> See J Gardner and S Shute 'The Wrongness of Rape' in J Horder *Oxford Essays in Jurisprudence 4th Series* (Oxford: OUP, 2000). For further discussion, see V Tadros 'Harm, Sovereignty, and Prohibition' *Legal Theory* (forthcoming).

And there are cases where it is permissible to harm a person as a means to a much *greater* degree than is suggested by the multiplier idea. For example, in at least some cases, we are permitted to harm a person as a means to avert a threat that the person bears moral responsibility for creating. If you have sent a trolley down a slope in order to kill five people on the track, it is permissible for me to throw you in front of the trolley if that is the only way for me to prevent it from killing the five. In fact, that would be permissible even if killing you was required to save *one* person. This example suggests that some other factors such as responsibility, causation, or culpability might be important in determining the limits of our status not to be used as a means. Which of these factors is important is a controversial matter, one that I will address in Part III of the book when we consider the scope of a person's liability to be harmed in cases of self-defence and other cases.

When we consider these cases we will get a better understanding of the *means principle*. The best underlying explanation of them is as follows: it is permissible to harm a person as a means to an end if that person would have had an enforceable duty to avert the threat were she able to do so, even if, in exercising her duty she would be harmed to the same degree. The idea is that when we harm a person as a means, we impose a cost on that person for the sake of the pursuit of a goal. It is wrong to do this if that person would not be required to pursue that goal at the same cost were she able to do so. It is also wrong even if she would be required to act if it would be wrong to force her to carry out her duty—i.e. if the person has a right to do wrong.

Return to the case where the person suffers some bruising as a means to save the life of the person who would otherwise be run over by a bus. Most people think that there is a duty to rescue others from being harmed, but that this duty is not very stringent. If I can save you from being killed at some small cost to myself I must bear that cost. If I can prevent you from being run over by a bus, but that will lead to me being bruised, I must bear the bruising in order to save your life. And if I have a duty to accept being harmed in order to save your life in the case involving the bus others are permitted to make me bear that cost. For, with some exceptions, I cannot complain about being made to suffer the degree of harm that I have a duty to bear in order to avert a threat to you. What we see from this example is the important relationship between the *means principle* and the duty to rescue. The extent to which a person can be harmed as a means to the good of others depends on the scope of the duty to rescue.

The duty to rescue and the *means principle* have a common source. They are both concerned with the extent to which we are required to accept that we must bear some cost for the sake of others. And they both have similar limits. In the case with the bus, the person cannot complain that he is used as a means because the cost that he bears is one that he has a duty to bear. Let us explore this in a little greater depth. There is a distinction to be drawn between actions that are merely permissible and actions that are required. There is often a range of permissible things that a person can do: she can set some valuable goals amongst a range of potential goals that she could have adopted as her end. She may recognize the value of a broad range of goals, but may come to adopt a narrower set of goals within that range as things that she values. When she values a goal that goal provides a special consideration in her life.<sup>23</sup> She structures her decisions around the thing that she values. Setting herself a goal that is not *the best* amongst the range of goals that she might have set for herself does not make her action wrong. Recognizing this fact is important: restrictions on action are set in a way that allows people to give shape to their own lives. They may do so by coming to value only some of the things that are valuable.

When we require a person to act for the sake of some goal we put a constraint on her ability to decide which goals to adopt. When we do that we must ensure that it does not interfere too greatly with her ability to give shape to her own life by structuring her plans around the things that she values. The scope of our duties is constrained in this way: we don't always have a duty to do what is best because were we to have such a duty we would not be able to give shape to our own lives in pursuit of the things that we value.

For that reason, there are limits on the duty to rescue: we need not bear a great cost for the sake of saving another person from harm for were we to have such a duty we would not be able to adopt a set of plans to pursue the things we value. We would have a particular end, the saving of the person to be rescued, imposed on us at the expense of the pursuit of the things we value. When a person is harmed as a means to an end that person is also required to bear a cost for the sake of that end. His life, or his autonomy, is sacrificed for the sake of that end. It is wrong to require a person to sacrifice his life or his autonomy for the sake of an end that he need not adopt as his end at that cost. Hence the duty to rescue and the *means principle* are corollaries of each other.

In helping to confirm this view, let us consider a set of cases which begin with the duty to rescue and end up with a person being harmed as a means. Let's start with a standard rescue case:

*Pond Rescue.* A child is drowning in a pond. I am the only person who can rescue the child.

Whether I have a duty to rescue the child depends on the costs that I would incur were I to do so. People differ about the degree of cost that would vitiate the duty. Most people believe that if saving the child would cost me my life I need not save the child. If saving the child would only cost me my shirt I must save the child. But what if saving the child will cost me my little toe, or my finger, or my foot, or my leg, or all of my limbs? I'm not sure how much cost I must bear. Let us stipulate that I must save the child if it will cost me my finger but I need not do so if it will cost me my foot. We work on the assumption that my particular projects and goals will be set back further if I lose my foot than if I lose my finger, and that losing my foot will be more painful.

<sup>23</sup> For more on the distinction between regarding something as valuable and valuing it, see M. Bratman 'Three Theories of Self-Governance' in *Structures of Agency* (Oxford: OUP, 2007).

Here is another way of putting this idea: if saving the child will only cost me my finger I must take saving the child as my end, despite the cost. If saving the child will cost me my foot I need not take saving the child as my end, given the cost. Normally I *may* save the child if doing so will cost me my foot but doing so is supererogatory. This will not be true, though, if there are other stringent duties that I will violate by losing my foot. Suppose that I need my foot to save two of my children. It would then be wrong for me to save the child at the cost to my foot. This might even be true if my foot is necessary for my own sake. I may have self-regarding duties.<sup>24</sup> Normally, though, the person who saves a child at a cost to his foot is a hero not a villain.

#### *i) Enforceable Duties*

Suppose that saving the child would cost me my finger. I have a duty to save the child. Now suppose that I love my finger and hence refuse to save the child. You would like to see the child saved, but the only way to do this is to coerce me. Suppose that you can coerce me by threatening me that if I don't save the child you will chop my finger off anyway. Is it permissible (or even required) for you to threaten me? We can call a duty that it is permissible to coerce a person to fulfil an *enforceable* duty. I think that it is permissible, and perhaps required, for you to coerce me to save the child. If so, I have an enforceable duty to save the child. If I ignore your threat then it may be wrong for you to cut off my finger. So you may only be permitted to threaten me insincerely. Or perhaps you may cut off my finger as punishment, in which case you would be permitted sincerely to threaten me.<sup>25</sup> Either way, you are permitted to threaten me in order to get me to rescue the child.

It may be objected that it is not always permissible to coerce a person to do their duty. This is true. We sometimes have a right to do wrong, in the sense that it is sometimes wrong to prevent a person doing wrong.<sup>26</sup> It may be wrong to prevent a person doing wrong even if preventing the person from doing wrong costs them nothing. But where my wrongdoing is grounded in the fact that I will do, or fail to prevent, very serious harm to another I typically have no right to do wrong.

<sup>24</sup> For further discussion, see V Tadros 'Consent to Harm' *Current Legal Problems* (forthcoming).

<sup>25</sup> I discuss threats in more detail in chapter 12.

<sup>26</sup> For a good discussion, see J Waldron 'A Right to do Wrong' in *Liberal Rights: Collected Papers 1981–1991* (Cambridge: CUP, 1993).

Whether a duty is enforceable depends on a range of factors. These include the moral significance of the duty, the extent to which it is likely that the ambitions that ground the duty are likely to be advanced through enforcement, the harms that will typically be imposed on the duty bearer through enforcement, the extent to which it is important that the person acts on the duty for good reason rather than because she is forced to do so, and so on. In this case, we are mainly concerned with a fundamental interest, the interest in being free from harm, which grounds the duty to save others. Given its moral significance, where life is at stake our duties are typically enforceable. It is not wrong to coerce me to prevent me from killing others and it is not wrong to coerce me to feed my children if that is the only way to get them fed. Similarly, it is not wrong to coerce me to save the child at a cost to my finger.

But if you coerce me to save the child at a cost to my finger you also harm me as a means to a greater good. You have the goal of saving the child. I do not. You coerce me to save the child to serve *your* end of saving the child. You impose that end on me in the sense that you require me to pursue the end of saving the child. And you do this even though it will cost me my finger. The fact that it is not wrong for you to do this helps to demonstrate that it is not wrong to harm a person as a means to a greater good where the person has an enforceable duty to pursue the greater good.

In this case, you coerce me to rescue the child by threatening me to do what I am required to do. Now imagine that I love my finger and I do not respond to your threats. What you can do is to push me into the pond. If you do this then the child will be able to clamber to safety. However, I will lose my finger (say because it will be eaten by piranhas). I believe that just as it is permissible for you to coerce me into saving the child it is also permissible for you to push me into the pond if I don't respond to your threats. In both cases you will use my body for your ends. In one case you do that by interfering with my will. In the other you do it by taking control of my body, bypassing my will. In both cases you manipulate my body by subjecting it to your will.

Here is another way of understanding these claims. Within certain limits I am given control over my body. I can put it to use in the service of any end within those limits. If I have an enforceable duty to put my body to use in the service of some end you may coerce me to use my body in the service of that end. And if you are unable to do that you may use my body in the service of that end. It is permissible to take command of another person's body and use it for an end if the person has an enforceable duty to use their body in the service of that end.

I now want to consider cases where the person cannot do her duty without assistance. Consider the following variation on *Pond Rescue*.

*Assisted Pond Rescue.* A child is stuck in a pond. The child can be rescued from drowning only by blocking a rivulet flowing into the pond, preventing the water rising above the child's head. I cannot do this alone for I am temporarily paralysed from the neck down. You cannot do this alone for you are too small to block the rivulet. However, the rivulet could be blocked if you were to push me into it. If you did this I would lose a finger.

Are you permitted to push me into the rivulet? I think you are. I have a duty to rescue the child at a cost to my finger. I cannot do this without assistance. But I could do it by authorizing you to push me into the rivulet. It is difficult to believe that I have a duty to rescue the child in *Pond Rescue* but not in *Assisted Pond Rescue*. The fact that I can rescue the child only with your assistance does not negate my duty to rescue. I have a duty to get you to assist me if I can. Now suppose that I am unwilling to authorize you to rescue the child. If there is no significant difference between *Pond Rescue* and *Assisted Pond Rescue*, and I believe that there is not, I have an enforceable duty to authorize you to push me into the rivulet. And if I fail to authorize you to do that you are permitted to push me into the rivulet without my authorization.

Perhaps it might be argued that this conclusion does not follow. Whilst I can compel you to rescue the child, or compel your body to rescue the child, you cannot compel me to authorize you to do anything. If authorization is required for it to be permissible for you to use my body you cannot use my body without authorization. It does seem true that it is important for you to *seek* my authorization before pushing me in. The question is whether my refusal to authorize you to push me in should be treated as decisive.

Those who think that it is are likely, I think, to have been influenced by the typical role that authorization has. Normally, authorization is required to make it permissible for one person to interfere with the interests of another person. For example, it might be permissible to perform a certain operation on me that will improve my health only if I authorize that to be done.

In *Assisted Pond Rescue* the duty that you have to seek my authorization has a different role. By requiring you to seek my authorization I retain a degree of control over what happens to my body; the control that I would have were I not temporarily paralysed from the neck down. And I should prefer that my body be pushed into the rivulet after I have authorized you to do it. For in that way I do my duty in rescuing the child with your assistance. Better to do my duty with your help than to have my duty imposed on me. This is just the same judgement that we make about *Pond Rescue*. It is better that I rescue the child of my own free will than that I am coerced into doing so by you. But just as in *Pond Rescue* if I fail to do what I have a duty to do, you may force me to do my duty.

I now want to consider a more difficult case. Suppose that I am not able to authorize you to push me into the rivulet. Suppose that rather than being paralysed from the neck down I am unconscious. You cannot wake me up. You can push me into the rivulet though. My face will be above water so I will survive, but I will lose my finger. Is it permissible for you to push my body into the rivulet?

We might distinguish this case from the previous case on the following grounds. In the previous case were I not to authorize you to push me into the rivulet I would violate my duty to save the child. I have an obligation to authorize you to push me into the rivulet and if I cannot do that you can force me to do what I was required to do. In this case, in contrast, there is no question of me doing anything wrong. Being unconscious I can do neither right nor wrong. This difference, I will suggest, is less significant than it may seem.

The first thing to note is that it is certainly permissible to use an unconscious person's body as a means to *some* degree in some cases to avert a threat. Suppose that my child and I are in a car accident. We are both unconscious. If nothing is done I will make a full recovery but my child will die. The child can be saved but only by operating on me in a way that will cause me minor discomfort for the rest of my life. It is obviously permissible to operate on me—but why?

One familiar way to resolve the question of what to do to, or with, a person's body where that person is unconscious is to consider what she would consent to were she able to do so. For example, in deciding whether to perform an operation on a person who is unconscious we ask what the person would want were they conscious. It may be wrong to perform a blood transfusion on an unconscious Jehovah's Witness even if it will enhance her well-being to do so. Perhaps even this does not provide an absolute restriction on what it is permissible to do: perhaps it is not wrong to perform a blood transfusion on a Jehovah's Witness if doing so is required to save her life.

We might draw on this idea to argue that it is permissible to operate on me to save my child because I would consent to the operation were I able to. It is not clear what justifies us in considering what a person would consent to were she able to. Perhaps hypothetical consent is the best way of determining what is in the person's interests. Or perhaps hypothetical consent is the closest that we can come to ensuring that the unconscious person determines what will happen to her whether or not that is in her interests. I am not sure what the right answer to this question is, though I suspect that the latter answer is better than the former.<sup>27</sup>

Whatever the answer to it, though, there are some limits on the role that hypothetical consent can play in determining what can be done to (or with) a person's body when she is unconscious regardless of what she would consent to. One kind of limit is that we cannot use the idea of hypothetical consent to impose a weighty burden on a person that she would have no duty to bear were she conscious. Secondly, we cannot use the idea of hypothetical consent to justify the decision to refrain from imposing a burden on a person that she has a duty to bear.

We can see that the first conclusion is attractive by considering the following example. Suppose that both a stranger and I are unconscious. Using my body as a means could save the stranger, but that would lead me to lose my foot. Suppose also that I often go beyond the call of duty. Were I conscious, you conclude, I would save the stranger at the cost of my foot even though I am not required to do so. Would it then be permissible for you to use my body as a means to save the stranger? I'm not sure but I doubt it. As far as supererogatory acts go, actual authorization is probably required. Hypothetical consent is insufficient.

The converse is also true. If a person *would not* consent to the use of her body to do something that she is required to do that does not make it wrong to use her body for that purpose. Suppose that I wouldn't authorize you to use my body to save a child at the cost of my finger were I able to do so. Does that prevent you from using my body as a means to save the child? I think not. The fact that were I able to I would violate an enforceable duty by refusing to authorize you to use my body cannot compel you not to use my body.

If actual consent is not required for you to use my body as a means to save the child in *Assisted Pond Rescue*, hypothetical authorization is not required for you to use my body as a means to save the child where I am unconscious. In other words, where a person is unconscious and hence incapable of authorizing us to use her body as a means to a good it is permissible to use her as a means to the extent that duty would require of her were she able to consent and no more.

We should conclude that the inability to authorize use of our bodies makes little difference to the permission to harm a person as a means. If a person would have a duty to allow her body to be used for some good end were she able to consent to its use, we are permitted to use her body as a means to that end regardless of whether she would consent were she able. If a person has no duty to allow her body to be used for some good end were she able to consent to its use, it would be wrong to use her body as a means to that end regardless of the fact that she would consent were she able. Hence, the permissibility of using a person as a means does not depend on whether that person is conscious.

Just as it is true that it is permissible to harm a person as a means based on the enforceable duties that she has, or that she would have were she able to rescue, it is wrong to harm a person as a means if she lacks such a duty. For example, suppose that a person needs to be rescued from drowning. I am the only person who can save that person, but if I do so I will lose my leg. I am not required to save the person at the cost of the loss of my leg. For my ability to pursue the things that I regard as valuable would greatly be interfered with through the loss of my leg. Were I to be required to save the drowning person I would be required to adopt the life of the person drowning as my end at a very significant expense to my pursuit of other things that I value. So my ability to shape my own life in pursuit of the things I value would be greatly interfered with.

That is not to say that consent makes no difference to the permissibility of harming others, of course. As it is permissible for a person to sacrifice her life for the sake of the life of another, it is also permissible to help her to do this, even if it involves harming her. But in this case, there is no question of harming the person as a means—she has set herself the end of saving the person’s life, and so it is mistaken to imply that she is merely used for that end.<sup>28</sup>

This analysis also helps to explain the other case that I discussed, involving the wrongdoer who sets the trolley down the track. That person is responsible for creating the threat through his wrongdoing. For in this case the person who is responsible for creating the threat *does* have a duty to avert that threat, even at the cost to his own life. If he could prevent the trolley from hitting the five by throwing himself in front of it, it is plausible that he would have a duty to do that. And hence it is permissible for others to throw the wrongdoer in front of the trolley. As this person, through his wrongdoing, does have a duty to adopt the end of averting the threat at a cost to his own life it is not wrong to compel him to bear that cost for the sake of the end either by coercing him or by using his body as a means to pursue that end.

This helps us to resist the idea that restrictions on the scope of the *means principle* are ad hoc.<sup>29</sup> It is sometimes complained that we must either treat the *means principle* as absolute or reject it altogether. For example, if the *means principle* is grounded in the idea that we are inviolable, it is implied that the *means principle* is absolute. As few would believe it to be wrong to harm a person to a modest degree for the sake of saving the life of another, this view would undermine the plausibility of the *means principle*. But if we understand the *means principle* as a violation of the duty not to compel a person to do what they have no duty to do, we can meet this objection. Where a person has a duty to pursue some end at a cost to herself, there will often be nothing wrong with compelling the person to bear that cost for the sake of that end. For example, because I am required to feed my children, there is nothing wrong with threatening me to feed my children. That is so even though I am threatened as a means to the good of the children, and it is so even if feeding my children is costly to me.

It also helps to explain why there are cases in which a person may not be used in the service of a good even if using them does them no harm. For example, in the case where one person has intercourse with another person in a coma, the person in the coma would have no duty to provide the first person with sexual pleasure, even if there was no cost involved in providing this service. Hence, it is wrong for the first person to use the second for sexual pleasure in this way. Of course, we need an argument why there is no duty to provide others with sexual pleasure. But assuming that we can find one, and it seems as though we will be able to, we can explain why it is wrong to use the person at no cost to herself.

Can this idea also be used to justify a weaker restriction in cases where the person harmed is harmed as a side effect? It can. Where a person is harmed as a side effect of the pursuit of the good, the person harmed is not compelled to take the good as her end. There is no question of compelling a person to pursue a goal that she has no reason to pursue. For in cases where a person is harmed as a side effect to save a life she is not compelled to adopt the saving of the life as her goal. She does not contribute to the pursuit of the goal and so pursuit of the goal is not imposed on her. And hence it is permissible to harm a person as a side effect in the pursuit of a greater good even if that person does not have a duty to aim at the greater good herself. She is not required to expend her life for the sake of the greater good if she is harmed as a side effect. Although her ability to set goals for herself may equally be eroded through this action she is not compelled to adopt the lives of those who are rescued as her own goal. And were we not to turn the trolley the ability of the five to pursue *their* ends would also be undermined. Hence, in this case, unlike in cases like *Bridge*, she cannot complain that she is forced to do what she has no duty to do. She is not forced to do anything, or to be harmed in an equivalent way.

For this reason it is permissible to turn the trolley away from the five and towards the one in *Trolley Driver* even though the one would not have a duty to turn the trolley away from the five and towards himself were he able to do so. In *Trolley Driver* we can minimize the erosion of people's abilities to pursue their own goals. We are compelling no one to adopt the lives of others as a goal for themselves. In *Bridge* this is not so. We could rescue the five only by requiring the one to participate in pursuit of a goal that he is not required to pursue.

To improve our understanding of the scope of the *means principle*, then, we must understand the way in which it interacts with other duties. The *means principle* is a principle that is concerned with averting harm to some by intentionally harming others. But if those others have a duty to bear the harm, the *means principle* may not be violated. This is very similar to the case where I coerce you to do what you have a duty to do. Coercing a person to act is wrong, but coercing a person to do what they have a duty to do is often not wrong. It is wrong for one person to threaten another person with harm even to do some trivial action. But it is not wrong for one person to threaten another person with harm to ensure that the other person fulfils a basic duty such as the duty to rescue another person from a lethal threat.

## Conclusions

If we reject the significance of moral status we have no special reason to think that harming one person is wrong if it produces the greater good. So we have no special reason to object to general deterrence as the justification of punishment. In that case, we should endorse general deterrence as a justification of punishment without further argument.

But if there is no special reason to be concerned about harming one person as a means to a greater good, we also have only weak and contingent reasons to object to punishing the innocent or punishing the guilty disproportionately if doing those things will achieve a greater good. Of course, punishing the innocent often *won't* produce a greater good, and certainly an institution that sometimes permitted punishing the innocent would be highly unstable.<sup>30</sup> That, though, is an empirical assertion. Most people think that both punishment of the innocent and disproportionate punishment of the guilty violate basic moral requirements—doing these things is fundamentally unjust rather than merely imprudent.

The most obvious moral reason to doubt the permissibility of punishing an innocent person, even in circumstances where doing so will produce a greater good, is that it conflicts with the *means principle*. The *means principle* is a significant feature of ordinary morality, and one which can be given some plausible, even if controversial, moral foundation. If we accept that principle, the best account of it is one that rests on the moral status that we have, and that we wish to preserve, not to be available for use by each other in a way that harms us.

But even if we accept this idea, it is not very plausible that our moral status is pure. Our moral status does not ground an absolute constraint against harming one person as a means to produce the good. Our desire to maintain our moral independence through endorsing a set of rules that conform to the *means principle* may be less significant than the value that we put on avoiding some much greater harm.

Furthermore there may be other reasons to reject principles that do not permit harming one person as a means to achieve a greater good. If a person has wrongfully created a threat, that person can be harmed as a means to prevent that threat occurring. That is so even if the good produced is not significantly greater than the harm that we would do to that person. That invites us to try to make further progress on the limits that there are to the *means principle*. We do so by focusing on the enforceable duties that people have to pursue the good. Ultimately, I will show that a system that punishes the guilty for reasons of general deterrence ought to be accepted even if we accept the *means principle*. But there are several issues to divert us before we get there. The first thing to do is to consider the best interpretation of the *means principle*. That will provide my focus in the next chapter.

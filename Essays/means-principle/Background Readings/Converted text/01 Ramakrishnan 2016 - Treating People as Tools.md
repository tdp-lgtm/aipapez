The moral asymmetry between failing to aid in *Guinea Pig* and *Scarcity* and the moral asymmetry between harming in *Block* and *Explosion* appear to have a unified foundation. In other words, there seems to be a common explanation of why the actions in *Guinea Pig* and *Block* are so difficult to justify, some principle in virtue of which these actions are much harder to justify than their counterparts in *Scarcity* and *Explosion*. This same principle, whatever it may be, also seems to condemn a range of more familiar actions, such as dropping lethal bombs on innocent civilians in order to terrorize their government into ending its unjust aggression (*Terror Bombing*). Nonconsequentialist moral philosophers have expended considerable energy trying to determine what this principle is.

Most of them have pursued one of two basic strategies. The first strategy explains the special difficulty of justifying harming in cases like *Block*, and failing to aid in cases like *Guinea Pig*, by reference to the fact that the agent in these cases *intends harm* for another person. The second strategy explains the special difficulty of justifying these actions by reference to the way the agent *uses* or *exploits* the person whom he harms or allows to suffer harm. In my view, the second approach is the more promising one, but I do not think that existing accounts develop it in a fully satisfying way. So I will defend the following account in this essay:

*Utility*: It is especially difficult to justify infringing a person's rights on the basis of her usefulness to others.

As I will explain, *Utility* presumptively disables the fact that a person will be useful to others only if her rights are infringed from grounding a sufficient justification for infringing her rights. I will argue that this principle has some important extensional advantages over other principles that seek to explain our intuitions about cases like *Guinea Pig* and Block.<sup>3</sup> It also has considerable intuitive appeal in its own right. For it seems plausible that, if a moral theory routinely abrogates our moral protections on the basis of our usefulness to others, it treats us too much like tools or resources for others' gain.<sup>4</sup> One attraction of Utility is that it renders our rights especially resistant to infringement on this basis.

I

#### Consider

*Heart Attack:* Elsa has a rare but harmless medical condition that is related to a deadly disease currently threatening the lives of five other people. Scientific prodigy Waldo is preparing to run some harmless medical tests on Elsa, without her consent (she is unconscious), in order to devise a cure for the disease and save the five. Just as Waldo is about to run his tests, however, Elsa starts to suffer a lethal heart attack; unless this heart attack is treated immediately, its effects will eventually kill her. Waldo can treat Elsa's heart attack, thereby stopping it and saving her life. But if he takes the time to do this, she will be useless for his purpose of saving the five, for he needs to start testing Elsa immediately if he is to devise a cure in time to save them. So Waldo decides to leave Elsa's heart attack untreated and run his tests on her instead. Some time later, after he has obtained the results he needs from testing her, she dies from the heart attack's effects.<sup>5</sup>

Waldo's action in this case seems impermissible, and it seems to be impermissible on the same grounds as the failure to aid in Guinea Pig. The failure to aid in Guinea Pig may be wrong for many reasons, but the most *important* reason it is wrong seems to apply to the failure to aid in Heart Attack as well. That is why the failure to aid in Heart Attack does not seem much less wrongful, if less wrongful at all, than the failure to aid in Guinea Pig.

Our moral theory should be able to explain why Waldo treats Elsa impermissibly in Heart Attack. It should also be able to capture our intuitive sense that the failures to aid in Heart Attack and Guinea Pig are impermissible on largely the same grounds. I will now argue that existing principles that seek to explain the impermissibility of failing to aid in Guinea Pig can perform neither of these explanatory tasks, since these principles have nothing to say about Heart Attack (or close variants on it that I will soon offer). To be clear, I am not trying to establish that all of these principles are incorrect. But the fact that these principles do not condemn the failure to aid in Heart Attack (or its variants) suggests that they do not capture the most significant reason that the failure to aid in Guinea Pig is impermissible. For these failures to aid seem equally wrongful, or nearly so, and this appears to be in virtue of some significant feature they share.

#### A. Harmful Intention

Consider, first, *Harmful Intention*.<sup>6</sup> According to this principle, it is presumptively impermissible to act on an intention that another person suffer harm (or something “close” to harm), because intending harm is more objectionable than merely foreseeing harm. This principle is widely endorsed as the explanation of why the actions in Guinea Pig and Block are impermissible.<sup>7</sup> The closeness clause of the principle is meant to account for the fact that the agent, in such cases, need not actually intend harm for anyone. For example, even though the agent in Guinea Pig intends that the one person from whom he withholds medication suffer her deadly disease, he need not intend that she actually die, from the disease or any other cause. Nevertheless, the defender of Harmful Intention will claim, the state of affairs that consists in suffering this deadly disease bears a very tight metaphysical connection to the state of affairs that consists in suffering death. Because of this tight connection, intending the former state of affairs inherits the moral objectionability of intending the latter. And so it is wrong to intend that someone suffer a deadly disease, even in order to save five other people.

Even if Harmful Intention successfully handles Guinea Pig, in this way, it cannot explain the wrongfulness of Waldo's failure to aid in Heart Attack. For Waldo does not intend harm, or anything plausibly regarded as close to harm, for Elsa. The only thing that Waldo intends for her is that she be harmlessly tested by him, thereby playing a certain role in his plan to save the five. Playing this role does not metaphysically constitute suffering the harm of death,<sup>8</sup> nor is it inconceivable that Elsa should play this role without suffering harm,<sup>9</sup> nor is there a true *ceteris paribus* generalization that being harmlessly tested in this way issues in harm,<sup>10</sup> nor are we lacking for close-by possible worlds in which Elsa is harmlessly tested but does not suffer harm.<sup>11</sup> None of these accounts of closeness, therefore, can support the suggestion that Waldo intends something close to harm for Elsa. It is hard to rule out the possibility that another account of closeness will eventually succeed where these existing accounts fail. But I think it is unlikely that any such account is forthcoming, especially since there does not seem to be, intuitively, any tight metaphysical connection between what Waldo intends for Elsa and the harm she suffers. There is, instead, only the utterly contingent connection that he cannot both use her and save her from harm.<sup>12</sup>

#### *B. Harm as Means*

Let us turn, then, to a related principle, *Harm as Means*.<sup>13</sup> According to this principle, if the harm a person suffers (or something “close” to the harm she suffers, or her being affected in a harmful way) is a necessary causal means of bringing about a certain good, that good does not justify harming the person or allowing her to be harmed. This principle may be able to explain the impermissibility of failing to aid in Guinea Pig. For it might be argued that the one’s suffering something close to harm (that is, her deadly disease) is a necessary causal means to the good of the five’s continued survival, for which reason this good does not justify letting the one die. But Harm as Means does not explain the impermissibility of letting Elsa die in Heart Attack, since neither the harm Elsa suffers, nor anything plausibly regarded as close to this harm, nor her being affected in a harmful way is a necessary causal means to saving the five. Indeed, none of these things is a means *of any kind* to saving the five. If Elsa were not suffering a heart attack, Waldo’s ability to save the five would not be hindered in any way.

#### *C. Causal Intention*

Consider, next, another widely endorsed principle for explaining the impermissibility of the actions in Block and Guinea Pig. According to *Causal Intention*, it is presumptively impermissible to act on an intention that another person be used or affected in a way that will foreseeably cause her harm.<sup>14</sup> This principle forbids involving a person in one's plans when the mode of her involvement will foreseeably harm her. Since suffering a deadly disease will foreseeably kill the one person in Guinea Pig, Causal Intention forbids intending that she suffer this disease.

Suppose that Silvia is the only agent in a position to save a drowning child, and she chooses to save five other people instead. Silvia's action makes it certain that the child will die. But her action does not *harm* the child. It is a failure to aid, not a harming. And her action is, in the relevant respect, analogous to Waldo's failure to aid Elsa: in both cases, an agent's action makes it certain that a person will die because the agent is the only one in a position to save that person, and the agent chooses to save others instead. Just as it would be mistaken to claim that Silvia's action harms the drowning child, therefore, it would be mistaken to claim that Waldo's action harms Elsa. To be sure, I believe that Waldo acts wrongly by running tests on Elsa instead of saving her. But this is not because he harms her by doing so.

There is a different response open to defenders of Causal Intention.<sup>16</sup> Some accounts of causation contemplate *causation by omission*: if an event  $E_1$  would have prevented another event,  $E_2$ , and  $E_1$  does not occur, then its failure to occur is (or can be) a cause of  $E_2$ .<sup>17</sup> Defenders of Causal Intention might embrace the idea of causation by omission, and use it to argue that Waldo's choice to use Elsa was a cause of her death, as follows. If Waldo had aided Elsa, she would not have died. So Waldo's failure to aid Elsa was a cause by omission of her death. And Waldo failed to aid Elsa only because he chose to use her. So Waldo's choice to use Elsa caused a cause of her death. His choice to use her was itself, therefore, a cause of her death.

There are two significant problems with this strategy. The first is that, even if the idea of causation by omission has valid application in other domains, the morality of harming and aiding seems to operate with a different, less expansive causal notion. That is why Silvia's failure to aid the drowning child does not count as causing it harm, in the relevant sense. But then Waldo's failure to aid Elsa should not count as causing *her* harm—unless “harm” is being used here in some nonstandard sense that presupposes, rather than explains, the objectionability of Waldo's action.

The second problem arises even if we waive this worry. Consider

*Heart Attack\**: Elsa is dying of a heart attack. Waldo can easily drive her to a nearby hospital, where doctors will save her life. Alternatively, he can drive himself to the cinema, in which case an ambulance will eventually arrive for Elsa. By the time the ambulance takes Elsa to the hospital, it will be too late for the doctors there to save her life. But the doctors, seeing that Elsa is beyond saving, will run a battery of harmless medical tests on her in order to devise a cure for a deadly disease threatening five other people (Elsa has a harmless medical condition that is related to this disease). The doctors will not run these tests on Elsa if Waldo drives her to the hospital, for they will spend their time treating her heart attack instead. Waldo drives himself to the cinema instead of driving Elsa to the hospital; he knows, but does not intend, that she will die of her heart attack, and that the doctors will use her to save the five.

Waldo's failure to aid Elsa in this variant of Heart Attack seems wrong, and it seems to be wrong for the same reason—or, at least, the most significant reason—that his failure to aid Elsa is wrong in the original case. That is why the failure to aid in Heart Attack\* does not seem much less wrongful, if less wrongful at all, than the failure to aid in Heart Attack. But Causal Intention cannot explain why Waldo acts wrongly in Heart Attack\*, for he does not intend that Elsa be used in any way. It might be suggested that Waldo *needs* to intend that Elsa be used, if he is to intend enough good to justify letting her die.<sup>18</sup> Maybe so. But it is not true that Waldo needs to intend that Elsa be used *in a way that causes her harm*, which is the sort of intention that Causal Intention forbids. By the time Elsa reaches the hospital, it will be futile for the doctors to aid her, since she will be beyond saving. So their withholding of aid from Elsa will not be a cause, *even by omission*, of her death. Nor will their choice to use her cause their withholding of aid: they will use her only after, and because, they see that aid would be futile. For two reasons, then, the doctors' using of Elsa will not be a cause of her death. And so it is false that, in order for Waldo to intend enough good to justify letting Elsa die, he must intend that she be used in a way that will cause her harm. Thus, Causal Intention does not forbid his action.

In one way, my criticism here is quite modest, for I will later argue that the most plausible motivation for Causal Intention is an appealing one. But I will suggest that this motivation finds better expression in Utility than in Causal Intention.

#### *D. Compulsion*

Turn next to *Compulsion*, according to which it is presumptively impermissible to force another person to serve an end that she may permissibly decline to serve. Victor Tadros defends this view. According to Tadros, “the claim that it is wrong to use someone as a means [in cases like Block and Guinea Pig] is grounded in an independent moral judgment about a person’s right to set her own ends.”<sup>19</sup> Tadros presents Compulsion as a version of Causal Intention: it is impermissible to act on an intention that another person be used or affected in a way that foreseeably harms her just because, in so acting, one compels her to serve an end that she is at liberty not to serve. Since Compulsion is a distinctive version of Causal Intention, however, I will press a criticism that targets it specifically.

18. This suggestion, which I owe to Tom Sinclair and an editor, is implicitly proposing a modest revision of Causal Intention:

*Causal Intention (R)*: It is presumptively impermissible to harm someone (or let her suffer harm) if you could not intend enough good to justify your action without intending that this person be used or affected in a harmful way.

The basic idea that people have a right against being forced to serve ends that they may permissibly decline to serve has a good deal of plausibility. But I do not think this idea can explain the impermissibility of *failing to aid* in cases like Guinea Pig and Heart Attack.<sup>20</sup> For it does not seem correct to say that failing to aid a person *forces* or *compels* her to pursue any end. Refraining from acting on a person’s body does not, in any ordinary sense of the term, compel her to do anything. It is true, of course, that one can use a person without acting on her body, but it is quite unnatural to say that using someone in this way is a form of force or compulsion. So Tadros’s view can condemn wrongful failures to aid only by straining our ordinary understanding of what it is to compel someone to serve an end.

It might be suggested that we should expand our ordinary understanding of compulsion in just this way. Perhaps I can compel you to serve my end, in some linguistically odd but still morally significant sense, even if I do not act on you: refraining from acting on you can count as a form of compulsion, in this unorthodox sense. The problem is that this suggestion undermines the initial plausibility of Tadros’s guiding idea, that people have a right against being compelled to serve ends they may permissibly decline to serve. For consider

*Drowning:* Your child is drowning, and so are five other children. Marzia’s body is about to fall onto a button that will save your child. She wants to save the five, however, and she demands that you redirect her body so that it will fall onto a different button that will save them instead.

It seems permissible to let Marzia fall in her current direction. You may refrain from acting on her body so that it will serve an end (saving your child, rather than the five) that she is at liberty not to serve. Saying that you compel Marzia to serve your end by refraining from acting on her is not just linguistically odd; it voids the initial appeal of the basic thought that people may not be compelled to serve ends that they may permissibly decline to serve. The intuitiveness of that idea presupposes our ordinary conception of compulsion: you may not *force* someone to serve an end she is at liberty not to serve. Merely refraining from acting on someone, as in Drowning, does not force her to do anything.

Thus, even if there is some unorthodox sense in which you can compel a person to serve an end by refraining from acting on her, this sense of compulsion does not appear to have much moral significance. So even if Compulsion can explain the impermissibility of harming, in cases like Block, it does not seem well equipped to explain the impermissibility of failing to aid, in cases like Guinea Pig and Heart Attack.

#### *E. Opportunism*

Let us examine a final principle that is commonly invoked to explain our intuitions about cases like Block and Guinea Pig: *Opportunism*.<sup>21</sup> According to this principle, goods that could not be secured in a person's absence do not justify harming her or letting her suffer harm. This principle explains the impermissibility of failing to aid the one person in Guinea Pig by reference to the fact that, without her presence, it would not be possible to secure the good of the five's continued survival. That is why it is wrong to let her suffer a deadly disease in order to save the five. To exploit her presence in this way, the defender of Opportunism maintains, is to treat her unduly like a resource for others' benefit.

Unlike the other principles I have considered, Opportunism does explain why Waldo acts impermissibly in Heart Attack. For it would not be possible to save the five in Elsa's absence, or so we can suppose. But there is a slight variant of the case that Opportunism is unable to account for:

*Heart Attack\*\**: Through no fault or choice of her own, Elsa is lying on top of a certain pill, which is the only known cure for a deadly disease threatening the lives of five other people. She cannot move herself or be moved. As it happens, Elsa also has a rare but harmless medical condition that is related to this deadly disease. Thus, scientific prodigy Waldo can devise a different cure for the disease, and thereby save the five, by running some harmless medical tests on Elsa. Just as Waldo is about to run his tests, however, Elsa starts to suffer a lethal heart attack; unless this heart attack is treated immediately, its effects will eventually kill her. Waldo can treat Elsa's heart attack, thereby stopping it and saving her life. But if he takes the time to do this, she will be useless for his purpose of saving the five, for he needs to start testing Elsa immediately if he is to devise a cure in time to save them. So Waldo decides to leave Elsa's heart attack untreated and run his tests on her instead. Some time later, after he has obtained the results he needs from testing her, she dies from the heart attack's effects.

In this case, the good of the five's continued survival does not depend on Elsa's existence or presence. If she did not exist, or were not present at the scene, Waldo would be able to save the five with the pill located beneath her. It nevertheless seems wrong to run tests on her instead of saving her from her heart attack. The case is admittedly complex, and bringing its details perspicuously to mind takes some work. But once I do so, I find that my intuition about the impermissibility of using Elsa instead of saving her life is firm. Our moral theory should, I believe, be capable of explaining this intuition, and Opportunism does not do this.

There is another reason that Opportunism cannot capture the main moral problem with failing to aid in Heart Attack. Consider

*Room:* Five people and one other person, Archibald, are suffering from a deadly virus. Waldo has enough medicine to save the five or to save Archibald, but not both. For Archibald has an especially malicious strain of the virus, and curing him would thus require Waldo's entire stock of medicine. Archibald's legs are in the doorway to the room that contains the five, keeping the door ajar; if Archibald's legs were not in the doorway, the five people would be inaccessible to Waldo, and Waldo would be unable to treat them. Waldo chooses to save the five instead of Archibald.

This failure to aid is opportunistic: instead of saving Archibald, Waldo exploits an opportunity that exists only because of Archibald's presence in order to save five other people instead. But Waldo's action here seems clearly permissible, unlike his action in Heart Attack. So Opportunism does not specify the feature that makes the latter action impermissible.<sup>22</sup>

#### *F. Person as Means and Intend as Means*

All of the principles we have considered so far have been explicitly defended by at least one philosopher. Let us conclude by considering two principles that may be more novel. According to *Person as Means*, if a person is a necessary causal means to some end, that end does not justify harming the person or letting her die. According to *Intend as Means*, if an agent must intend that a person serve as a means to some end, in order to intend the eventuation of that end, that end does not justify harming the person or letting her die. Both principles successfully condemn Waldo's actions in Heart Attack, Heart Attack\*, and Heart Attack\*\*. In all three cases, Elsa is a necessary causal means to the good of the five's continued survival: if this good is to obtain, her body must play a role in bringing it about. And in all three cases, Waldo must intend that Elsa play this causal role if he is to intend that the five be saved. So Person as Means and Intend as Means both imply that the good of the five's continued survival does not justify letting Elsa die. They similarly imply that, in Guinea Pig, the good of the five's continued survival does not justify letting the one die.

22. Some versions of Opportunism prohibit exploiting a person's presence for others' benefit, instead of aiding him, only if he has a claim to aid. So the defender of Opportunism might maintain that Waldo may save the five because Archibald has no claim to aid. But it is implausible, I think, to suppose that Archibald has no claim to aid (rather than a claim that is defeated by the claims of the five). In any case, we can modify the case so that Archibald *clearly* has a claim to aid: just suppose that Archibald and the other five all have contractual claims on Waldo's assistance, or that all six are Waldo's children.

These principles are nevertheless incorrect, because they also condemn the intuitively permissible action in Room. In that case, Archibald is a necessary means to the end of the five's continued survival—his body plays a necessary causal role in bringing about their survival, by keeping open the door to the room that contains them, thereby enabling Waldo to enter the room and medicate them. And in order for Waldo to intend to save the five, he must intend that Archibald play this causal role. Thus, Person as Means and Intend as Means both imply that procuring the end of the five's continued survival does not justify letting Archibald die.

None of the principles we have considered can adequately explain why failing to aid in the three Heart Attack cases is wrong, or explain why these failures to aid seem to be wrong on the same grounds as the failure to aid in Guinea Pig. Each principle either has nothing to say about one of the Heart Attack cases, or it is incorrect, because it condemns the permissible action in Room. So we need to find a principle that provides a unified explanation of the impermissibility of failing to aid in Guinea Pig and the Heart Attack cases, without implying that the failure to aid in Room is also impermissible. In the next section, I will argue that Utility is a principle of this kind.

###### II

Recall that, according to Utility, it is especially difficult to justify infringing a person's rights on the basis of her usefulness to others. Examining how the principle operates in Heart Attack will help to clarify its content.

Ordinarily, the fact that five people will die if you save one other person provides a sufficient justification for letting that one person die. Thus, it is permissible to withhold lifesaving medication from the patient in Scarcity, in order to medicate five other patients instead. So why is it wrong, in Heart Attack, for Waldo to let Elsa die in order to save five other people by running tests on her? Because, I suggest, of the role played by her usefulness to the five in grounding the justification for letting her die. The reason that the five other people will die if Waldo saves Elsa is that she will be useful to the five—she will serve as a means for their benefit, through being tested by Waldo—only if he lets her die. Because of this relationship, I propose, the fact that the five will die if Waldo saves Elsa has much less power to justify infringing her right to be saved than it would otherwise possess. More generally:

Suppose some fact F provides a *pro tanto* justification for infringing a person P's claim C. And suppose that F holds in virtue of the different fact that P will be useful to others (that is, she will serve as a means for their benefit) only if C is infringed. Then F provides a substantially weaker *pro tanto* justification for infringing C than it otherwise would.

This is a more technical statement of Utility, and it explicates the sense in which Utility deems it especially difficult to justify infringing a person's rights on the basis of her usefulness to others. Utility substantially reduces the justificatory weight of any facts that are grounded in the fact that a person will be useful to others only if her rights are infringed. In this way, it presumptively disables the fact that a person will be useful to others only if her rights are infringed from grounding a sufficient justification for infringing her rights.

This presumption is not absolute, however. Thus, Utility allows that instrumentalizing rights infringements can be permissible when necessary to prevent sufficiently great harms. Imagine, for example, a variant of Heart Attack in which Waldo can devise a cure for a *million* dying people only by running tests on Elsa instead of saving her. This seems permissible, and Utility is consistent with this judgment. The fact that Waldo can save a million people only if he infringes Elsa's right to aid provides an extraordinarily strong justification for doing so. Because this fact holds in virtue of the fact that Elsa will be useful to the million only if her right to aid is infringed, it generates a substantially weaker justification for infringing her right than it otherwise would. But even after the weight of this justification is discounted accordingly, it remains an extremely strong justification indeed—strong enough to constitute a sufficient justification for letting her die.

Now that I have said more about Utility's content and the mechanics of its operation, I will highlight some of its attractions.

First, it explains the impermissibility of failing to aid in Guinea Pig, Heart Attack, Heart Attack\*, and Heart Attack\*\*. In each case, if an agent saves one person, five other people will die. But this is because the one will not serve as a means for the five's benefit if the agent saves her. Because of this relationship, the fact that the five others will die if the agent saves the one loses much of its power to justify infringing the one's right to be saved. This fact does not, in consequence, provide a sufficient justification for letting the one die. Compare the situation in Scarcity. There it is also true that, if an agent saves one person, five other people will die. But this fact does not hold in virtue of any fact about the one's usefulness to the five—it holds simply because there is not enough medicine to save both the one and the five. So its justificatory power is undiminished, and it constitutes a sufficient justification for letting the one die. Thus, Utility explains why the sort of failure to aid in Heart Attack and Guinea Pig is more difficult to justify than the sort of failure to aid in Scarcity.

Recall that two of the principles we considered above, Person as Means and Intend as Means, can also explain the impermissibility of failing to aid in Guinea Pig and the Heart Attack cases. But these principles are incorrect, for they imply that the failure to aid in Room is impermissible as well. Utility does not generate this counterintuitive conclusion. What justifies Waldo's failure to aid Archibald is the fact that, if he aids Archibald, the five small people will die. And this is simply because Waldo does not have enough medicine to save both Archibald and the five—it is *not* because Archibald will serve as a means for the five's benefit only if Waldo lets him die. So the justificatory power of the fact that the other five will die, if Waldo aids Archibald, is undiminished. This fact accordingly constitutes a sufficient justification for letting Archibald die.

My analysis of Room might be disputed on the following grounds. Archibald's body is enabling Waldo to access the five, by keeping the room that contains them accessible. But this will benefit the five only if, rather than saving Archibald, Waldo enters the room and saves the five instead. So Archibald's body will benefit the five (that is, he will serve as a means for their benefit) only if Waldo lets him die. For this reason, it might be suggested, Utility *does* condemn Waldo's failure to aid Archibald. But this suggestion misconstrues the kind of significance that Utility attributes to the fact that someone will serve as a means for others' benefit only if his rights are infringed. Utility does not accord any justificatory weight, negative or positive, to this fact. Instead, Utility diminishes the justificatory weight of any facts that hold *in virtue of* this fact. In Heart Attack, five other people will die if Waldo saves Elsa *because* she will only serve as a means for their benefit (through being tested by Waldo) if he lets her die. If Waldo takes the time to save Elsa, there will not be enough time for her to serve as a means for the five's benefit, and this is *why* the five will die if he saves her. Thus, the fact that the five will die if Waldo saves Elsa loses much of its power to justify infringing her claim to aid. Compare the situation in Room. It is true that Archibald will only serve as a means for the five's benefit if Waldo lets him die. But this is not *why* the five will die if Waldo saves Archibald. The five will die if Waldo saves Archibald simply because Waldo lacks enough medicine to save both Archibald and the five.<sup>23</sup> So far as Utility is concerned, therefore, Room is just like Scarcity.

The distinction that Utility draws between Room and Heart Attack is an extremely subtle one.<sup>24</sup> But I believe it is an important distinction, for it underwrites the stark intuitive contrast between these cases. It allows us to make sense of other cases as well. Consider *Six Heart Attacks:* As in Heart Attack, Waldo is preparing to run a battery of harmless medical tests on Elsa in order to learn how to devise a cure for a deadly disease afflicting five other people. But now both Elsa and the five start to suffer lethal heart attacks. Waldo can save the five from their heart attacks or save Elsa from hers, but he cannot do both (for some reason, treating Elsa would take as much time as treating all of the five). Waldo chooses to treat the five's heart attacks instead of treating Elsa's. After he has saved the five from their heart attacks, he runs his battery of medical tests on Elsa. He finishes these tests, thereby gaining the knowledge necessary to save the five from their deadly disease, before Elsa dies of her heart attack's effects.

23. This fact, in turn, holds in virtue of various other facts, such as the fact that Archibald has an especially malicious strain of the virus. Indeed, the relevant explanatory relationship runs in the opposite direction: Waldo does not have enough medicine to save both Archibald and the five, which is why the five will die if Waldo saves Archibald, which is why Archibald will only benefit the five (that is, he will only serve as a means for their benefit) if Waldo lets him die. In Heart Attack, by contrast, it is because Elsa will only serve as a means for the five's benefit (through being tested by Waldo) if Waldo lets her die that the five will die if Waldo saves Elsa. Only the latter sort of explanatory relationship invites Utility's concern.

Utility allows us to make sense of this distinction.<sup>25</sup> In Six Heart Attacks, it is true that Elsa will only be useful to the five if Waldo lets her die (if Waldo saves her, the five will die of their heart attacks, and so Elsa will not benefit them through being tested by Waldo). But this is not *why* the five will die if Waldo saves Elsa. The five will die if Waldo saves Elsa simply because he does not have enough time to treat both her heart attack and their heart attacks. It is because there will not be enough time for Elsa to serve as a means for the five's benefit (through being tested by Waldo), if Waldo saves her, that the five will die if Waldo saves her. In sum, Utility successfully shoulders the explanatory burden we identified in the previous section: it provides a unified explanation of the impermissibility of failing to aid in cases like Heart Attack and Guinea Pig, without implying that failing to aid in cases like Room is also impermissible.

Second, Utility explains why it is especially difficult to justify the kind of harming in Block, Terror Bombing, and similar cases. In all of these cases, if an agent refrains from killing one person, several other people will die. But this is because the one person will not be useful to the others if the agent refrains from killing him. In Block, for example, the bystander will not serve as a means for the ten's benefit, by blocking the trolley headed in their direction, unless he is tripped into the trolley's path. This is why the ten will die if the agent refrains from tripping the bystander. Thus, the fact that the ten will die unless the bystander is tripped into the trolley's path loses much of its power to justify infringing the bystander's right against being killed in this way, and killing him is unjustifiable, all things considered. Compare the situation in Explosion. There it is also true that, if an agent refrains from killing a bystander (by detonating the bomb), ten other people will die. But this fact does not hold in virtue of any fact about the bystander's usefulness to the ten. So its justificatory power is undiminished, and it constitutes a sufficient justification for killing him. In this way, Utility explains why the sort of harming in Block is harder to justify than the sort of harming in Explosion.

Heart Attack, Guinea Pig, Block, and Terror Bombing all involve wrongful using: in each case, an agent instrumentalizes a person for others' benefit. Utility explains why all of these actions are wrong. But it also applies to certain cases in which an agent does not use her victim, such as

*Avalanche:* Wanda can get to the hospital in time to save her pinky from amputation only if she drives in a way that will trigger an avalanche. This avalanche will inflict lethal injuries on a bystander, Billy. However, it will also destroy a roadblock that has been preventing a team of medical scientists from accessing Billy and driving him to their research facility. It will thereby allow these scientists to run a battery of harmless medical tests on Billy, in order to devise a cure for a deadly disease currently afflicting ten other people (Billy has a rare but harmless medical condition that is related to this disease). Wanda chooses to drive to the hospital, thereby triggering the avalanche. She is fully aware that the avalanche will both kill Billy and enable the scientists to use him for the ten's benefit, but she does not intend for either of these things to happen.

It seems impermissible for Wanda to kill Billy, but this is not because she uses him. The concept of using appears to be an intentional one: in order for an agent to use anything, including a person, she must intend that it play some role in her plans.<sup>26</sup> Since Wanda does not intend anything for Billy, she cannot be said to use him. Yet her harmful action seems to be wrong for the same reason—or, at least, the most significant reason—that it is wrong to harmfully use persons in cases like Block.<sup>27</sup> That is why the killing in Avalanche does not seem much less wrongful, if less wrongful at all, than the killing in Block.

None of the other principles we have considered can adequately explain, I believe, why Wanda acts wrongly in Avalanche. Nor, for this reason, can they explain the intuition that the killings in Avalanche and Block are wrong on largely the same grounds. So we need some other principle to perform these explanatory tasks.

Harmful Intention cannot explain why Wanda acts wrongly in Avalanche, for Wanda does not intend the harm that Billy suffers, or anything plausibly regarded as close to this harm, or his being harmfully affected. And she does not need to intend any of these things in order to intend enough good to justify killing Billy. Since none of these things is causally necessary for saving the ten, Harm as Means does not condemn Wanda's action either. Nor does Causal Intention condemn it, since Wanda does not intend that Billy be used or affected in a harmful way. And she does not need to intend this, in order to intend enough good to justify killing him; she needs only to intend that he be used, harmlessly, by the scientists. Since Compulsion is a version of Causal Intention, it does not explain why Wanda acts wrongly either. Opportunism, Person as Means, and Intend as Means *can* explain why Wanda acts wrongly, but as I have already argued, these principles are incorrect.<sup>28</sup>

Unlike these other principles, Utility successfully explains why Wanda acts impermissibly in Avalanche. Utility explains the impermissibility of killing the bystander in Block in exactly parallel fashion. Thus, it explains why the killings in Block and Avalanche seem to be wrong on largely the same grounds.

Third, Utility has a good deal of inherent plausibility, that is, directly appreciable intuitive appeal. Not only does it support compelling case-based intuitions, but when we step back and consider it in relative abstraction from these intuitions, it simply seems (to me, at least) like a plausible principle. It seems right that it should be especially difficult to justify infringing a person's rights on the grounds that she will only serve as a means for others' benefit if her rights are infringed. This is an appealing interpretation of the basic idea, comfortably at home in ordinary moral thought, that people are not resources for others' benefit.

Utility also captures what is most attractive about another plausible principle we considered, Causal Intention. That principle (recall) forbids intentionally involving a person in one's plans if doing so will foreseeably cause her harm. In my view, the most attractive motivation for this principle was put forward by its first and most famous proponent, Warren Quinn. Quinn said that someone who harmfully involves another person in his plans must "treat them as if they were then and there *for* his purposes," in some morally objectionable sense.<sup>31</sup> Treating a person in this way offends against her standing "not to be pressed, in the apparent violation of [her] prior rights, into the service of other people's purposes."<sup>32</sup> I think that Utility better captures this underlying thought than Quinn's principle does, in a couple of respects.

For one thing, actions like the failure to aid in Heart Attack seem to offend against this same kind of standing, but Causal Intention does not condemn them. Declining to save Elsa in order to run tests on her does seem to treat her as "then and there" for others' purposes, in some morally objectionable sense.<sup>33</sup> The fact that Elsa can only be “pressed into the service” of other people if she is allowed to die should not, it seems, so readily render it permissible to let her die. It is an advantage of Utility that, unlike Causal Intention, it can capture our sense that such actions treat people as available for others’ purposes in a morally problematic way.

###### III

I have argued that Utility has several attractions. It explains a range of compelling intuitive judgments, about cases like Heart Attack, Avalanche, Guinea Pig, Block, and Terror Bombing; it is uniquely equipped to explain some of these judgments; it captures our sense that the actions in these cases are wrong on largely the same grounds; and it enjoys a good deal of inherent appeal. There are nevertheless many reasons, of course, that one might resist endorsing it. In what follows, I will limit myself to considering a type of objection that seems to me both especially important and relatively underexamined.<sup>36</sup> This objection concerns the principle's explanatory foundations, or its lack thereof. Utility explains a wide range of important moral propositions, but I doubt there is an explanation of why Utility itself is true. If it is true at all, I suspect it is true *underderivatively*: it is a primitive moral proposition, one that does not hold in virtue of any more basic proposition or set of propositions.<sup>37</sup> I would be delighted to be proven wrong about this. But given that I cannot think of any successful deeper explanation for Utility, and given that the deeper explanations I have seen offered for similar deontological principles all seem to me to fail, I am not terribly hopeful.<sup>38</sup> Nevertheless, I believe, Utility underwrites a sufficiently extensive range of sufficiently compelling case-based intuitions, and it has enough intuitive appeal in its own right, that we can justifiably think it is true.

Why might one demand a deeper explanation for a principle like Utility? All sorts of reasons. For example, one might deny that *any* normative proposition can be accepted as underivatively true. Many neo-Kantians hold this, because they think that all normative facts must be grounded in facts about the constitutive conditions of practical agency. I think that this metaethical view is probably incorrect, and that at least some normative facts are underivatively true. Alternatively, one might claim that principles like Utility require a deeper rationale because they appear paradoxical, inasmuch as they forbid minimizing their own violation.<sup>40</sup> My own view is that the appearance of paradox is illusory.<sup>41</sup> Finally, one might appeal to the way that Utility only applies to certain kinds of entities.<sup>42</sup> While Utility governs our treatment of human persons, it is far from clear that it governs our treatment of, say, insects. It is probably permissible to kill one insect as a means of saving ten others. This asymmetry might be taken to support the position that Utility has a deeper rationale, which explains why it applies to persons and not to insects. But the appealing basic thought to which Utility gives expression is that persons are not resources for others' benefit. The thought that insects (or nonpersons, more generally) are not resources for others' benefit is much less compelling. So it does not strike me as implausible to insist that persons and nonpersons simply are different, in this way, with no deeper explanation of why that is so. That said, the objection does call attention to an interesting and plausible proposal: that Utility governs the treatment of entities in proportion to their personhood, that is, their possession of whatever qualities endow beings with moral status. If this proposal is correct, there may in fact be a presumption, just an extremely weak one, against killing one insect as a means of saving others. But that does not seem like a troubling result.

None of these arguments against taking Utility as primitive, therefore, disturbs me too deeply. Nor do various other arguments that I have heard. But there is at least one argument that does seriously trouble me, and I will conclude by considering it.

There exist moral principles that seem to explain a range of our case-based intuitions but that have little appeal on more abstract reflection. Consider the principle that an agent's distance from a person directly bears on the strength of her duty to aid him. I believe that our intuitions about concrete cases do accord distance (roughly) this sort of moral significance.<sup>43</sup> But when we turn our attention from cases, and reflect more abstractly on duties to aid, the idea that distance bears this kind of moral weight has quite little appeal. And that makes it unconvincing to insist that distance *just does* modulate the strength of duties to aid: if distance does in fact possess this moral significance, it seems there must be some deeper explanation of why it does. In this respect, the distance principle differs from various other normative propositions, such as the principle that experiencing pleasure is *pro tanto* good for us. That is a principle I *would* be very comfortable taking as primitive, given how overwhelmingly plausible it is both in the abstract and in its implications for cases.

Some might say that Utility is more like the distance principle than the pleasure principle: it does not have enough inherent plausibility for us to regard it as underderivatively true, and so, if we find that it lacks a deeper explanation, we should not regard it as true at all. I have claimed, of course, that Utility does possess a good amount of inherent plausibility. But in closing I must acknowledge one central feature of the principle that diminishes its store of inherent plausibility, and substantially so. Utility is ultimately a principle about causal structure. It holds that certain facts about a person's causal position vis-à-vis others are presumptively disabled from providing a sufficient justification for infringing her rights. And if Utility is underderivatively true, there is no explanation of why causal structure has this moral significance; rather, it simply does. Now, philosophers sometimes claim that it is mysterious why causal structure should bear the sort of moral importance that our intuitions about Block, Guinea Pig, and similar cases appear to accord it.<sup>44</sup> The best way to interpret this claim, I think, is as follows: the idea that mere causal structure should possess such moral significance does not have enough appeal, once we step back from the case-based intuitions it explains, for us to accept that causal structure simply does have such significance. So we need to dispel the mysteriousness, as it were, by explaining why causal structure should be so important.

I feel the force of this claim. At least in a certain frame of mind, I do find it strange that a person's location in the causal architecture of the world should have such enormous importance in determining the permissibility of harming him or allowing him to suffer harm. Unlike (say) the fact that a person has acted culpably or meritoriously, or the fact that he could easily have avoided incurring a burden, a person's location in causal space sometimes strikes me as a terribly arbitrary feature of his situation—not much less arbitrary than his location in physical space, and almost as mysterious a candidate, therefore, for bearing great moral weight. So when I linger on the fact that Utility is a principle fundamentally about causal structure, it does lose a significant portion of its inherent appeal.

Its appeal diminishes a bit further when I consider that it ascribes moral significance to causal structure in a very subtle, fine-grained way. Utility presumptively disables the fact that a person will be useful to others only if his rights are infringed from grounding a sufficient justification for infringing his rights. But it does *not* preclude a person's position as useful to others from preventing the obtaining of a would-be defeater of an otherwise sufficient justification for infringing his rights. That is what happens in Tracks. Nor does Utility forbid harming or failing to aid a person, in order to save others, when he is serving as a necessary causal means to their survival, or when he is causally responsible for the opportunity to save them, or (indeed) when he will be useful to them only if his rights are infringed. All three are the case in Room and Six Heart Attacks. So the permissibility of harming or failing to aid a person, under Utility, does not just turn on his location in causal space. It turns on whether he is occupying a rather specific type of location in causal space.

That Utility ascribes such enormous moral significance to mere causal structure is sufficiently unsettling that the principle may, in the final analysis, possess only a modest amount of inherent plausibility. Perhaps it has even less than that. But I do not think that any similar deontological principle—that is, any principle that might successfully ground our intuitions about cases like Block and Guinea Pig—is on a firmer footing. For, though I cannot argue the matter here, I suspect that all of these principles must ultimately take causal structure, or something equally mysterious, as their central locus of concern.<sup>45</sup> And none of these principles has been provided with a successful deeper explanation, in my view. Furthermore, although the comparatively fine-grained character of Utility does generate an additional loss in inherent plausibility, I do not think this loss is too great. What is so unsettling about Utility and similar deontological principles, it seems to me, is not *how* they attribute moral significance to mere causal structure, but that they attribute such enormous moral significance to mere causal structure in the first place. Whatever additional loss in inherent plausibility Utility incurs by ascribing moral significance to causal structure in a comparatively fine-grained manner, this loss is more than compensated for by the principle's unique ability to explain compelling intuitions about relevant cases.<sup>46</sup> Or so I am inclined to think, though the matter certainly merits more consideration.

In sum, I do not think there is any similar deontological principle that fares better along the dual dimensions of inherent and case-based plausibility than Utility does. If we accept any of these principles, I think we should accept Utility. Obviously, though, we must consider the possibility that all of these principles should be rejected. My tentative view is that endorsing some such principle is much more plausible, all things considered, than jettisoning the firm case-based intuitions we need it to underwrite. This is so despite the fact that such principles may possess only a modest amount of inherent plausibility, or perhaps even less, in the final analysis. Indeed, I am tempted to say that we would be justified in endorsing some principle like Utility, as a primitive moral proposition if need be, even if all such principles were *devoid* of inherent plausibility. Rejecting all such principles would require us to abandon a wide range of judgments about cases. Many of these judgments, albeit not all, are extremely robust. They are significantly more compelling than (say) our case-based intuitions about distance and duties to aid.<sup>47</sup> Faced with so stark a divergence between fidelity to our particular moral convictions and solicitude for the inherent plausibility of our moral principles, my inclination would be to privilege the former.<sup>48</sup>

# A CRITIQUE OF SOME RECENT VICTIM-CENTERED THEORIES OF NONCONSEQUENTIALISM

(Accepted 17 February 2020)

**ABSTRACT.** Recently, Gerhard Øverland and Alec Walen have developed novel and interesting theories of nonconsequentialism. Unlike other nonconsequentialist theories such as the Doctrine of Double Effect (DDE), each of their theories denies that an agent's mental states are (fundamentally) relevant for determining how stringent their moral reasons are against harming others. Instead, Øverland and Walen seek to distinguish morally between instances of harming in terms of the circumstances of the people who will be harmed, rather than in features of the agent doing the harming. In this paper, we argue that these theories yield counterintuitive verdicts across a broad range of cases that other nonconsequentialist theories (including the DDE) handle with relative ease. We also argue that Walen's recent attempt to reformulate this type of theory so that it does not have such implications is unsuccessful.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Our moral reasons against harming innocent, non-threatening people<sup>1</sup> are very stringent. They are stringent in the sense that one cannot easily act against them by appealing to the cost to oneself of complying with them, or to the greater good one can bring about by doing so. Sometimes, however, it can be easier to justify acting against these reasons than at other times, and nonconsequentialists have offered different theories to explain why this is so. The Doctrine of Double Effect (DDE), for example, relies on a distinction between intending harm and merely foreseeing harm.<sup>2</sup> It maintains that, while there are stringent moral reasons against acting in ways that foreseeably harm innocents, these reasons are less stringent than reasons against intentionally harming innocents, all else being held equal. For instance, while it can be permissible to kill one innocent as a foreseeable effect of saving five, it would be impermissible intentionally to kill one innocent in order to save five.

---

<sup>1</sup> We'll refer to this class of people simply as 'innocents' in what follows.

The DDE is an *agent-centered* theory of nonconsequentialism in the sense that one of its core tenets is that the state of an agent's mind can be relevant for determining the stringency of her moral reasons against harming others. In recent years, several scholars have challenged such agent-centered theories of nonconsequentialism.<sup>3</sup> As alternatives, some have proposed theories of nonconsequentialism that do not consider the mental states of agents to be (fundamentally) relevant for determining the stringency of their moral reasons. Such theories are victim-centered in the sense that they seek to distinguish morally between instances of harming in terms of the circumstances of the *people who will be harmed*, rather than in terms of the features of the agent doing the harming. Our aim in this article is to evaluate two such theories: Gerhard Øverland's theory of moral obstacles (TMO)<sup>4</sup> and Alec Walen's Restricting Claims Principle (RCP).<sup>5</sup> Walen's first formulation of this RCP is extensionally equivalent to the TMO. However, in response to objections we raised,<sup>6</sup> and which we develop in greater detail in this article, Walen has recently given up on his initial formulation of the RCP in favor of a substantially different formulation, the RCP(R).<sup>7</sup> Since Øverland remained committed to the TMO, the first part of our article focuses on the TMO, explaining its motivations and showing why Øverland's attempts to defend it from these objections do not succeed. We will then discuss Walen's RCP(R) and show why it too cannot serve as a plausible basis for explaining the varying stringency of our reasons not to harm others.

Before proceeding, it is worth noting that the theories on which we focus in this article are not the only victim-centered theories of nonconsequentialism. Frances Kamm, for example, has developed a theory according to which the causal features of a situation determine how stringent the moral reasons against a harmful act should be.<sup>8</sup> For example, she argues that it can be easier to justify harmful acts if the harm is not causally upstream from the good end – that is, the causal sequence through which the harm results should not occur before the good end is brought about. For example, according to this view, our reasons against killing an innocent person and taking his organs in order to save five other innocent persons who are in need of the organs are especially stringent, because the causal sequence through which the harm results occurs before the good end is brought about. We shall not engage in detail with Kamm's victim-centered approach in this article.<sup>9</sup> We shall, however, point out in passing that her view is vulnerable to some, but not all, of the objections that we make against Øverland's TMO and Walen's RCP(R). This should suggest that we are not simply assuming an agent-centered approach to nonconsequentialism when criticizing the TMO and the RCP(R).

## II. THE TMO

The general idea of the TMO is that if an individual's presence makes it the case that some innocent person will have to be harmed, then that individual is a moral obstacle and his claim to protection from harm is weakened, relative to what it would otherwise be. Øverland offers the following definition of moral obstacles: Moral obstacles are people who come to be in circumstances—no matter how innocently and non-responsibly—that will result in either these people being harmed by the defending party's defensive actions, or the defending party being harmed by the initial threat if she abstains from using her defensive means in order not to harm them (means she otherwise could have used without harming anyone), or everyone being harmed in some measure when the defending party partly restrains her defensive actions.<sup>10</sup>

<sup>8</sup> Kamm, *Intricate Ethics*, Chapter 5.

Øverland develops the TMO by considering when an agent is permitted to defend herself against an aggressor:

**Flamethrower:** Tom impermissibly attacks Mary and 19 other people with lethal means. The only way Mary can save herself and the other 19 people is by killing Tom with a flamethrower. Behind Tom is a person, Andy. If Mary uses her flamethrower on Tom, she will also kill Andy.

**Rifle:** Tom impermissibly attacks Mary and 19 other people with lethal means. There is a house to the left of Tom. On the roof of this building is a person, Frank. Mary has a rifle with which she can shoot Frank. Frank would fall and make Tom's attack ineffective. Since Tom is hiding behind some trees, Mary cannot shoot Tom with the rifle.

In Flamethrower, Andy is a moral obstacle in Øverland's sense. Andy's presence makes it the case that some innocent people will be harmed if Mary defends herself and the 19 others. In particular, if Mary uses the flamethrower, Andy would be killed; and if Mary refrains from using the flamethrower, Tom would kill her and the 19 others. If Andy had been absent, Mary would have been able to use the flamethrower on Tom without harming any innocent party, and it would not be the case that any innocent people need be harmed.

In contrast, Frank is not a moral obstacle in Øverland's sense. Frank's presence does not make it the case that some innocent people will be harmed if Mary tries to defend herself and the 19 others – his presence merely creates a causal opportunity to prevent harm. For one thing, Mary cannot shoot Tom directly since Tom is hiding behind some trees. More importantly, if Frank were absent, there would be nothing Mary could do to stop Tom's attack, and thus innocent people would nonetheless be harmed.

According to Øverland, being a moral obstacle is relevant to determining how stringent the moral reasons against harming a person are, because moral obstacles *give rise to cost*. Indeed, for Øverland, it is because moral obstacles give rise to cost that moral reasons against harming them are less stringent.<sup>11</sup> In what sense do moral obstacles give rise to cost? In the sense that 'some people will be harmed as a consequence' of their presence.<sup>12</sup> Øverland argues that 'fairness dictates that those under threat should not be the only ones required to bear cost as a consequence of the presence of moral obstacles; the moral obstacles themselves should also bear some cost'.<sup>13</sup> Hence, since Andy's location in Flamethrower makes Mary's defensive action more difficult, his presence gives rise to cost. According to Øverland, fairness therefore requires that Andy bear at least some of the cost of Mary's defensive action. In contrast, Frank's presence in Rifle does not make Mary's defensive action more difficult. Because Frank's presence does not give rise to cost, fairness does not require that he bear some of the cost of Mary's defensive action. According to the TMO, it is therefore easier to justify killing Andy than Frank; and this seems to accord with people's intuitive judgments about these two cases.

<sup>10</sup> Øverland, 'Moral Obstacles', p. 483.

<sup>11</sup> *Ibid.*, p. 486. He notes that there are other ways that agents can give rise to cost besides being a moral obstacle: for example, by doing harm or enabling it to occur, or by being a physical obstructor.

The TMO can also be employed to vindicate judgments about trolley cases.

**Standard Trolley:** A runaway trolley is headed toward five innocent people who are on the track and who will be killed unless something is done. Abigail can push a button, which will redirect the trolley on to a second track, saving the five people. However, on this second track is an innocent person, Joey, who will be killed if the trolley is turned this way.

**Move:** A runaway trolley is headed toward five innocent people who are on the track and who will be killed unless something is done. Brandon can push a button, which will activate a movable platform that will move an innocent person, Kevin, in front of the trolley. The runaway trolley will be stopped by hitting Kevin, thereby saving the five but killing Kevin.

On the TMO, Joey in Standard Trolley, standing on the second track, is a moral obstacle, because his presence makes Abigail's action more difficult.<sup>14</sup> Had Joey not been there, Abigail would have been able to redirect the trolley without causing harm to the innocent. Joey's presence thus gives rise to cost, and according to the TMO, fairness requires that he bear at least some of this cost.

In contrast, Kevin is not a moral obstacle in Move, because his presence does not make Brandon's action more difficult. Had Kevin not been there, there would be nothing Brandon could do to save the five. So, according to the TMO, Kevin's claim to protection in Move is not reduced in the same way as Joey's claim in Standard Trolley, and the reasons against harming him are correspondingly more stringent.

<sup>13</sup> *Ibid.*, pp. 483–4.

<sup>14</sup> We assume that the justifications that the agents have in trolley cases are of a lesser-evil sort – the question that theories like the TMO are trying to answer is: what can make lesser-evil justifications for killing some to save a larger number compelling in some cases but not in others? For discussion of lesser evil-justifications more generally, see Helen Frowe, 'Lesser-Evil Justifications for Harming: Why We're Required to Turn the Trolley', *The Philosophical Quarterly* 68(272) (2018): pp. 460–480.

## III. THREE CHALLENGES TO THE TMO

As we have seen, the TMO appears to be able to vindicate our judgments about various cases, including the classic trolley cases. Moreover, it seems to have an intuitively powerful idea underpinning it: if a person's presence makes it the case that some innocent person will now be harmed, their claims to protection from harm are weakened, relative to what they would otherwise be. This theory thus appears to offer a way of determining the stringency of moral reasons against harming a person that is grounded in features of the situation of the prospective victim of harm, rather than in features of the agent doing the harming. Given that the DDE has been criticized on a number of grounds,<sup>15</sup> it might be thought that the TMO thus provides an attractive alternative to the DDE. However, we shall now discuss three significant challenges that the TMO faces.

### A. *The TMO is unable to draw morally significant distinctions*

Consider:

**Flamethrower 2:** Tom impermissibly attacks Mary and 19 other people with lethal means. The only way Mary can save herself and the other 19 people is by killing Tom with a flamethrower. In front of Tom is a person, Bob. If Mary uses her flamethrower on Tom, she will also kill Bob.<sup>16</sup>

Flamethrower 2 is similar to Flamethrower except that, while Andy is behind Tom in Flamethrower, Bob is in front of Tom in Flamethrower 2. Bob is a moral obstacle in this case too, because his presence makes Mary's defensive action more difficult. If Bob had not been there, Mary would be able to use the flamethrower on Tom without harming any other innocent person. Hence, Bob gives rise to cost, and the TMO holds that fairness requires that Bob bear some cost, just like Andy, and that Bob's claim to protection be correspondingly reduced.<sup>17</sup>

<sup>15</sup> See, e.g., Kamm, *Intricate Ethics*; Scanlon, *Moral Dimensions*.

<sup>16</sup> We are assuming that it is not the case that Mary can only kill Tom by aiming at Bob, but that both will be killed in any flame blast that is sufficient to kill Tom.

However, we think that there is a moral difference between Flamethrower and Flamethrower 2. To bring this difference into sharper relief, consider the following case, adapted from Philippa Foot<sup>18</sup>:

**Car Rescue:** Suppose that Jack must drive on a road to the hospital in order to save himself and 19 other people. There is some innocent, immovable person lying on the road. To get to the hospital, Jack would have to drive over the person, thereby killing him.

Someone who is sympathetic to the relevance of intentions for moral permissibility could argue that it would be impermissible for Jack to proceed, not because Jack intends to harm the innocent person on the road (he need not have such an intention), but because Jack intends the car to go over the spot where the person lies in order to save the 19 others.<sup>19</sup> Indeed, Warren Quinn has argued that proceeding is impermissible because Jack would be intending an action of the car (which is in his power to stop) that would cause the person's death: namely, its passing over the spot where he lies.<sup>20</sup>

Here it is worth making clear that, for Quinn, what explains the impermissibility of Car Rescue is, in part, the distinction between what is intended and merely foreseen, but not the DDE, as he understands it.<sup>21</sup> As Quinn understands the DDE, the DDE depends on 'whether or not a victim is himself an intentional object, someone whose manipulation or elimination will be useful'.<sup>22</sup> In Quinn's view, however, what is intended in Car Rescue is 'not something for [the victim]—that he be affected in a certain way—but some action of an object that (foreseeably but quite unintentionally) leads to his death'.<sup>23</sup>

<sup>17</sup> One might think that what explains the impermissibility of acting in Flamethrower 2 is that Bob is being used as a means, broadly understood, since he has a claim right to the space he occupies. However, this feature of Bob's situation does not distinguish him from Andy's in Flamethrower, since Andy too presumably has such a right to the space he occupies. For a discussion of the relevance to defensive rights of rights to occupy space, see Jonathan Quong, 'Killing in Self-Defense', *Ethics* 119 (2009): pp. 507–537.

<sup>18</sup> Philippa Foot, 'Killing and Letting Die', in *Moral Dilemmas: And Other Topics in Moral Philosophy* (New York: Oxford University Press, 2002), Chapter 5.

<sup>19</sup> S. Matthew Liao, 'The Closeness Problem and the Doctrine of Double Effect: A Way Forward', *Criminal Law and Philosophy* 10(4) (2016): pp. 849–863.

<sup>20</sup> Quinn, 'Actions, Intentions, and Consequences: The Doctrine of Double Effect', pp. 298–301.

<sup>21</sup> As Quinn writes, 'The surprise in this is that we must bring the distinction between what is intended and merely foreseen into the DDA' (ibid., p. 300). In this sense, the DDA contributes another agent-centered feature to Quinn's view. We are grateful to a referee for helping us see this.

<sup>22</sup> Ibid.

It is noteworthy that even some other victim-centered theories of nonconsequentialism would also regard the reasons against killing in Car Rescue to be especially stringent. For instance, on Kamm's causal structure theory there are especially morally stringent reasons not to perform this action because the harm of driving over the spot where the innocent person lies is a direct side-effect of the means, which is causally upstream from the good end of saving Jack and the other 19 people.<sup>24</sup>

Similar verdicts can be reached in Flamethrower 2. It is impermissible for Mary to proceed, not because Mary intends to harm Bob (she need not have such an intention), but because Mary intends that the flame goes through the spot where Bob is standing. Likewise, for Quinn, in proceeding in Flamethrower 2, Mary would be intending an action of the flamethrower (which is in her power to stop) that would cause Bob's death: namely, its passing over the spot where he stands. Again, the impermissibility of the action here is not due to the fact that Bob is there (hence the DDE on Quinn's understanding does not apply), but because the flame must pass that spot if Mary and the 19 others are to be saved. On Kamm's view, the harm of the flame's going over the spot where Bob is standing is a direct side-effect of the means, which is causally upstream from the good of saving Mary and the 19 people. Given this, in Flamethrower 2, Mary's killing of Bob would also be impermissible on Kamm's causal structure theory.

In contrast, in Flamethrower, one need not intend the action of the flamethrower that would cause Andy's death – that is, its passing over the spot where he stands. So someone who believes in the relevance of intentions for moral permissibility or who follows Quinn can explain why the reasons against harming Andy are less stringent. Likewise, the harm to Andy is causally downstream from the good of saving Mary and the other 19 people. So Kamm's causal structure theory can also explain why in Flamethrower Mary's killing Andy is permissible. If so, several other theories of nonconsequentialism (including some victim-centered ones) would reach different and more intuitively plausible judgments with respect to these cases, in comparison with the TMO.<sup>25</sup>

<sup>23</sup> Ibid.

<sup>24</sup> Kamm writes regarding Car Rescue: 'going over the spot, which is a spot he already occupies, is something we must do, and so we must involve him given that he is there, which directly causes him harm' (Kamm, *Intricate Ethics*, p. 147).

### *B. The costs of denying the relevance of intentions*

A second challenge to the TMO is that there are considerable costs to accepting a theory that denies the relevance of intentions to the permissibility of harming innocents. We recognize that advocates of victim-centered theories are less likely to be moved by this concern, but we do think it worthwhile to highlight the costs of accepting it, especially for those who have not made up their minds one way or another. For instance, consider:

**Enemy Trolley:** A runaway trolley is headed toward five innocent people who are on the track and who will be killed unless something is done. An enemy, who is passing by, can push a button, which will redirect the trolley on to a second track, saving the five people. However, on this second track is an innocent person, Luke, who will be killed if the trolley is turned this way. The enemy does not care about saving the five. He hates Luke, he does not try to hide the fact that he hates Luke, and he sees this as his opportunity to kill Luke. In addition, however, the enemy is not committed to trying to kill Luke by other means if this means of redirecting the trolley becomes unavailable.

Structurally speaking, Enemy Trolley is just like Standard Trolley. In Standard Trolley, it is generally accepted that there is no requirement to push the button to redirect the trolley.<sup>26</sup> That is, it is generally accepted that, while it is permissible for Abigail to kill Joey, she is not required to kill him to save the five. In other words, Abigail is also permitted to allow the five to die if she chooses. An explanation is that if Abigail does not push the button, then she merely lets the five die. But if she pushes the button, she kills Joey. Within certain limits, so the thought goes, agents should not be required to do harm to others even to prevent greater harm, especially if they have deep personal convictions against doing so. In this regard, Standard Trolley is what might be called a ‘may act’ case.<sup>27</sup> In contrast, there are other cases in which it is not merely permissible to kill some to save the greater number, but it is required to do so. Consider the following:

<sup>25</sup> It might be thought that in Flamethrower 2, Bob is a *physical obstructor* rather than a moral obstacle. But this is incorrect. It is not true that Bob 'must be removed for the person under threat to make effective use of her means of survival' (Øverland, 'Moral Obstacles', p. 495). Nor can Bob be described as a 'physical hindrance' to her defensive action (ibid., p. 486). Mary can perfectly well use her flamethrower effectively while Bob is there; Bob will simply die as a result of her having done so. In any case, Øverland expresses skepticism that there are morally significant differences between obstructors and moral obstacles (ibid., p. 496). For more on obstructors, see Helen Frowe, 'Threats, Bystanders and Obstructors', *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 108(3) (2008): pp. 365–372.

<sup>26</sup> Judith Jarvis Thomson, 'The Trolley Problem', *The Yale Law Journal* 94 (1985): pp. 1395–1415; Kamm, *Intricate Ethics*, p. 14.

**Enemy 10,000 to 1:** A runaway trolley is headed toward 10,000 innocent people who are on the track and who will be killed unless something is done. An enemy, who is passing by, can push a button, which will redirect the trolley on to a second track, saving the 10,000 people. However, on this second track is an innocent bystander, Sam, who will be killed if the trolley is turned this way. The enemy does not care about saving the 10,000 people. She hates Sam, she does not try to hide the fact that she hates Sam, and she sees this as her opportunity to kill Sam. In addition, however, the enemy is not committed to trying to kill Sam by other means if this means of redirecting the trolley becomes unavailable.<sup>28</sup>

In Enemy 10,000 to 1, it seems that the enemy would be morally required to push the button. In contrast to Standard Trolley, this is what can be called a ‘must act’ case. Indeed, in this case killing the one seems morally required even if the enemy hates Sam and aims to murder him. The reason is that the good of saving 10,000 people is sufficient to outweigh the especially stringent reason against maliciously killing Sam. As this is a ‘must act’ case, arguably the enemy is required to redirect the trolley toward Sam. In other words, the enemy’s bad intention cannot turn a morally required act into an impermissible act.

Since Enemy Trolley is structurally the same as Standard Trolley (the number of people involved in these cases is identical), it seems plausible that Enemy Trolley too is a ‘may act’ case in which the agent’s moral reasons are such that he is permitted but not morally required to push the button. That is, just as it would be permissible in Standard Trolley not to save the five by redirecting the trolley, it would also be permissible for the enemy in Enemy Trolley not to save the five by redirecting the trolley. Suppose that this is so. It seems that it would be impermissible for the enemy to push the button in Enemy Trolley in order to kill Luke. If the enemy were to push the button, it seems that he would have murdered Luke. In Enemy Trolley, it seems perfectly cogent to require that the enemy *not* push the button if he is going to push the button in order to kill Luke. Note that agent-centered approaches like the DDE can handle Enemy Trolley with ease since they hold that intentions can sometimes make an action impermissible that would otherwise be permissible. It is impermissible for the enemy to kill Luke because killing him is the intended aim. The TMO cannot reach such a verdict.<sup>29</sup> Luke is a moral obstacle and his presence gives rise to cost. If he had not been there, some innocent people would not have had to be harmed as a result. Given this, on the TMO, Luke's claim to protection would be reduced and the enemy's reasons against pushing the button would be correspondingly less stringent. While such a case does not perhaps constitute a knock-down argument to the TMO, for those who find this implication implausible, this further counts against the TMO as a plausible theory of nonconsequentialism.

<sup>27</sup> S. Matthew Liao, ‘Intentions and Moral Permissibility: The Case of Acting Permissibly with Bad Intentions’, *Law and Philosophy* 31(6) (2012): pp. 703–724.

<sup>28</sup> See also S. Matthew Liao, ‘The Closeness Problem and the Doctrine of Double Effect: A Way Forward’, *Criminal Law and Philosophy* 10(4) (2016): pp. 849–863.

### *C. The problem of specifying the relevant counterfactual*

The third challenge to the TMO relates to the manner in which the TMO must employ counterfactuals to reach certain judgments. Consider again how the TMO treats Flamethrower and Rifle. As we have seen, in Flamethrower, Andy is a moral obstacle. If Andy had not been there, then Mary would have been able to take defensive action without harming any innocent person. Also, on the TMO, in Rifle, Frank does not give rise to cost because if Frank had not been there, Mary would not have been able to do anything to save herself and the other 19 people. The TMO therefore specifies the counterfactuals by considering what courses of action would be available to Mary if a particular person, Andy in Flamethrower and Frank in Rifle, *had not been there*.<sup>30</sup>

However, suppose that the counterfactuals are instead the following: **Flamethrower 3:** Tom impermissibly attacks Mary and 19 other people with lethal means. The only way Mary can save herself and the other 19 people is by killing Tom with a flamethrower. Behind Tom is a person, Andy. If Mary uses her flamethrower she will also kill Andy. Suppose further that Andy happens to be standing in front of a door. If Andy had not been there, 50 other innocent people would have come through the door. The 50 people would be killed if Mary used her flamethrower on Tom.

---

<sup>29</sup> It is worth mentioning that other victim-centered theories, including Kamm's causal structure theory, also cannot reach this verdict in Enemy Trolley.

<sup>30</sup> This is evident when we consider Øverland's more detailed presentation of the TMO: 'When an innocent person A is under threat of harm and has a defensive action available to her (independent of the presence of other people), another innocent person B, who poses no threat or physical hindrance, is a moral obstacle if his presence has the consequence that either (i) B will be harmed (if A performs her available defensive action) or (ii) A will be harmed (if A completely restrains her defensive action), or (iii) both A and B will be harmed in some measure (if A partially restrains her defensive action)' (Øverland, 'Moral Obstacles', p. 486).

**Rifle 2:** Tom impermissibly attacks Mary and 19 other people with lethal means. There is a house to the left of Tom. On the roof of this building is a person, Frank. Mary has a rifle, with which she can shoot Frank. Frank would fall and make Tom's attack ineffective. Since Tom is hiding behind some trees, Mary cannot shoot him with the rifle. Suppose further that some builders have been putting up logs on the roof. If Frank had not been there, the builders would have placed a large log where Frank stood. If so, Mary would have been able to shoot the log, and the log would have fallen on Tom and make Tom's attack ineffective.

In Flamethrower 3, if Andy had not been there, 50 other innocent people would have been. If Mary used the flamethrower, she would have killed 50 other people in order to save herself and 19 others. This seems impermissible given that proportionality is not satisfied. If so, in Flamethrower 3, Andy's presence does not give rise to cost. Indeed, if Andy had not been there, Mary would not have been able to take any defensive action. If Andy had not been there, taking defensive action would mean harming 50 other innocent people, and this would be impermissible. Accordingly, the TMO should deliver the verdict that Andy's claim to protection is not reduced, so that killing him would be impermissible, just as it would be impermissible in Rifle for Mary to kill Frank. The problem is that Mary's conduct in Flamethrower 3 does seem permissible, just as it is in Flamethrower. If so, the TMO seems to reach the wrong verdict regarding Flamethrower 3. This calls into question the key tenet of the TMO: when an agent gives rise to cost, their claim to protection is reduced relative to the claims of those who do not give rise to cost, all else being equal.

In Rifle 2, Frank's presence gives rise to cost. If Frank had not been there, Mary would have been able to take certain defensive action against Tom: that is, shoot the log to make Tom's attack ineffective. Given this counterfactual, on the TMO, fairness dictates that since Frank gives rise to cost, his claim to protection is correspondingly reduced. According to the TMO, Mary's reasons against shooting Frank are consequently less stringent. However, Rifle 2 is just like Rifle – that is, in *both* cases Mary ought not to shoot Frank. If so, the TMO also seems to give the wrong verdict regarding Rifle 2.

Indeed, the TMO also implies counterintuitive verdicts about the trolley cases similarly modified. Consider: **Standard Trolley 2:** A runaway trolley is headed toward five innocent people who are on the track and who will be killed unless something is done. Abigail can push a button, which will redirect the trolley on to a second track, saving the five people. However, on this second track is an innocent person, Joey, who will be killed if the trolley is turned this way. Suppose further that Joey is standing on a button that maintains Abigail's redirection of the trolley on the second track. If Joey had not been there, Abigail's redirecting the trolley would have resulted in the trolley's being further redirected on to a third track on which there are 10 other innocent people who would be killed.

**Move 2:** A runaway trolley is headed toward five innocent people who are on the track and who will be killed unless something is done. Brandon can push a button, which will activate a movable platform that will move an innocent person, Kevin, in front of the trolley. The runaway trolley will be stopped by hitting Kevin, thereby saving the five but killing Kevin. Suppose further that workers had been loading heavy crates on to a number of movable platforms. If Kevin had not been there, they would have loaded a heavy crate on to the movable platform where he is standing. Brandon's pushing the button would have shifted a movable platform with a heavy crate in front of the trolley, stopping it from killing the five.

In Standard Trolley 2, Joey is not a moral obstacle because his presence does not give rise to cost. If he had not been there, Abigail's redirecting the trolley would have resulted in killing 10 other innocent people on the third track. However, intuitively, it seems that Standard Trolley 2 is like Standard Trolley. That is, Abigail acts permissibly in both cases.<sup>31</sup> If so, the TMO also reaches the wrong verdict regarding Standard Trolley 2.

In Move 2, Kevin's presence gives rise to cost. If he had not been there, then there would have been a heavy crate on the movable platform, and Brandon would have been able to move the heavy crate in front of the trolley to stop it and save the five. Yet, Move 2 is, morally speaking, just like Move. In both cases it is impermissible for Brandon to kill Kevin. Given this, the TMO appears to arrive at the wrong conclusion on Move 2. We conclude that the TMO delivers plausible moral verdicts only when the counterfactuals are specified in a particular way.

Øverland is aware of this concern,<sup>32</sup> and tries to address it by stipulating that the counterfactual should be specified in the following manner: To better capture the morally relevant counterfactual we should therefore say: all else being equal, a moral obstacle gives rise to cost by being at a particular place relative to a situation in which no one is in that place (and gives rise to cost by their presence). All else being equal, if no one were behind Tom, Mary could defend herself without harming or killing an innocent non-threatening person. Whenever *some* person is in the way of Mary's defensive means that person's presence gives rise to cost.<sup>33</sup>

<sup>31</sup> It might be thought that on Quinn's interpretation of the DDE, Abigail harms Joey opportunistically (that is, intentionally) in Standard Trolley 2, because she exploits his presence to do something she would not be able to do if he were not there, thereby foreseeably causing him harm. However, even if this is correct, it is worth pointing out that Abigail is not exploiting Joey's presence in order to save the five. Given this, a case can be made, though Quinnians might disagree, that Abigail is harming Joey as a side effect when she redirects the trolley, which makes Abigail's action permissible. Similar things can be said about Standard Trolley 3. See also Walen, 'The Restricting Claims Principle Revisited', p. 223. We thank an anonymous reviewer for this point.

<sup>32</sup> Øverland, 'Moral Obstacles', p. 489.

Øverland thus attempts to avoid the unwelcome implications that we have pointed out by stipulating that the relevant counterfactuals for determining when an agent has given rise to cost are ones in which no one or nothing else would be present if the relevant person had not been there. For instance, in Move 2, if Kevin had not been there, Øverland requires that *nothing else* would be on the movable platform.

One problem with this response is that it would narrow the applicability of the TMO merely to cases in which no one or nothing else would be present if the relevant person had not been there. The TMO would no longer apply to Move 2 since a heavy crate would have been there if Kevin had not been there. However, even if we accept Øverland's restrictive stipulation, the TMO would deliver counterintuitive verdicts. Consider:

**Flamethrower 4:** Tom impermissibly attacks Mary and 19 other people with lethal means. The only way Mary can save herself and the other 19 people is by killing Tom with a flamethrower. Behind Tom is a person, Andy. If Mary uses her flamethrower she will also kill Andy. Suppose further that Andy is leaning against a button that remotely activates Mary's flamethrower. If Andy had not been there, Mary would not have been able to use her flamethrower on Tom.

In this case, if Andy had not been there, no one or nothing else would be present and Mary would not have been able to use her flamethrower on Tom. On the TMO, Andy would therefore not be a moral obstacle and would not give rise to cost. It seems that the TMO would therefore treat Flamethrower 4 as a case of impermissible killing, just like Rifle. Yet, Flamethrower 4 seems to be a case of permissibility, just like Flamethrower.

Or, consider:

**Standard Trolley 3:** A runaway trolley is headed toward five innocent people who are on the track and who will be killed unless something is done. Abigail can push a button, which will redirect the trolley on to a second track, saving the five people. However, on this second track is an innocent person, Joey, who will be killed if the trolley is turned this way. Suppose further that Joey is standing on a button that remotely activates Abigail's button. If Joey had not been there, Abigail would not have been able to use the button to redirect the trolley.

In this case, if Joey had not been there, no one or nothing else would be present and Abigail would not have been able to use the button to redirect the trolley. On the TMO, Joey would not give rise to cost. It seems that the TMO would therefore treat Standard Trolley 3 as case of impermissible harming akin to Move. Yet, Standard Trolley 3 seems to be a case of permissible harming akin to Standard Trolley. Flamethrower 4 and Standard Trolley 3 suggest that the TMO leads to counterintuitive results even if the counterfactuals invoked to assess whether some agent has given rise to cost or possesses restricting claims are ones in which no one or nothing else is present.

<sup>33</sup> Ibid., pp. 489–90.

Finally, as indicated, it seems that there is no moral difference between, for example, Standard Trolley, Standard Trolley 2 and Standard Trolley 3. All are cases in which Abigail acts permissibly when she kills. Note that other nonconsequentialist theories, including the DDE, are able to reach such verdicts, even though the counterfactuals are different in these cases. For instance, under the DDE, it would be permissible for Abigail to redirect the trolley in Standard Trolley, Standard Trolley 2 and Standard Trolley 3, given that she does not intend to kill Joey on the second track. Since other theories can reach the correct verdicts *robustly*, irrespective of how the counterfactuals are specified, the fact that the TMO does not arrive at the same judgment in these cases and has to stipulate the counterfactuals in a certain way in order to explain Standard Trolley makes it seem *ad hoc*. Without such stipulation, the verdicts the TMO reaches are counterintuitive. And, even with such stipulation, there are other cases such as Flamethrower 4 and Standard Trolley 3 that the TMO cannot handle.

## IV. UNRELIABLE INTUITIONS?

As noted earlier, Alec Walen once defended an extensionally equivalent theory to the TMO, the Restricting Claims Principle (RCP), but now favors a different formulation, which we will examine in the next section. In this section, we address some of Walen's attempts to respond to the challenges that we have posed to the TMO above.

As we shall see, Walen's general strategy is to employ a burden-of-proof argument. That is, he questions why intuitive judgments about certain cases, in particular cases that support agent-centered views, should be given more weight than, for example, cases that support victim-centered views. It is beyond the scope of this article to enter into a debate about philosophical methodology and the evidentiary status of moral intuitions.<sup>34</sup> Here we wish to acknowledge that such concerns can have more force when raised by theorists who do not appeal to our intuitive judgments about cases in order to develop their theories. However, dialectically speaking, given that both Walen and Øverland appeal quite extensively to such judgments in developing their views, this complaint has much less force. Let us now consider Walen's specific responses to our objections.

Recall that one argument we have made against the TMO is that it cannot readily explain why the killing in cases such as Flame-thrower 2 and Car Rescue seems impermissible. Walen seeks to diffuse what appear to be counterintuitive implications of the RCP (and therefore the TMO) by suggesting that our judgments about cases such as Car Rescue are not reliable. In particular, appealing to Joshua Greene's notion of personal force, Walen says, 'The explanation that seems most likely to me now is that people are uncomfortable exercising direct physical force on another'.<sup>35</sup> As Walen elaborates, people are uncomfortable with running someone over or choosing that someone be run over in order to produce a greater good.<sup>36</sup>

This response is not convincing. Greene invoked the notion of personal force to explain how people's judgment about the Footbridge case might be a kind of confabulation, when compared with people's judgment about the Switch case. Very briefly, in Switch, which is a variation of Standard Trolley, one can hit a switch that will turn a runaway trolley away from five people to one. In Footbridge, which is a variation of Move, one can push a person off a footbridge and into the path of a runaway trolley, saving five people further down the track. Greene proposes that people are more likely to judge the killing in Footbridge to be impermissible because they are squeamish about having to exert direct personal force and push someone over a Footbridge. Setting aside the dispute over whether Greene's notion of personal force can explain away our judgment about Footbridge,<sup>37</sup> notice that the level of personal force involved in Car Rescue is about the same as the level of personal force involved in Switch. In both cases, one is choosing that someone be run over in order to produce a greater good, but in neither case is one exerting direct personal force in Greene's sense. To see this point more clearly, consider the following:

**Car Rescue 2:** Suppose that Jack must hit a switch which would send a self-driving car to the hospital in order to save himself and 19 other people. There is some innocent, immovable person lying on the road. To get to the hospital, the self-driving car would have to drive over the person, thereby killing him.

In Car Rescue 2, the action involved is exactly the same as Switch; in both cases, one has to hit a switch in order to save some number of people. Assuming that Switch does not involve personal force, it should be the case that Car Rescue 2 also does not involve personal force. As with Car Rescue, Car Rescue 2 remains a case of impermissible killing. If so, Walen would not be able to explain our judgment about the impermissibility of Car Rescue 2 away by appealing to personal force.

Walen might still insist that our intuitive judgments about Car Rescue, Car Rescue 2, Flamethrower 2, and so on, are unreliable and we should just reject them. Indeed, Walen has said that 'the best position in terms of reflective equilibrium is to reject the intuition as unsound and stick to the RCP'.<sup>38</sup> But Walen has not explained why we should accept this. Importantly, these judgments are supported by other nonconsequentialist theories, of both agent-centered and victim-centered types. As noted above, both Quinn's theory and Kamm's theory also support the judgment that these cases are cases of impermissible killing. Given this, it seems more plausible that 'the best position in terms of reflective equilibrium' would be to regard our intuitions about these cases as calling into question the RCP (and the TMO) on this score.

Recall next that the TMO has difficulty explaining why it seems impermissible to kill in cases such as Enemy Trolley. Walen agrees that the enemy acts for a bad reason in such a case. As he says of Enemy Trolley, I do not want to insist that this is merely a manifestation of bad character or a basis for moral criticism. I think we can require people both not to act on such malicious reasons, and to perform the act that they realize is justifiable for morally acceptable reasons.<sup>39</sup>

Still, Walen argues that cases like Enemy Trolley need not undermine his theory:

we should be careful about just what we are accepting if we say that a patient's claim not to be killed for a malicious reason grounds the impermissibility of an agent's acting on that reason. We are not saying that the patient has a claim not to be killed in that situation; we are saying that he has a claim not to be killed for a bad reason. This suggests that his claim refers to the offensive quality of the agent acting on that bad reason. That, however, seems quite different from the kind of wrong at stake when the very act itself cannot be justified.<sup>40</sup>

This remark is puzzling because it is not clear how it vindicates his theory. For one thing, no one is claiming that 'the very act itself cannot be justified', if 'the very act itself' refers to the causal structure of Enemy Trolley and the range of possible actions pertaining to it. We are not disputing that Standard Trolley, which has the same causal structure and the same range of possible actions as Enemy Trolley, is a case of permissible killing. In addition, it does not seem that Walen can accept the judgment that the bystander, Luke, 'has a claim not to be killed *for a bad reason*' in Enemy Trolley without giving up on the distinguishing features of victim-based theories of nonconsequentialism. Indeed, accepting this judgment would seem to amount to accepting that the state of an agent's mind can be relevant for determining whether or not the harms they cause are permissible.

Walen attempts to defend his theory in another way:

Compare the malicious trolley turner who seeks to exploit a moral opportunity to perform a justifiable act for a bad reason with someone who would topple the massive man onto the tracks ... this wrong [of toppling the massive man] seems worse—it is not just killing for a bad reason, it is unjustifiable killing—and yet it is hard to see why it should be more offensive to use someone as a means than to aim to kill him out of malice ... This mismatch indicates that we should look for some other account—one that does not appeal to the intentions of the agent.<sup>41</sup>

Walen seems to think that someone who believes that intentions are relevant for moral permissibility would hold that it is 'more offensive to use someone as a means than to aim to kill him out of malice'. But why should those who endorse the relevance of intentions for moral permissibility affirm this? The enemy in Enemy Trolley intends to commit murder, while Brandon in Move is at least also intending to save five other people. It seems that someone who believes in the relevance of intentions for moral permissibility would and should say that the enemy performs an act that is worse than Brandon's act, even if both are impermissible.

## V. A REVISED BASELINE?

Previously, we argued that Øverland's TMO specifies the counterfactuals in an arbitrary fashion when it comes to characterizing whether an agent's presence gives rise to cost. Unlike Øverland, Walen accepts that this is a reason to reject the TMO and the RCP as it was originally formulated. Instead of searching for an appropriate 'counterfactual baseline', Walen now proposes that a plausible version of the RCP should employ instead what he calls a 'toolkit baseline'.<sup>42</sup> Walen explains his toolkit baseline in terms of 'property notions, broadly construed'.<sup>43</sup>

[The toolkit baseline] concerns the things in the world that an agent can presumptively use to pursue her ends: her body, her property, and things that, while not her property she is presumptively free to use (things others own but that they have given her permission to use, things in the public domain, etc.).<sup>44</sup>

In addition, Walen claims that an agent's toolkit should also include things that, given the needs of some, she has a situational right to use. Walen calls these empowering claims.<sup>45</sup> According to Walen, on this reformulated RCP (henceforth the RCP(R)), if a victim/patient's claim pushes to restrict what an agent can do with her toolkit, it is restricting. If a patient's claim pushes to limit what she can count as part of her toolkit, it is non-restricting.

Note that, unlike the original counterfactual baseline which tries to ground a moral difference in terms of non-moral features of the victim's situation, the toolkit baseline is avowedly moralized, drawing on prior moral notions such as an agent's rights to use various things for various purposes. Therefore, we need to consider whether the RCP(R) both illuminates our judgments about the cases we have been considering and remains meaningfully distinct from what Walen refers to as the means principle (MP), according to which 'it is impermissible to cause significant harm to others, or allow significant harm to befall others, when the harm would result from using them merely as a means'.<sup>46</sup>

<sup>42</sup> *Ibid.*, pp. 222–8.

<sup>43</sup> *Ibid.*, p. 228.

<sup>44</sup> *Ibid.*

<sup>45</sup> *Ibid.*, p. 229.

How does the RCP(R) account for our judgments about classic trolley cases such as Switch and Footbridge, discussed earlier? According to Walen, in the case of Switch, the trolley company initially has a non-restricting claim to keep its switches from people other than its employees. However, the claims of the five who are about to be killed provide the agent who can throw the switch an empowering claim to throw the switch. Once the agent has been empowered to throw the switch, so argues Walen,

the claims of the five register as restricting. They push the agent in the direction of being required to save them. In that context, they have to be weighed against the restricting claim of the sidetrack man and the agent's claim to be free from having to serve them.<sup>47</sup>

This explains how the RCP(R) reaches the judgment that it is permissible to throw the switch in this case.

With respect to Footbridge, Walen argues that, while the claims of the five empower the agent to use the trolley switch, they do not empower the agent to use the body of the massive man.<sup>48</sup> According to Walen, the massive man's

interest is not merely in controlling the use of his property (his body), but in protecting his life. In that case, the five's empowering claims fail to empower the agent to help them. As a result, she has no baseline freedom with which their restricting claims can engage, and thus their restricting claims never meaningfully come into play.<sup>49</sup>

There are two challenges that can be raised against the RCP(R). First, it is not obvious that it can adequately distinguish between restricting and non-restricting claims. For instance, with respect to Switch, Walen asserts that the sidetrack man's claim is restricting. But why is it restricting? Why does the sidetrack man not have an interest 'in controlling the use of his property (his body)' *and* 'in protecting his life'? Indeed, the sidetrack man does have this interest, just like the massive man in Footbridge. Alternatively, why would the claim of the massive man in Footbridge be non-restricting, if the claim of the sidetrack man in Switch is restricting? Walen's idea is that we do not have baseline freedom to use his body in certain ways. But why can we not use his body to save the five when it is permissible for us to destroy the body of the sidetrack man for this purpose? What it seems to boil down to is that we would be using the massive man's body, to which he has a claim, to save the five, whereas we would not be using the sidetrack man's body to save the five, though we would kill him. But just why is it problematic to use the massive man's body? Here it becomes difficult to marshal a response that does not sound very much like the MP, to which the RCP(R) is being offered as an alternative – the massive man has a right that we not use his body like a tool to save the five, which implies that we have a correlative duty not to use his body as a means of saving them. We return to this point below.

Second, the RCP(R) also has counterintuitive implications. To see this, consider Walen's discussion of what he calls Trolley Switch Tool cases.<sup>50</sup> First, consider:

**Trolley Switch Tool:** In order to throw the switch to save the five, an agent needs to use a tool. She sees one lying nearby that she knows belongs to the sidetrack man. If she doesn't use his tool, she can't save the five, but if she uses it, she will kill him.

It seems permissible for the agent to use the sidetrack man's tool. However, one might have thought that on the RCP(R), it would be impermissible to use the switch to save the sidetrack man, given that the sidetrack man owns the switch. If so, this would seem to be a problem for the RCP(R).

Recognizing that the RCP(R) could have this implication, Walen offers the following response:

the harm of being hit by a trolley is not directly connected to the interest we normally take to be protected by a property claim over an object like a tool. We normally take that kind of property claim to protect a few limited interests: having access to the tool when one wants to use it, excluding others from using it if one wants to, and being able to sell it, loan it, or give it away as one sees fit. But that interest is the kind of slight interest that could be outweighed by the empowering claims of the five.<sup>51</sup>

The difficulty with this argument is that it seems that we are no longer talking about property rights understood as the things in the world that an agent can presumptively use to pursue her ends. It seems that Walen has added a further requirement: that in order for certain things in the world that an agent can presumptively use to count in certain cases such as trolley dilemmas, the agent must have also intended to use them for a specific end. This is problematic for two reasons. First, this seems to be appealing to an agent's mental state, which should be disavowed by victim-centered theories such as Walen's. Second, in this case, it does not seem to make a normative difference whether an agent has intended to use a particular thing for a particular end. Consider:

<sup>51</sup> *Ibid.*

**Trolley Switch Tool 2:** In order to throw the switch to save the five, an agent needs to use a tool. She sees one lying nearby that she knows belongs to the sidetrack man. Furthermore, the agent knows that the sidetrack man bought the tool so that he can use it for a daily visit to a doctor and that the sidetrack man would die if he cannot get to the doctor for his daily treatment. If she doesn't use his tool, she can't save the five, but if she uses it, she will kill him.

Walen claims that in this case 'the agent has no right to disrupt the owner's use of his switch', because the sidetrack man's 'ownership interest is directly connected with his interest in his life'.<sup>52</sup> But intuitively, the agent's moral reasons against using the sidetrack man's tool in this case seem no more stringent than in Trolley Switch Tool. If this is right, then the fact that an agent has intended to use a particular thing that he owns for a particular end does not seem to make a difference, contrary to the RCP(R).

Let us return to considering whether the RCP(R) is truly distinct from the MP. Walen has stressed that 'the key insight in the RCP is that restricting claims lower the barriers to causing or allowing a patient to suffer harm' and he has claimed that this is theoretically different from the MP, according to which it is much harder to justify causing or allowing someone to suffer a particular kind of harm *as a means* to some end than as a side-effect of pursuing some morally equivalent end.<sup>53</sup> However, one might have thought that the claim that 'restricting claims lower the barriers to causing or allowing a patient to suffer harm' is implied by the claim that 'non-restricting claims raise the barriers to causing or allowing a patient to suffer harm'. This in effect is precisely what the MP says when it says that 'it is much harder to justify causing or allowing someone to suffer a particular kind of harm as a means to some end'. Consider an analogy. Suppose that someone asserts that negative rights are more stringent than positive rights. This seems to imply that positive rights are less stringent than negative rights. If so, one might wonder whether, understood this way, the RCP and the MP are truly distinct. Of course, it is open to Walen to claim that while non-restricting claims and harming someone as a means to some end both raise the barriers to action, they do so in different ways and to different degrees. If so, it is incumbent on Walen to explain what those differences are.

Walen does endorse a discussion by Øverland in which, purportedly, the TMO (and therefore the RCP) and the MP give different verdicts:

**Armor:** Tom impermissibly attacks Mary with lethal means. She can save herself by killing Tom with a flamethrower. To the left of Tom is a person, Mo. If Mary uses her flamethrower she will kill Mo. Killing Mo to save only herself would be disproportionate and Mary decides not to do so. However, Mo is wearing light armor, and can be used as a shield without being killed. But using Mo as a shield will impose some cost on him.<sup>54</sup>

In Armor, Mary needs to use as a means a person that she is not permitted to kill with the flamethrower because doing so would be disproportionate. According to Øverland, it could be permissible intentionally to impose a certain cost on Mo when using him as a shield even though it would not be permissible to impose the same cost on a bystander, even if the bystander were also wearing light armor. For Øverland (and therefore Walen), the reason it is permissible to impose some additional cost on Mo is because Mo's presence gives rise to cost. Øverland holds that the MP is unable to explain why it is permissible to impose some cost on Mo but impermissible to impose the same cost on a bystander who is also wearing a light armor.

However, Øverland has not explained why it would be impermissible to impose some cost on a bystander or why the MP would imply this. After all, it is stipulated that the bystander would also be wearing a light armor and could be used as a shield without being killed. Given this, it seems that a case can be made that Mary would be permitted to defend herself even if this would impose some cost on the bystander, as long as the cost was proportionate. In other words, even on the MP, the bystander could be used as a shield as long as the cost was proportionate, for instance, if the bystander will not be killed. If so, it is not obvious that this case shows that the TMO (and therefore the RCP) and the MP would give different verdicts.

## VI. CONCLUSION

Øverland and Walen have presented novel and interesting variants of nonconsequentialism. Upon closer scrutiny, however, it seems that each would reach the wrong verdict in a broad range of cases in which other nonconsequentialist views, both agent-centered and victim-centered, would reach the correct verdict. Moreover, we have seen that Walen's reformulated version of the RCP using a toolkit baseline is beset with problems of its own. We conclude that these views are not plausible alternatives to agent-centered nonconsequentialist views such as the DDE.

## 6

## The Significance of Intentions

War inevitably results in the deaths of both combatants and non-combatants. Whilst some think that the deaths of combatants, especially those who fight in unjust wars, is of relatively little moral significance (something I will call into question in later chapters), many agree that killing non-combatants is often wrong, and has great moral significance even when it is not wrong. Yet non-combatant casualties do not necessarily make war wrong. The value that can be achieved through war, or individual acts in war, can make war or acts in war proportionate. This is so if the good is sufficiently great to outweigh the harm. In the previous chapter, we explored a way of understanding the significance of killing, when compared with letting die, whether that killing is done intentionally or as a side effect of a further good that one is seeking.

Direct and intentional harm to both combatants and non-combatants is also a familiar part of war. Whilst war inevitably involves direct and intentional attacks on combatants, and these are not widely condemned, many condemn direct and intentional attacks on non-combatants, especially if those attacks are lethal. Such attacks are also legally prohibited, even when they are part of a war that is justified overall.

One reason why such attacks are normally unjustified is that they are pointless—the harm that is inflicted on non-combatants often achieves little or nothing that could justify harming others, whereas harming combatants directly is necessary to avert the threats they pose. If non-combatants are harmed as a side effect of killing combatants, though, their deaths can be justified by the value that killing combatants achieves. So, their deaths are not pointless. But this explanation for the perceived significance of direct attacks on non-combatants is at best incomplete. It seems much harder to justify intentionally and directly attacking non-combatants than harming them as a side effect of attacks on combatants (or at least liable combatants) other things being equal—when doing so is equally effective in promoting the ends of the war.

A standard view in non-consequentialist ethics explains the fact that harm to non-combatants is tolerated as long as they are not directly attacked—the view that it is harder to justify intentional killing than to justify killing as a side effect. And that view has a long history in just war theory. However, the view that intentions are relevant to permissibility has come under attack from both consequentialists and non-consequentialists. My aim in this chapter is explain why both consequentialists and non-consequentialists should accept the relevance of intentions to permissibility.

In section 6.1, I defend the view that intentions are relevant to permissibility if consequentialism is true. This conclusion is interesting in itself, as the view that intentions are irrelevant to permissibility has often been seen as one controversial feature of a consequentialism.<sup>1</sup> But my argument also helps to clarify differences in the role that intentions play in consequentialist and non-consequentialist ethics. Section 6.2 shows that a non-consequentialist view of the significance of intentions is intuitively powerful both in and out of the context of war. Section 6.3 explores the relationship between the significance of using a person, her personal prerogatives, and the disvalue of heteronomy. This section suggests a unified approach to the wrongness of killing and the wrongness of intentional killing—ideas that have seemed distinct. Section 6.4 offers an account of the way in which heteronomy impacts on the justification of harming others, and draws conclusions about the scope and strength of the principle that it is objectionable to involve others in our plans. Section 6.5 explores the moral significance of acting on bad intentions in the light of the previous discussion. Section 6.6 then explores the significance of intentions in the context of war, and especially in the context of the distinction between combatants and non-combatants.

### 6.1 Consequentialism and the Significance of Intentions

In the old days (perhaps these days never existed . . . ) the philosophical terrain was simple. Non-consequentialists thought that intentions could make conduct wrong. Consequentialists thought otherwise. The relationship between consequentialism and non-consequentialism on the one hand, and the relevance or irrelevance of intentions to permissibility on the other, though, is more complex than this suggests.

Consequentialism (as I will understand it) is the view that we are always required to act in a way that has the best consequences. A more formal and complete way of understanding this idea is as follows. A person is presented with a set of options, where the selection of an option makes a possible world actual. If consequentialism is true, she is required to pick the option (or one of a range of options) where taking that option would bring about a possible world that has equal or greater value than the world that would be brought about by taking any other option.

Non-consequentialism is just the view that consequentialism is false. Non-consequentialists might differ from consequentialists in thinking that people are sometimes permitted to bring about worlds that are not best, or that they are required to do so. Many non-consequentialists now reject the significance of intentions. On this view, although we are sometimes permitted or required to take options that consequentialists consider suboptimal, this is not because of the intentions with which we act if we take an optimal option. Thus, problematic intentions do not make our optimal acts wrong.

<sup>1</sup> See, especially, S Kagan *The Limits of Morality* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1989).

Just as non-consequentialists may reject the significance of intentions to permissibility, consequentialists may accept the significance of intentions to permissibility because the intention with which one acts may make a difference to the value of a world that one brings about. So where one has the option of acting with a certain intention, or not, one may be required either to act with, or without, that intention, depending on the difference that the intention makes to the value of possible worlds. I will argue that the best version of consequentialism implies that intentions are relevant to permissibility.

Consequentialism is a general view with many variations, where views differ in the facts they treat as valuable. A simple utilitarian view is that aggregate happiness is the only kind of value. The best world that one can bring about, on this view, is the world that has the greatest aggregate happiness. Few consequentialists believe this simple utilitarian view. Most endorse pluralism about value, where there are irreducible qualitative differences in the valuable facts that one might find in a world. For example, some think that certain kinds of relationships are intrinsically valuable, others that certain distributions of welfare, such as equal distributions, are intrinsically valuable, and still others that it is intrinsically valuable that people have the level of welfare that they deserve to have.

Many consequentialists also believe that certain actions have intrinsic value.<sup>2</sup> This is the view that the value of some state of affairs inherently depends on the quality of the actions that a person performs, and not simply on the consequences of those actions. This seems clearly true of many actions. The value of great artistic and sporting achievements is not reducible to the outcome of the actions that achieve what they do. The acts of expressing gratitude, giving gifts, fulfilling promises, and so on are also valuable in virtue of intrinsic features of the acts themselves, rather than merely the consequences of such acts.

It is a short step from this idea to the view that certain actions can be intrinsically disvaluable. Torturing a person is disvaluable not only because of the consequences for the person tortured, but also because of features of the torture itself. Betraying a friend is disvaluable not simply because of the results of the betrayal but because of features of the act of betrayal itself, and so on.

It is a short further step to the view that intentions are relevant to permissibility if consequentialism is true. For intrinsically valuable acts are valuable in part because of the intentions with which they are performed, and intrinsically disvaluable in part because of the intentions with which they are performed.

<sup>2</sup> See S Kagan *Normative Ethics* (Colorado: Westview, 1998) 28–9.

Expressing gratitude, giving gifts, and fulfilling promises are intrinsically valuable as acts because they are performed with the appropriate intentions. Torturing a person, and betraying a friend, are intrinsically disvaluable as acts because they are performed with bad intentions.

The idea that intentions can make a world better or worse is even clearer in clean hypothetical cases. Consider this familiar case:

*Trolley:* A trolley is heading on a track towards five people. If Dorabella, a bystander, does nothing the five will be killed. Dorabella can save the five only by pulling a lever, diverting the trolley to another track where Fiordiligi is situated. Fiordiligi will then be killed.

To see how intentions contribute to the value of a world, compare these two worlds. In World 1, Dorabella turns the trolley in order to save the five, regretting the fact that Fiordiligi will be killed. In World 2 she does so in order to kill Fiordiligi, regretting the fact that the five will be saved. Other things equal, World 1 is obviously better than World 2—it is better that a person who kills some and saves others acts with good intentions rather than bad intentions.

Indeed, non-consequentialists who reject the significance of intentions to permissibility frequently accept that intentions are relevant to some other morally significant facts about the person or the action, and this makes their view more plausible. For example, Judith Jarvis Thomson thinks that Dorabella does not act wrongly because of any bad intention that she has; but she accepts that intentions can affect the gravity of wrongdoing, for she accepts that intentions affect the gravity of crimes, and presumably that the gravity of crimes is related to the gravity of wrongs.<sup>3</sup> And she accepts that a person who does the right thing from a bad intention acts badly, though not wrongly.<sup>4</sup> Similarly, T M Scanlon rejects the significance of intentions to permissibility, but accepts that Dorabella's permissible act would have a bad meaning if she acts from a bad intention.<sup>5</sup>

But a world containing graver wrongs is worse than a world containing less grave wrongs; a world containing bad acts is worse than a world containing good acts; and a world containing acts with a bad meaning is worse than a world containing acts with a good meaning.<sup>6</sup> So if these non-consequentialists are right about the ways in which intentions can affect the quality of acts, and consequentialism is true, World 1 is better than World 2. It follows that Dorabella makes the state of the world worse if she acts on bad intentions rather than good intentions, and consequentialists should conclude that her bad intentions make her act wrong.

<sup>3</sup> See J J Thomson 'Physician-Assisted Suicide: Two Moral Arguments' (1999) 109 *Ethics* 497, 515.

<sup>4</sup> Thomson 'Physician-Assisted Suicide' 517.

<sup>5</sup> *Moral Dimensions: Permissibility, Meaning, Blame* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2008).

<sup>6</sup> Thomson, it should be noted, rejects all of these views, because she rejects the view that things can just be good or bad *simpliciter*—they can be good only as kinds on her view. This suggests that worlds cannot be good or bad *simpliciter*. See *Normativity* (Chicago: Open Court, 2008). Of the daft views that have been defended by excellent philosophers, this one is right up there! For a vivid reply to views of this kind that I am in sympathy with, see L Temkin *Rethinking the Good: Moral Ideals and the Nature of Practical Reasoning* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2012) 20–2.

Non-consequentialists, like Thomson and Scanlon, can coherently reject the significance of intentions to permissibility because they reject the consequentialist view that all morally significant facts are relevant to wrongness and permissibility. They think that some facts are morally significant in altering the value of an act without altering its permissibility. Some facts about an action contribute to the character and/or meaning of that act without making it permissible or wrong. But this distinction between good-making properties of acts that are duty-grounding and those that are not is unavailable to consequentialists, as I characterized it above, for consequentialism just is the view that all valuable properties of a state of affairs are duty-grounding—they all contribute to making one world better than another, and that determines what we should do.

Perhaps some consequentialists might dig their heels in and claim that the value of a world does not depend on Dorabella's intentions. This seems in itself hard to believe, but to reinforce the conclusion that intentions make such a difference, consider larger groups of acts. Suppose, for example, that every person ever saved from disaster was saved in order to benefit that person. Now compare a world where all of these savings were done for personal glory, rather than in order to benefit these people. The first world is obviously better in this respect than the second. Even more extreme: compare a world where every act ever performed was done with good intentions with a world where other things are equal, but all of these acts were done with bad intentions. The first world is obviously better than the second. But these conclusions are justified simply because it is better that a person acts on good intentions rather than bad intentions, and this is enough to support the view that intentions matter to permissibility if consequentialism is true.

Consequentialists should thus accept that intentions are relevant to permissibility. Consequentialists should even embrace the more extreme conclusion that we should always act on the best intentions—for surely a world where we act on the best intentions is better than a world where we act on intentions that are less good. The view that intentions are irrelevant to permissibility is more plausible if consequentialism is false.

### 6.2 Non-Consequentialism and Bad Intentions

Given that intentions affect the value of acts, the best non-consequentialist argument for the irrelevance of intentions to permissibility draws on the idea that some facts that affect the value of acts do not make those acts permissible or wrong. Here I draw together, summarize, and in some cases develop arguments that I have made elsewhere for the significance of intentions to permissibility.

#### 6.2.1 A Brief Case for the Significance of Bad Intentions

First, I provide some positive support for the view that bad intentions affect permissibility. Suppose that a person intentionally kills her enemy—a completely innocent person—simply out of spite. Doing this is normally wrong. But now suppose that doing it also causes some good to occur, which would be sufficient to justify the killing. If that good plays no causal role at all in the person's decision to kill her enemy, it cannot justify or exonerate the person's conduct. It would have been sufficient to exonerate conduct that is outwardly similar. But it does not exonerate the person's actual conduct, with all of the features that it has.

It fails to justify the act for two reasons. First, it is hard to see how the moral quality of the act can be affected by some fact if that fact has no bearing on its performance. Justified wrongdoing is wrongdoing that is done for sufficient reason. If the good does not play an appropriate role in bringing that conduct about by motivating the person's decision to act in the relevant way, it does not justify it.

Second, the person's actual conduct has features that make it bad, and the good that the conduct brings about in no way justifies the conduct having these features. The relevant feature in this case is the intention to kill the enemy out of spite. Consider Dorabella. What justifies her killing Fiordiligi intentionally out of spite, rather than doing something else—either not killing her, or killing her only without intending to do so, and with regret? The good occurs in both killings, so it cannot justify killing with the former properties rather than the latter. But the former properties, everyone agrees, make the conduct bad. Why should these bad features of the conduct not be duty-grounding? Dorabella ought not to kill Fiordiligi out of spite. If she kills Fiordiligi, she ought to do so to save the five, and with regret. And that is because of the bad properties that her action will have if she kills her intentionally out of spite.

Given this basic case, what arguments are available for the irrelevance of intentions to permissibility? Thomson and Scanlon support their view in three ways. First, they appeal to the intuition that it is bizarre for a person deciding how to act to judge whether some act is permitted based on the intention that she will have if she acts. Second, Scanlon argues that the lack of free choice over what intention to act with makes intentions irrelevant to permissibility. Third, they point to the fact that we don't have a reason to discourage a person from acting in a way that would otherwise be permitted on the basis of the bad intentions with which she will act. These arguments all fail.

#### 6.2.2 Deciding about Intentions

When considering whether some act is permitted, one does not normally consider directly the intentions with which one will act, but rather the consequences that the act will have for those affected by it. For example, in deciding whether it is permissible to turn the trolley in *Trolley*, Dorabella would normally consider the importance of saving the five, and the significance of the fact that the one will be killed. It would be odd, Thomson and Scanlon argue, to consider whether she will act in order to kill Fiordiligi in determining whether turning the trolley is permitted.

This does seem odd, but the conclusion that intentions are irrelevant to permissibility does not follow. Suppose that in deliberating about whether it is permissible for her to turn the trolley, Dorabella considers the value of saving the five, and the significance of the fact that the one will be killed, as she should. All agree that these are the relevant facts. A person who deliberates in this way is already committed to recognizing the value of the lives of the one and the five in the appropriate way; she is committed to those values bearing on her action. If she concludes that the value of the five is sufficient to justify turning the trolley, despite the moral significance of killing the one, she is deciding on the basis of facts about her action that favour it or count against it. If she concludes that the considerations that favour her action permit it, and countervailing considerations are not decisive, and she concludes that she may act on this basis, there is no further reasoning that she needs to go through to decide which intention to act on. The considerations that favour her action just are those that constitute her intention. The question ‘but what if I will act in order to kill Dorabella’ simply does not arise.

It would be artificial for a person to deliberate on the appropriate grounds about the permissibility of saving the five and believe that she will not be motivated by these grounds when she decides to act. For the whole point of determining whether it is permissible for her to turn the trolley is to determine whether the considerations that apply to her action count decisively for or against it or not.

This explains why it seems so odd for a person to consider her intentions in determining what it is permissible to do—her intentions just follow from her deliberations about the facts that make her action permissible or not. But it does not follow that a person who does not deliberate appropriately about whether an act is permitted, and thus acts on bad intentions, acts permissibly despite those bad intentions.

When we consider whether a person acts wrongly if she acts on bad intentions, we do better to consider our intuitions from the third-person perspective. There is room for dispute about cases like *Trolley*, where different people have different intuitions about whether Dorabella acts wrongly if she acts on bad intentions. But there are some cases where it seems very clear that intentions make a difference to permissibility.

Here are some examples that I have offered. It seems permissible to distribute leaflets in support of the Nazis where one’s family will be killed if one does not comply, but only if one distributes those leaflets to save one's family and not simply to support the Nazi cause.<sup>7</sup> It is sometimes permissible to kill a person to pre-empt her suffering an even worse death, but this does not exonerate contract killers who pre-empt even worse contract killers from acting.<sup>8</sup> It is permissible to buy rat poison in order to control the rats in one's house, but not in order to kill one's partner. And it is permissible to lie to one's partner in a committed relationship in order to organize a surprise party for him, but not in order to cheat on him.<sup>9</sup>

In such cases, if the person acts on bad intentions, she does not decide to act on the basis of determining whether her acts are permitted and why. The bad reasons for which she acts cannot justify her action. The fact that she could have acted for better reasons is irrelevant.

#### 6.2.3 Choosing Intentions

Scanlon argues that we lack the ability to choose which reasons we act for, and therefore lack the ability to choose to act for one intention rather than another, and thus that intentions do not affect permissibility.<sup>10</sup>

Here are two responses. First, Scanlon's argument relies on the following idea. Suppose that there are two actions (or one action with two properties),  $x$  and  $y$ , where the difference between  $x$  and  $y$  is some valuable property,  $p$ , that  $x$  has but  $y$  lacks. I can be required to do  $x$  rather than  $y$  only if I can choose whether to bring  $p$  about, for only then can I choose  $x$  over  $y$ .

This might be understood as an implication of a version of 'ought implies can'. But it is a strict version of this principle, and there are several reasons why it is false.<sup>11</sup> The one that is most important in this context is this. Where one 'can't' choose to bring  $p$  about because one has a bad character or is badly motivated, one nevertheless ought to bring about  $p$ . For example, suppose that a black defendant is rightly convicted of a serious offence before a racist judge. The judge ought to condemn the conduct of the black defendant, let us suppose, out of respect for the victim and her family. But suppose that the racist judge cannot choose whether to convey racist hostility if he condemns the defendant; his racist hostility will just come out. Nevertheless, it is wrong for him to do this. Or suppose that a person with a disability needs to be instructed about how to do a certain job. The person's boss may not have free choice over whether to give the instructions in a patronizing tone of voice; no amount of reflection at that time will lead him to be able to control this tone of voice. Yet giving the instructions in this tone of voice is wrong.

<sup>7</sup> See V Tadros *The Ends of Harm: The Moral Foundations of Criminal Law* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2011) 158.

<sup>8</sup> For a more complex version of this case, see *The Ends of Harm* 159.

<sup>9</sup> I discuss these cases in much greater depth in *Wrongs and Crimes* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2016) ch. 16.

<sup>10</sup> *Moral Dimensions* 59–60.

<sup>11</sup> I give a further reason in *The Ends of Harm* 146—that the lack of ability to choose can ground an excuse to wrongdoing, rather than vitiating wrongdoing itself.

The same thing is true of a person who cannot choose whether she acts for better or worse reasons. Suppose that if Dorabella turns the trolley, she is unable to choose to turn it in order to save the five, but will inevitably do so in order to kill Fiordiligi. If this is true because of Dorabella's bad character or attitudes, her inability to choose her reason for action does not undermine her obligation to act for appropriate reasons.

A second response is that choice does feature in intention formation in a way that is appropriate to ground an obligation to act on one intention rather than another. Scanlon's account of the view that intentions matter to permissibility is presented in the following artificial way: a person first decides whether to perform a certain action, and then decides whether to perform it for one reason or another. This does make the idea of choosing which intention to act for hard to understand. How did the person come to decide to perform the action in the first place? She must have thought the action valuable in some way. But then, it seems, she gives up valuing it in that way and comes to value it in some other way. And that seems hard to do.

But this is not the most plausible way in which choice features in intention formation—intentions are formed in the course of deciding what to do, rather than after we have decided what to do. We form intentions to act by considering the value of certain actions, and in the light of these values we decide between them. A person may decide whether to pursue some goal, such as saving five people. If she sees that it is valuable to do this, she forms an intention to do it. And when she then considers the costs to herself or others, she may either retain or abandon that intention. Alternatively, she may value killing a person, and in the light of that form the intention to do so. She may then find doing so too costly to herself or others, and so abandon that intention.

Now consider Dorabella. She might either value the saving of the five, and then form the intention to do that. Or she might value the killing of Fiordiligi and then form the intention to do that. If her values determine which intention she forms, she has the appropriate control over which intention she forms. She ought to decide to save the five, and then consider whether the killing of the one would make furthering that intention wrong. And, after deliberating in the appropriate way, she ought to act on her intention. She ought not to decide to kill Fiordiligi, and then consider whether saving the five gives her reason not to do so!<sup>12</sup>

<sup>12</sup> This complements a similar analysis of intention formation and choice in the context of inchoate wrongs in *Wrongs and Crimes* 315–17. For further analysis of choice and intention formation, see N Kolodny 'Scanlon's Investigation: The Relevance of Intent to Permissibility' (2011) 52 *Analytic Philosophy* 100.

#### 6.2.4 Discouraging Wrongdoing

A further challenge to the relevance of intentions to permissibility comes from the attitudes and actions of third parties, where a person's act will be done with bad intentions but would be justified if done with good intentions.

Thomson considers the case of a doctor who would be permitted to disconnect life support from a terminally ill patient, but who will do so intending death. She writes:

Would we have the hospital official in charge of supervising such decisions to say to the patient (or to his guardian, if he is in a coma): "It would be illegal for her to do the disconnecting. You'll just have to wait until we can find a doctor who won't disconnect with that intention."<sup>13</sup>

This seems absurd. But its absurdity does nothing to establish the irrelevance of intentions to permissibility.

Here is why it is absurd. Suppose that the patient's condition is sufficiently grave to justify turning off the life support. Certain intentions cannot contribute to justifying that decision. It is not clear that intending death is one of those intentions, but there are clear cases, such as the intention that the doctor might enjoy seeing the family watch the patient die. Suppose that the doctor will act only with this intention, and not to relieve the suffering of the patient.

Now the family and the official have a choice—either the doctor turns off the life support with the bad intention, or they wait. Between these options, they may have good reason to prefer that the doctor turns off the life support, at least if the wait is too long. But there is a third thing that the doctor could do—turn off the life support in order to relieve suffering. Because of the doctor's bad motivations, the family cannot bring it about that the doctor takes this option, and this may give them reason to ensure that the doctor turns off the life support, even if this is done with a bad intention. But the extra option that the doctor has makes his taking this option wrong.

It is only if we treat the option of turning off the life support as irrelevant to the doctor's decisions, for one of the other reasons explored earlier, that the options from the first-person and the third-person perspectives are relevantly similar. As we have already seen, there are no good grounds for treating this option as irrelevant.

### 6.3 Intending Harm or What Is Close to Harm

The idea that bad intentions can make conduct that is otherwise permitted wrong, even if it is true (as I have argued), does not completely explain our intuitions in standard hypothetical cases where conduct seems wrong because of the intentions with which it is done, nor the prohibition on intentional attacks on non-combatants in war.

In standard cases, I harm a person or group as a means to some greater good. But then I act on a good intention—to achieve the greater good. And I may act with an appropriate attitude about the harm that I inflict as a result of executing my intention—I inflict it only with regret. For example, suppose that as part of a just war against a brutal enemy, my unit can demoralize the enemy by killing completely innocent non-combatants. Doing this will end the war more quickly, securing victory for the just side. Furthermore, suppose that fewer people will die as a side effect if I do this than will be killed if we attack combatants. If the same people will be killed through being targeted that would be killed as a side effect of attacking unjust combatants, doing this may well be permitted.<sup>14</sup> But if different people will be killed, it seems that I must target the combatants rather than the non-combatants.

Our diverging intuitions in cleaner pairs of hypothetical cases seem closely related. Compare *Trolley* with:

*Bridge:* Dorabella is on a bridge with Fiordiligi. A trolley is heading on a track under the bridge towards five people who will be killed if Dorabella does nothing. Dorabella can save the five only by throwing Fiordiligi from the bridge onto the tracks. Fiordiligi's body will stop the trolley, saving the five, but Fiordiligi will be killed.

In contrast with *Trolley*, it seems wrong for Dorabella to kill Fiordiligi even if she acts with the best intentions that she could have—the intention to save the five, regretting the killing of the one. But if she acts on the best intention that she has, it is not obvious that she brings about a world with less value than if she does not.

Here are two familiar and contrasting explanations of the significance of intentions to permissibility that might explain our diverging intuitions in *Trolley* and *Bridge*. The first, which is the focus of this section, is that there is a stringent prohibition on intending harm because those with those intentions set their will towards something that one should be especially averse to. The second, which I explore in the remainder of the chapter, is that it is normally wrong to use a person for the sake of some goal if using them harms the person.

<sup>14</sup> This is an application of what Frances Kamm calls the Principle of Secondary Permissibility. See *Intricate Ethics: Rights, Responsibilities, and Permissible Harm* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007) 169–73.

The first explanation is well expressed by Thomas Nagel in the context of the wrongness of twisting a child's arm to achieve a greater good:

Why should we consider ourselves far more responsible for what we do (or permit) intentionally than for consequences of action that we foresee and decide to accept but that do not form part of our aims (intermediate or final)?... When I twist the child's arm intentionally I incorporate that evil into what I do: it is my deliberate creation and the reasons stemming from it are magnified and lit up from my point of view. They overshadow reasons stemming from greater evils that are more "faint" from this perspective, because they do not fall within the intensifying beam of my intentions even though they are consequences of what I do.<sup>15</sup>

This explanation is neither morally compelling nor does it have intuitive appeal.<sup>16</sup> It is not morally compelling because the fact that one aims at something that has bad properties does not seem morally problematic when one does not aim at it because it has these bad properties. It is not the bad intrinsic properties of the thing that motivates one to intend to bring it about, but its good instrumental properties. So one does not direct one's will towards something that is intrinsically bad because of the facts that make it intrinsically bad. One does so because of the facts that make it instrumentally good. And it is hard to see what is morally amiss about this.

For example, if Dorabella throws Fiordiligi off the bridge in order to save the five in *Bridge*, she aims to do something that harms Fiordiligi. But it is not the fact that it harms Fiordiligi that motivates her to throw her off the bridge, but the fact that throwing her off the bridge will stop the trolley, saving the five. Thus, she does not set her mind towards harming Fiordiligi as such; she sets her mind towards doing something to her that will bring about a good result. For that reason, she need not have an inappropriate attitude to the harm that Fiordiligi suffers, any more than is true in *Trolley*. In both cases, she does something that inevitably results in harm, regrets that this is so, but concludes that the regrettable harm that she brings about is justified by the saving of the five.

This is confirmed when we consider variations on *Bridge* where it is clearer that the person has not set her mind towards harm itself; cases where one person uses another whilst attempting to avoid her suffering harm. The fact that the person is intending to avoid harm, in such cases, makes little difference to the wrongness of using another where the harm is inevitable.

<sup>15</sup> *The View from Nowhere* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1986) 180.

<sup>16</sup> I say more about this in 'Wrongful Intentions without Closeness' (2015) 43 *Philosophy and Public Affairs* 52.

For example, suppose that there are two ways for Dorabella to use Fiordiligi to stop the trolley in *Bridge*. If she immediately throws Fiordiligi off the bridge, Fiordiligi will certainly be killed. If she lowers Fiordiligi with a rope she may be able to get the trolley to hit Fiordiligi precisely in a place where Fiordiligi is wearing protective padding. Fiordiligi's body will certainly stop the trolley, but there is a tiny chance that the padding will prevent Fiordiligi from being harmed. It is dangerous for Dorabella to take the latter course, as she might herself be pulled in front of the trolley. Dorabella is willing to take this risk in order to give Fiordiligi a tiny chance of survival.

Dorabella is firmly committed to ensuring that Fiordiligi will not be harmed insofar as this is consistent with the pursuit of her goal of saving the five. She has not directed her will towards evil, but only towards using Fiordiligi's body; for it is possible that Dorabella can use Fiordiligi's body without harming her and she has put herself at risk to use Fiordiligi only in this way. It is difficult to support the view that the significance of harm is magnified by Dorabella's intentions. Yet the fact that Dorabella has provided Fiordiligi with this tiny chance of survival seems to make little difference to the permissibility of Dorabella's conduct.

Perhaps it might be argued that what matters is not whether harm itself is aimed at, but whether harm or something that is, in some sense, close to harm is aimed at. But it is not obvious that stopping the trolley with the person with padding is close to harm in any sense other than that it is very likely to occur, and harm is also very likely to occur in *Trolley*.

Furthermore, it does not seem to make a difference whether the person killed suffers harm as a direct result of her body being used to stop the trolley, or whether using her results in her being harmed as a side effect. For example, suppose that using her is not directly harmful, but if she is used and the trolley is stopped a fire will start in the engine room and she will be killed. This seems no less wrong than killing her in *Bridge*.<sup>17</sup>

Even failing to save a person from harm seems wrong, where the justification for failing to save the person rests on the fact that the person is useful, as Ketan Ramakrishnan points out.<sup>18</sup> To use Ramakrishnan's example, suppose that I can save a person from a heart attack by driving her to hospital, but if I do so this will prevent scientists running some harmless tests on the person that will provide a cure to five people who suffer from a rare disease. I ought to drive the person to the hospital, even though I do not use her, her being used is not harmful, and her being used does not itself cause her harm.

<sup>17</sup> For further discussion, see Tadros 'Wrongful Intentions without Closeness'.

<sup>18</sup> 'Treating People as Tools' (2016) 44 *Philosophy and Public Affairs* 133, 141–2.

### 6.4 Prerogatives, Duties, and Using

We have already seen that something like the following idea has powerful intuitive support. If a decision to harm a person, or not to prevent her from being harmed, is taken in order to achieve some good, and if the person is used to achieve that good, and the person does not consent to be harmed in that way, it is harder to justify the harm that is inflicted on her than it would be were the harm to occur as a side effect of an act that does not otherwise involve the person.<sup>19</sup>

#### 6.4.1 Prerogatives as Partial Ground of the Wrongness of Using

Here is a further intuition: if some good will occur only because a person is used, or is useful, in bringing it about, the fact that a person is permitted not to pursue a certain goal at a certain cost helps to explain why we cannot justify inflicting that cost on her. On this view, there is an important relationship between a person's personal prerogative not to pursue certain valuable goals and the wrongness of harmfully using her to pursue that goal. Personal prerogatives are at least partial grounds of the wrongness of using.

To see that this idea is appealing, notice that if the cost that would be inflicted on Fiordiligi were sufficiently low that she would be required to bear that cost to save five people from death, it does not seem wrong for Dorabella to use her to save the five. Similarly, if Fiordiligi were required to bear a very high cost to save the five, say because she had wrongly started the trolley which threatened them, it would be permissible for Dorabella to inflict this cost on her to save the five.

Furthermore, the justification of inflicting costs on Fiordiligi seems to rest on the fact that she would be required to bear that cost to save the five were she able to do so. If Fiordiligi complains that she is used to save the five at some cost, it seems a good answer to her that she is required to bear that cost to save them anyway (or she would be so required were she able to save the five and bearing those costs was necessary to do so). This answer to the challenge that it is wrong to use Fiordiligi may not be complete, but it is at least a central part of a complete answer.

Similarly, suppose that the only way of saving the five would be to use Fiordiligi, and doing so would involve inflicting a cost on her. A good objection to her being used in this way is that she would not be required to save the five at the relevant cost. Her lack of consent to being used normally seems decisive in this case, because she does not consent to have her personal resources used in service of a goal where she is permitted not to use those resources in service of that goal.

<sup>19</sup> Roughly this idea is at the heart of Warren Quinn's seminal essay 'Actions, Intentions, and Consequences: The Doctrine of Double Effect' in *Morality and Action* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1993). Roughly Quinn's idea is further defended and developed in, for example, J McMahan 'Revising the Doctrine of Double Effect' (1994) 11 *Journal of Applied Philosophy* 201; D Nelkin and S Rickless 'Three Cheers for Double Effect' (2014) 89 *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 125; and Tadros 'Wrongful Intentions without Closeness'.

Note, here, that the idea that I am exploring is there is an asymmetric relationship between personal prerogatives and the wrongness of using, where personal prerogatives explain the wrongness of using, and not the other way around. When Fiordiligi makes her objection that it is wrong to use her because she would be permitted not to save the five at the relevant cost to herself, she is appealing to something more basic—her personal prerogative—to explain something derivative—the wrongness of using her.

To support the idea that this relationship between personal prerogatives and the wrongness of using is asymmetric, suppose that Fiordiligi would be able to save the five at the cost of her own life. It would be odd for her to object to the claim that she is required to do so by pointing to the fact that it would be wrong to use her. This explanation seems odd because her personal prerogative is more basic than the duty not to use her, and is a partial ground of that duty. Where she offers the wrongness of using her as an answer to the question whether she is required to save the five, at best she appeals to a heuristic test for when personal prerogatives arise, rather than to a ground of such a prerogative. Together, these ideas support the view that there is a deep relationship between personal prerogatives and the duty not to use others; that this relationship is asymmetric; and that the former is a partial ground of the latter.

Now note that the existence of such a grounding relationship between personal prerogatives and the duty not to use others does not imply that personal prerogatives are necessary and sufficient conditions of a duty not to use, nor that they provide a full explanation of the duty not to use. They may be partial grounds of such duties, and thus help to explain why we have these duties, without providing a complete explanation why we have them.

For this reason, I do not claim that it is always wrong to use a person in service of a goal at some cost that she would not be required to bear in service of that goal. There may be facts that ground a permission to use a person that do not ground a requirement on a person to pursue the relevant goal at the relevant cost. For example, the fact that a person who pursues a goal at some cost to herself will be the author of her own downfall may ground a permission on her not to pursue that goal. But, obviously enough, this does not ground a duty not to use her for the sake of that goal, for if she is used against her will she will not be the author of her own downfall.<sup>20</sup>

Nor do I claim that it is always permissible to use a person in service of a goal at a cost that she would be required to bear in service of that goal. Some facts may ground a duty to pursue some goal at some cost that do not ground a permission to use a person. For example, a person may be required to pursue a goal autonomously, because of the special value of autonomy. But that requirement could not ground a permission to use the person.

<sup>20</sup> Compare ‘Wrongful Intentions without Closeness’ 66–7 for the possible significance of the related distinction between exploiting and bypassing agency, and its role in explaining the non-uniform relationship between personal prerogatives and the wrongness of using.

This suggests that there may well be plenty of exceptions to the relationship between personal prerogatives and the wrongness of using. But this does not count against the view that there is an important explanatory relationship between personal prerogatives and the duty not to use others.

#### 6.4.2 Autonomy and Heteronomy

Now let us explore a challenge—that personal prerogatives not only make it wrong to use a person for the sake of some goal, but also make it wrong to harm a person as a side effect of the pursuit of some goal. To meet this challenge, we need a better understanding of the significance of using in particular—one that explains the special or deeper relevance of personal prerogatives in the case of harmful using than in the case of harming as a side effect. The best answer has to do with the different degrees to which people are integrated into the plans of others if they are either used on the one hand or harmed as a side effect on the other. The difference between these things can be understood as a difference in the degree of heteronomy between them. In short, when a person is harmfully used she is more deeply integrated into the plans of the person who uses her than if she is harmed as a side effect (other things equal), and this is a more objectionable form of heteronomy.

Begin with this rough account of autonomy. A person has autonomous control over her life if her decisions shape her life in the light of her character, values, and aims. The fact that a person autonomously controls her life is valuable insofar as her life is appropriately shaped by those decisions in the light of valuable features of her character, values, and aims. So, a person who controls her life in the light of her hatred of other races is autonomous, but this dimension of her life is not valuably autonomous. Furthermore, a person's life is valuable as an autonomous life only to the extent that the person does not act wrongly, even if the end pursued in the wrongdoing is valuable.

Personal prerogatives leave a person free to determine which ends to use her personal resources in service of. A person is permitted to set herself ends that are suboptimal from the impersonal point of view. This is appropriate because a person is not a mere vessel in service of the good. By reflecting on this idea, we are also able to determine what duties a person has—a person's duties depend not only on the value of the ends that a person might serve, but also on her own value as an autonomous agent who is not a mere vessel of the good.<sup>21</sup> A heteronomous life is a life lacking in autonomy. But although a lack of autonomy is central to heteronomy, it is insufficient for it. At least in central cases, heteronomy involves one person overriding or bypassing the other's autonomous decisions. The disvalue of heteronomy is not reducible to a denial of autonomy; it involves the imposition of one person on another in a way that fails to respect her autonomy.

<sup>21</sup> This idea is powerfully developed in F M Kamm *Morality, Mortality* vol. 2: *Rights, Duties and Status* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1996). See, for example, 229–30; ch. 10.

We can see that the disvalue of heteronomy normally depends on a lack of autonomy from the fact that cooperation and persuasion need not result in heteronomous lives. Where one person persuades another to cooperate with her in serving some end, the first person's aims shape the aims of the second, and yet the second person does not act heteronomously. For example, if you and I cooperate to achieve a goal, we deliberate with each other to determine which aim to pursue together, and then pursue an aim that we decide on. This involves your shaping my life according to your character, values, and aims, but this does not make my life more heteronomous because my autonomy is engaged rather than bypassed or overridden. The same thing is true where valid consent is given to being used.

Furthermore, just as autonomy can be valuable or disvaluable depending on the aim autonomously pursued, heteronomous interference need not be disvaluable. Or, at least, there may be no loss in value as a result of heteronomous interference when compared with what would have occurred without heteronomous interference. For example, suppose that I am strongly inclined to murder a person, and you prevent me from doing so. The prevention is heteronomous, but there is no loss in value compared with my murdering the person. I, as well as the victim, should be grateful for the interference, because I am prevented from committing a serious wrong.

Even in this case, though, heteronomy is worse than autonomy when we hold other things equal—it would be better were I to decide not to murder the person as a result of my own judgement, or as a result of persuasion, rather than being simply prevented from doing so.

Now note that heteronomy is scalar. One reason why is simply that lack of autonomy, which partly constitutes heteronomy, is scalar. A person's life might be shaped by her character, values, and aims to a greater or lesser extent. A cooperative relationship with another person may be in part governed by mutual deliberation and persuasion, and partly by coercion, manipulation, and deception. The degree that a person's life is heteronomous as a result depends on the mix of these things.

Similarly, a person may be coerced, manipulated, or deceived into pursuing an aim that she accepts, that she is neutral about, or that she rejects. These differences make a difference to the degree to which a person's life is heteronomous. For example, it is worse to deceive a vegetarian into eating something containing meat than a non-vegetarian, because the former is deceived into doing something that conflicts with her values whereas the latter is not. This is so even if there is nothing morally wrong with eating meat. This difference is a difference in the degree to which the person's life is heteronomous.

#### 6.4.3 Using, Affecting, and Heteronomy

Here is another dimension of heteronomy. Compare the difference between a person with a certain end, who coerces, manipulates, or deceives another person into serving that end, with a person who affects another as a side effect of an end that she has, where the effect on the other person is one that she would reject. The life of the former victim, I suggest, is more deeply and objectionably heteronomous than the life of the latter victim, other things equal. Both people are affected in a way that they reject. But the former is more deeply integrated into the plans of the person who affects her in this way than the latter.

This should not be surprising. Heteronomy is to do with extent to which a person's life is shaped by the character, aims, and values of others. One person integrates another into her aims in the light of her values and character more deeply if she uses the person in service of those aims than if she affects her as a side effect of her pursuit of those aims.

To be clear, my claim here is not that there is some independent idea of heteronomy that then grounds the wrongness of using a person. Rather, I suggest that using a person in service of an aim that the person rejects is a distinctive and deep kind of heteronomous interference; one that is especially objectionable, at least where the person rejects the relevant goal, and would be free to do so.

We can now understand the relationship between agent-relative prerogatives and the wrongness of using. Suppose that a person does not value the pursuit of a certain goal, or does not value it enough to bear a certain cost to pursue it. If she is used in service of that goal, she is deeply involved in the plan that another person has to pursue that goal. This aspect of her life is thus shaped by the aims, character, and values of another, and is to that extent heteronomous. Heteronomy of this kind is especially disvaluable where the person is permitted not to pursue the goal at the relevant cost, for she would be free to reject that goal. Heteronomous integration into the plans of the other person, in that way, fails to respond appropriately to her autonomy.

A person who affects another as a side effect may also pursue a goal that the other is free to reject. And the life of the second person is also shaped by the values and goals of the person who affects her. But she is not as deeply involved in the plans of the other person when she is not used in service of the ends of the other person. To that extent, even if she is harmed to an equal degree, and even if her autonomous pursuit of her own goals is set back to an equal degree, the disvalue of heteronomy is less deeply engaged. Thus, treating the person in that way is easier to justify.

### 6.5 Using and Usefulness

Suppose that I am right that using others against their will is a kind of heteronomous interference, and that this accurately characterizes the wrongness of using where using is wrong. The objection to using might be understood in one of two ways. First, using might be thought in itself bad. On this view, the fact that a person is used weighs against acting in a certain way. It might weigh heavily against the good that is done, and that makes it hard to justify using a person to achieve something good.

Second, the fact that the person is used to achieve some good might disable that good from justifying the conduct, even if otherwise that good would have been sufficient to justify it. On this second view, if using a person will serve a certain valuable goal, the value of that goal cannot contribute to a justification for using the person unless it is enabled. And it is enabled by facts about the person who is used—for example, the fact that the person would be required to serve that goal at the relevant cost, or the fact that the person consents to be used to serve that goal.<sup>22</sup>

This second view might also be understood in a scalar way—the justifying force of some end might depend on the extent to which a person is involved in the plans of others to achieve those ends. So, for example, the fact that a person is harmed as a side effect in the achievement of some goal makes the achievement of that goal less significant in justifying the conduct than it would otherwise be. And the fact that a person is harmfully used to achieve that goal makes the achievement of that goal even less significant in justifying the relevant conduct.

To illustrate the contrast between the first and second ideas, consider two different ways of understanding why it is wrong for Dorabella to act in *Bridge*. On the first view, the good that is done—the saving of the five—weighs heavily in favour of Dorabella’s act. But this good is outweighed by an even weightier bad—the harmful using of Fiordiligi. On the second view, the good that is done does not weigh as heavily in favour of Dorabella’s act, or weigh in favour of it at all, because Fiordiligi will be used to achieve this good.

On the best interpretation of the second view, the objection to using has greater scope. And when we see the implications of this interpretation, we also see that it is more appealing than the first view. It has greater scope because it implies that goods that would otherwise justify acting in a certain way do not do so if only a person being used can secure these goods. That is so even if the person is not in fact used.

<sup>22</sup> I offered a brief outline of this view in ‘Dimensions of Intentions: Ways of Killing in War’ in H Frowe and S Lazar, eds., *The Oxford Handbook of Ethics of War* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2018).

To see that how appealing this idea is, consider:

*Rush:* To get home in time for the football, Dorabella needs to run across a narrow bridge. Fiordiligi is on the bridge. If Dorabella runs across the bridge, Fiordiligi will be toppled off. She will then fall in front of a trolley, which her body will stop. She will be killed, but five will be saved, whom the trolley would otherwise have crushed.

It seems that if it is wrong for Dorabella to act in *Bridge*, it is wrong for her to act in *Rush*. And it seems that the explanations why both acts are wrong are closely related. But now suppose that Dorabella does not care for the saving of the five. She only wants to get home to watch the football. Then Dorabella does not use Fiordiligi to save the five. She kills Fiordiligi as a side effect of her act, but she also saves the five as a side effect of her act. It may thus seem that the principle against using cannot explain the powerful intuition that if it is wrong for Dorabella to act in *Bridge* it is also wrong for her to act in *Rush*.

There are two complementary responses that are consistent with the general idea that using a person makes Dorabella's conduct wrong, and that have identical implications. The first is that the fact that putatively justifies Dorabella's conduct in *Rush*—that the five will be saved—arises only because Fiordiligi's body blocks the track. It thus arises only because Fiordiligi's body is useful. And just as it is wrong to use a person, so it is wrong to rely on the fact that a person is useful. We might thus endorse:

*Usefulness View:* The fact that a person harms, or otherwise negatively affects a person, is especially difficult to justify if doing so relies on some good that will occur only because the person, or her body, is useful.<sup>23</sup>

This is still recognizably an intentions-based view of the wrongness of using, because the very idea of usefulness relies on intentions—something is useful if it is suitable to be used, and using is an intentional verb.

This principle is able to explain a wide range of intuitions that people have about the wrongness of harming a person, or failing to save a person from harm, where doing so achieves a greater good. For example, it explains not only why it is wrong to use a person to achieve some good in a way that harms them, but also why it is wrong to fail to save a person from harm because preventing harm to the person will make us unable to use the person to achieve a good.<sup>24</sup>

<sup>23</sup> This principle is very similar to the one defended in Ketan Ramakrishnan's excellent paper 'Treating People as Tools'. Exploring the differences here would take us too far afield.

Here is the second response. Not only does a person have a right not to be harmed where doing so will not bring about a sufficient good; she also has a right that if she is harmed where doing so will bring about a sufficient good, she is harmed with the right intention. Dorabella does not act with the right intention in *Rush*, and therefore her act is wrong.

To understand this approach, consider this simpler case:

*Trolley and Telly:* A trolley is heading on a track towards five people. If Dorabella, a bystander, does nothing the five will be killed. Dorabella can save the five only by pulling a lever diverting the trolley to another track where Fiordiligi is situated. Fiordiligi will then be killed. Pulling the lever will also turn on a football match that Dorabella wants to watch. She is indifferent about the deaths of Dorabella and the five, and pulls the lever only in order to turn on the match.

Some who favour the view that intentions are relevant to permissibility may believe that Dorabella acts wrongly, because she does not act for the reason that she needs to justify killing Fiordiligi—to save the five.<sup>25</sup> Fiordiligi not only has a right not to be killed where there is no reason to do so; she has a right not to be killed *without sufficient motivating reason*. And Dorabella has insufficient motivating reason.

Consider a complaint that Fiordiligi might offer: ‘It’s bad enough that I find myself in circumstances where I can be killed in order to save the five. If I am to be killed, it should be in order to save the five, and not for some trivial reason like watching the football.’ The same objection is available in *Rush*. Fiordiligi ought not to be killed in order to watch the football, but only for some sufficiently good reason, such as saving five people. But if Dorabella acts for that reason, she also uses Fiordiligi, and the case is just like *Bridge*.

Overall, then, we can respond to cases like *Rush* with two ideas—first, it is often insufficient to make harming another person permissible that doing so would bring about some greater good. The person must act in order to bring about that good, and that is not so in *Rush*. So Dorabella acts wrongly in part because she lacks a good intention. Furthermore, if she acts with the good intention of saving the five, she will use Fiordiligi to secure her goal, and doing that is wrong. So not only does she lack a justification for harming Dorabella, her act is not justifiable.

<sup>24</sup> See, especially, Ramakrishnan ‘Treating People as Tools’. This is an improvement on the version of the principle against using that I defended in ‘Wrongful Intentions without Closeness’, for my principle was concerned only with using a person in a way that causes harm. I did not extend it to failing to save cases as Ramakrishnan does.

<sup>25</sup> Some sceptics find this view implausible, an idea most forcibly expressed in J J Thomson ‘Physician-Assisted Suicide’. For a response, see Tadros *The Ends of Harm* ch. 7.

Altogether, this argument explains why sometimes the wrongness of achieving a good in a way that makes another person useful does not depend on the person having the intention to use others. But it does so in a way that relies on the significance of intentions to permissibility. It suggests that crossing the bridge in *Rush* is wrong either because Dorabella will use Fiordiligi to save the five, and thus act with impermissible intentions, or she will fail to act with a justification for harming Fiordiligi at all, because she will not act in order to save the five.

### 6.6 Using Combatants

The wrongness of intentionally harming others, or of using them to achieve a goal, has its most obvious application in the ethics of war in explaining why it is normally wrong to harm non-liable people to achieve a goal. Suppose that killing innocent civilians would demoralize the enemy so much that the unjust side would surrender. Doing this is often wrong, even if it saves lives when compared with alternatives, because these people are used in a way that seriously harms them, and they would not be required to give up their lives to save others. Thus, the wrongness of using others can partly explain the moral prohibition on killing non-combatants that is reflected in the law.<sup>26</sup>

It is worth noting that the stringent prohibition on using applies both to combatants and non-combatants. A person can be liable to be used to avert threats that others pose, where using her will kill her, but it takes a great deal to make a person liable to be lethally used. Thus, although I will defend non-combatant liability in both morality and war later in the book, especially in chapters 10, 11, and 12, this liability does not normally extend to liability to be lethally used to avert threats posed in war.

As I doubt that there is a fundamental distinction between the liability of combatants and non-combatants, my view implies that it is often wrong lethally to use unjust combatants to avert the threats of unjust wars either, and this might be thought implausible. However, I think that subject to a number of qualifications, doing this often is wrong. This view gains intuitive support from:

*Mines:* Kim and Kerry, combatants on the unjust side, have laid mines. They have some reason to believe that the cause that they are fighting for is just. Combatants on the just side have captured Kerry and she poses no further threat. However, she will not reveal the location of the mines. The mines will kill some just combatants if nothing is done. Kim is still in the field. Combatants on the just side can capture Kim without harming her. Alternatively, they can douse her with napalm and set her on fire. She will suffer a slow and excruciating death if the latter course of action is taken. However, if Kim is burned to death in front of Kerry, Kerry will be terrorized into revealing the location of the mines.

<sup>26</sup> I explore the limits of this explanation in ‘The Moral Distinction between Combatants and Non-Combatants: Vulnerable and Defenceless’ (2018) 37 *Law and Philosophy* 289.

If Kim is burned to death in front of Kerry, Kim is harmed as a means to avert a threat that she is responsible, and culpable, for posing to just combatants, though her culpability is not high. Many influential accounts of self-defence imply that Kim is liable to be killed to avert the threat that she poses, and I agree. Yet burning Kim to death seems wrong unless the number of lives that will be saved by doing so is large. Whereas killing Kim to eliminate the threat that she poses seems warranted, killing her as a means to influence Kerry to eliminate the threat that Kim and Kerry have together created seems wrong. Some might think that this is because of the particularly painful and gruesome way in which Kim will be killed. But I think the same thing would be true even if Kim would merely be shot in order to influence Kerry.

Against this, it might be argued that it is implausible that it is generally wrong to use combatants as a means to the greater good by killing them in order to terrorize other combatants. A standard way to resist an enemy attack involves the killing of some combatants in a unit so that other combatants in the unit flee. And wars typically end because sufficient combatants are killed that the rest of the armed forces become demoralized.

This, though, does not undermine the suggestion that the stringent prohibition on using others applies to combatants. The prohibition on using others is not without exception; lethal using may be permitted where culpability is high. It may be objected that using combatants is permitted even if with low levels of culpability. I agree that this is sometimes permitted—where there are sufficient reasons to kill them even if they are not used. In standard cases where some combatants are killed, demoralizing others, killing the first group of combatants is already justified to eliminate the threat that they pose. If they may permissibly be killed for eliminative purposes, they may also permissibly be killed as a means to secure further good ends.

Consider:

*Eliminate and Manipulate:* A unit on the unjust side of the war containing C and D attacks a unit on the just side containing X and Y. C and D have reasonable but mistaken beliefs that their cause is just. C will kill X unless X kills C first. There are two ways for X to kill C. The first way will avert only the threat that C poses to X. D will then kill Y. The second way in which X can kill C will also deter D from killing Y. The harm that will be done to C is not significantly different whichever method is used.

It is permissible for X to kill C in a way that deters Y from killing D. X can justify killing C on eliminative grounds. X's complaint about being killed in a way that protects D against being killed is, in that case, quite weak.<sup>27</sup>

Complications may arise in cases where the value of eliminating a threat that a person poses depends on their being manipulated. This is not true in *Eliminate and Manipulate*, for in that case, X's life will be saved even if the killing of C does not protect Y, and that alone would be sufficient reason for X to kill C. However, consider the following variation:

*Eliminate and Manipulate 2:* As *Eliminate and Manipulate* except that D, like C, poses a lethal threat to X and not to Y. That threat will be averted only if C is killed.

It would be pointless for X to kill C if he does not also use C to avert a threat that he faces from D. Yet it still seems permissible for X to kill C.

This case, though, is importantly different from *Mines*. In *Eliminate and Manipulate 2*, eliminating the threat that C poses to X provides sufficient reason to justify the killing of C, but only on condition that D is also deterred. C's threat can be eliminated only if C is killed. It is only the value of doing this that would be undermined by the threat that D poses were C's death not instrumental in averting D's threat.<sup>28</sup> In *Mines*, in contrast, simply capturing Kim in a way that does not harm her can eliminate Kim's threat. The *only* rationale for killing her rather than capturing her is that this will be useful as a means to influence Kerry. This may provide a decisive reason to distinguish the permissibility of killing in *Mines* and *Eliminate and Manipulate 2*.

### 6.7 Conclusion

We can see from this discussion that the principle against using has important implications for killing combatants. Whilst it interacts with other principles, and that makes it sometimes permissible to use combatants to influence others, there is no uniform permission to do so, even where combatants are liable to suffer other kinds of harm.

As we will see later in the book, the stringent prohibition on lethal using is extremely important more generally in determining the permissibility of killing in war. This is because a much larger number of people are liable to be harmed in war than many have thought. Non-combatants who causally contribute to unjust wars, as well as some who don't, are liable to be harmed or sometimes killed to avert the threats they pose. The wrongness of killing these people is often due to the fact that the good that might come from killing them, and that might be sufficiently important to justify doing so, arises only because they will be used, and using them in this way is wrong. For this reason, it is extremely important whether I am right that the *Usefulness View* is true, for that makes all the difference in shaping just decisions about the permissibility of killing non-combatants in practice.

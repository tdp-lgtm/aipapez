# The Means Principle

Larry Alexander\*

### I. Introduction

Michael Moore believes there are deontological constraints on actors' pursuit of good consequences. He believes these constraints are best conceived of as agent-relative and intention-focused prohibitions such as "you must not intentionally kill, batter, rape, steal, etc."<sup>1</sup> I, joined in recent years by Kimberly Ferzan, believe that the best interpretation of deontological constraints—the interpretation that best accounts for our intuitions about certain stock cases—is that they are constraints on the causal means by which good consequences may be achieved.<sup>2</sup> We believe those constraints can be unified under a single deontological principle, what we call the "means principle." It forbids actors from achieving otherwise good consequences by using, without their consent, others' bodies, labor, talents, or other resources that are rightfully theirs. The means principle can be viewed as a jurisdictional limitation on the reach of strong (legitimately enforceable) moral obligations.<sup>3</sup> Its focus is on the causal structure of how good consequences are to be achieved, not on the mental states of those attempting to achieve them. Actors' mental states can affect their culpability but not whether their acts are morally permissible. Even with respect to culpability, actors' beliefs rather than their intentions are the primary determinants. Their intentions can affect only the level of their culpability if they are culpable, but cannot render an otherwise non-culpable act culpable. Thus, the means principle is a stark contrast to Moore's agent-relative, intention-focused account of deontological constraints.<sup>4</sup>

### II. The Consequentialist Background

Deontological constraints do not and cannot exhaust all of our enforceable moral duties. Many of our acts are assessable only through a consequentialist lens. In the famous *Trolley* hypothetical, turning the trolley to save the five trapped workers but killing the one worker trapped on the siding is generally believed to be a morally permissible act. (Nor should it matter whether one is *deflecting* the trolley or instead *initiating* a new force, such as lowering a drawbridge that will stop the trolley but crush a bystander; deflection is, after all, a form of initiating.) If such acts are permissible, however, it is because they produce good consequences, the saving of a net four lives.<sup>5</sup> By the same token, turning the trolley from the track on which the one is trapped to the track on which five are trapped is a morally impermissible act because of its consequences.

I am going to assume that a consequentialist moral theory provides the backdrop to my discussion of deontological constraints and governs acts that do not fall afoul of those constraints or fall within the prerogatives that are the flip sides of those constraints. I shall not elaborate on what that consequentialist moral theory—call it C—looks like. For example, it is a matter of indifference for my discussion here whether “The Good” in C is monolithic or pluralistic. The nature of C, important as it is, is not my topic here.

### III. The Means Principle

The means principle (MP) is the principal deontological constraint on the pursuit of C. In other words, an act that is required by C but forbidden by the MP is morally forbidden. The MP trumps C.

What is the MP? As I said in the introductory paragraph, the best interpretation of the MP is a causal one. An act required or permitted by C is nonetheless forbidden if the good consequences that are the bases of its endorsement by C are produced by the unconsented-to use of persons’ bodies, labor, or talents.<sup>6</sup> In other words, the MP enjoins the appropriation of another’s body, labor, or talents without that person’s consent where appropriation is the causal path by which otherwise good consequences are produced. Put somewhat differently, the MP is a jurisdictional notion: A person’s body, labor, and talents are beyond the jurisdiction of strong (enforceable) morality unless the person consents to subjecting his body, labor, and talents to its jurisdiction.<sup>7</sup>

The MP explains the intuitions behind *Trolley* and other stock hypotheticals. In *Trolley*, turning the trolley to the siding is not a “using” of the one trapped worker’s body, labor, or talents. One would turn the trolley to the siding even if the one worker were not there or could escape, because turning the trolley itself saves the five whether or not the siding is occupied. The presence of the one trapped worker only reduces the amount of C-benefit turning the trolley produces. And the same applies to *Drawbridge*, in which lowering the drawbridge saves the five by stopping the trolley but kills a bystander who otherwise would not have been harmed by the trolley.

On the other hand, in *Fat Man*, where pushing the fat man onto the track is the only way to prevent the trolley from killing the five workers, the fat man’s body is the means by which the five are saved. Thus, pushing the fat man *is* a violation of the MP. So, too, in *Transplant*, is carving up the one patient to harvest his organs and save five dying patients. In both *Fat Man* and *Transplant*, the causal path by which the saving of lives is brought about runs through the unconsented-to use of a person’s body. In *Trolley*, it does not.<sup>8</sup> In every case, the relevant question is: How does the act in question satisfy C? If the answer is that it satisfies C only by using someone's body, labor, or talents as a resource without the person's consent, then the act violates the constraint by virtue of the causal path between it and satisfying C.<sup>9</sup>

I have thus far spoken of the MP as encompassing unconsented-to appropriation of another's body, labor, or talents. The talents part is perhaps redundant, as in the absence of the ability to read minds one can only appropriate another's talents through his labor. I include the reference to talents merely to make clear that appropriation without consent of another's intellectual or artistic creations violates the MP even if the creation is a song, a poem, or an idea for an invention or a story that has not yet been reduced to a tangible form.

In addition, the MP extends not only to the products of intellectual labor that do not employ any resources other than mental ones but also to the products of labor that employ one's rightful share of external resources, resources the rightful shares of which are determined by C. If one takes one's rightful share of external resources, then no one else has a claim on one's improvements of them. For were that not so, others would be appropriating not only one's rightful share under C, but the improvements produced by one's labor and talents.

Moreover, not only does the deontological constraint extend to the products of one's labor and talents that have used only one's rightful (under C) share of external resources, but it extends as well to products and resources that belonged to another but have been consensually transferred to one through gift, bequest, or exchange. (I leave open, however, whether one's C share of resources should be sensitive to one's other sources of well-being, namely the willingness of others to exchange with one or donate to one.)

Other apples are on the high branches, out of Short's reach but within Tall's. And some of the apples are on the intermediate branches, within the reach of both Short and Tall. There is a further extension of the MP worth mentioning. Suppose Al engages in conduct solely to make Barb suffer psychologically because Al derives enjoyment from Barb's psychological suffering. Were Barb not to exist, or exist but not suffer, Al would be less happy. Now Al might be violating C. But suppose his conduct is actually furthering C. Perhaps he gains more happiness from Barb's suffering than Barb loses. Perhaps Al is even among the worst off and Barb among the better off. Still, Al's furthering C through Barb's psychological suffering violates the MP. Al's using Barb's psychological suffering to further C is no different from his furthering C by using the fat man's body to save the five trapped workers.

Reflection on the preceding examples might lead one to the conclusion that the MP is really just equivalent to the following norm N: "Do not make yourself better off than had another person not existed by making the latter worse off than had you not existed."<sup>11</sup> And indeed, most cases of violation of the MP will be violations of N, and vice versa. Moreover, N would even appear to solve the so-called paradox of blackmail; for blackmail is conduct that violates N.<sup>12</sup>

There are two reasons for not equating the MP with N, however. First, N takes no account of whether the baselines for determining "worse off" and "better off" are justifiable. Suppose Barb, a candidate for high office, has cheated on Carl, her husband, and Al learns of this. Al can sell his information to a news outlet and make a lot of money. So suppose he does so. He is now better off than he would have been had Barb not existed, and Barb is now worse off than had Al not existed. Al has violated N, but he has done nothing wrong; for Barb does not deserve to be as well off as she would have been in Al's absence. She has been "used" but has not been wronged.<sup>13</sup>

Perhaps we could save N by adding a proviso that "better off" and "worse off" are to be gauged relative to a baseline of deserved well-being. There is another reason, however, not to reduce the MP to N. For the MP is violated, or so I would contend, even when the used person is, without his consent, made *better off* through the appropriation of his body, labor, talents, or psychological states. In other words, the MP is a constraint on paternalism (which might otherwise be required by C), whereas N is not. Even forcing another to labor or use his body, not only in furtherance of C, but for the benefit of the person himself, is a violation of the MP. So too is getting him to labor or use his talents by manipulating him psychologically through deception or through inducing unpleasant emotions, even if the deception and the unpleasant emotions will ultimately cause him to benefit and are consistent with C.<sup>14</sup>

One final point about the MP. It, like its close relative N, does not govern activities that we would and could carry on in the absence of those who might be harmed by those activities. For those activities, even if they harm others, do not use their bodies, labor, or talents without their consent. C, not the MP, governs those risky activities. Reckless behaviors are culpable and, if they harm others, impermissible.<sup>15</sup> But they are so generally by virtue of C, not the MP.

That is all I want to say about the MP itself. The core idea is that we are not resources for others' benefit without our consent. Nor are we resources for our own benefit except insofar as we ourselves determine. Enforceable morality has no jurisdiction over us as resources for its ends.

### IV. The Flipside of the MP: The Prerogative

The logical corollary of the MP is a prerogative not to be compelled to further C.<sup>16</sup> In other words, the MP entails a realm of moral freedom. (C itself may, and if C is indirect will, contain its own domain of prerogatives in addition to the realm carved out by the MP.)

To see this, consider *Trolley* again. As discussed, it is permissible to turn the trolley from the track with five trapped workers to the track with one. Such an act, which saves a net four lives, would further C, or so I shall assume. But even if turning the trolley furthers C, the person at the switch cannot be enforceably morally obligated to throw the switch. For to compel him thusly would be to use him as a resource without his consent and violate the MP, just as would compelling him to lie down in front of the trolley if there were no siding. Throwing the switch or not throwing it, lying down in front of the trolley or not doing so—these are all within the actor's prerogative. They are in a realm of moral freedom, not in the sense that they are beyond moral criticism, but in the sense that they are beyond rightful coercion.

On the other hand, if the trolley is heading toward the one rather than the five, switching it to the track with the five is not within the switcher's prerogative defined by the MP. The act violates C; and in prohibiting the act, the actor is not being used as a resource without his consent. Thus, his act violates C, and its prohibition does not violate the MP. His act can legitimately be forbidden on pain of punishment.

I said that the prerogative that is the flipside of the MP is an area of moral freedom but also that acts within the prerogative are morally assessable. What I mean is that the prerogative is the realm of the super- and suberogatory; the realm of virtue and vice. If one acts within one's moral prerogative, one cannot be deemed culpable—and thus justifiably punishable—for not furthering C, even if one could do so at little cost of time, effort, or expense. Not furthering C under such circumstances may well be suberogatory and thus morally discreditable. But it is not culpable and thus not deserving of sanction.<sup>17</sup>

### V. Limitations on the MP

Is the MP absolute, or is it subject to limits, and if so, which? Consenting and promising are non-controversial limits on the MP. Indeed, I would argue that they are really internal to the MP, which refers to *unconsented-to* uses of one's body, labor, and talents—not limits on it. There are other possible limits on the MP that are worth discussing. One stems from acts that have imperiled others and that will result in a violation of C unless the one whose acts are the source of the peril uses his body, labor, or talents to avert the peril. For example, if a man has accidentally thrown the trolley switch and redirected the trolley from the track with one worker to the track with five, and still has time to throw the switch again and redirect the trolley back to the one worker, he has an affirmative duty to do so. His original act, however non-culpable it was, will violate C unless he throws the switch. And he can be legitimately coerced to do so without violating the MP.

Are there other affirmative duties to act, ones not based on one's promises to do so (as might be the case with lifeguards, nannies, spouses, police officers, etc.), or based on one's acts that have imperiled others? The duty of parents to care for their children comes to mind. Does that duty apply only to parents who have voluntarily assumed it, or does it apply to all parents, regardless of how voluntarily they became parents? That is a deep and difficult issue that I will sidestep here.

Another possible external limit on the MP is also controversial, but it is one on which I have previously taken a stand. I have argued that a culpable actor who has yet to be punished to the full extent he deserves may be used as a means to further C or to protect others' rights. The use of the culpable is itself limited by the amount of suffering he deserves for his culpable act(s) in excess of the amount he has already been punished.<sup>18</sup>

The final possible (and again controversial) external limit on the MP comes under the heading of "threshold deontology." If the actor need only lift his finger to save a drowning infant, or if the sacrifice of his life would save a million lives, does the MP nonetheless protect him from being coerced to do so? Most deontologists believe that the MP does not. They believe the consequences of honoring the MP should matter and limit the MP's scope. The MP restricts the scope of C, but C restricts the scope of the MP, or so it is argued. Again, I need not settle the issue here. My interest is in the nature of the MP, not its limits. I have written on threshold deontology, not to take a final stand on it, but to point out its theoretical difficulties.<sup>19</sup> But, whether or not it is a plausible limitation on the MP, it does not bear on the nature of the MP within the limits it imposes.<sup>20</sup>

### VI. Permissibility versus Culpability

Moral permissibility turns on how things really are, not on what an actor believes. It is an ontic matter rather than an epistemic one. If there are five workers who will be saved by turning the trolley at the cost of the one worker on the siding, then unless there are qualitative factors that relevantly distinguish the one worker from the five, switching the trolley to the siding is morally permissible in fact, irrespective of the switcher's beliefs or motivations.

Culpability, on the other hand, *is* a matter of the actor's beliefs. If the actor who switches the trolley is unaware that he is saving the five trapped workers and aware that he is probably killing the one, his act of switching, though morally permissible, is highly culpable. Conversely, if the actor believes that by switching the trolley he will save the five trapped workers at the cost of the life of the one on the siding, then he acts non-culpably in switching the trolley, even if it turns out that the five workers were not trapped, or were not on the track, or were an optical illusion. For though his act was morally impermissible, his belief that he was saving the five and thus was acting permissibly renders him non-culpable.<sup>21</sup>

So far, nothing that I have said should be controversial. We recognize that one can be culpable for attempting to harm or for unjustifiably risking harm, even if the attempt turns out to be harmless or beneficial, or the "risk" of harm turns out to be zero. Indeed, we recognize as well that the culpable attempter or risker who is unknowingly performing a permissible act can be encouraged by third parties. By the same token, those whose mistaken beliefs lead them to perform morally impermissible acts—who in the self-defense literature are referred to as "innocent aggressors"—may be permissibly restrained but not punished for their non-culpable acts.

My next points *are* controversial, however. First, although the actor's beliefs affect his culpability, his intentions cannot render an otherwise permissible act impermissible. At most, the actor's intentions may aggravate his culpability if he *is* culpable. But his intentions cannot render him culpable. To see this, consider the actor who switches the trolley. If switching the trolley to save the five is morally permissible, and if the switcher believes switching will save the five, then his switching the trolley is non-culpable *even if his only reason for doing so is to kill the one on the siding*.<sup>22</sup> We want him to switch the trolley (it furthers C). And he has the prerogative not to switch it (by virtue of the MP). If the only motivation he has for switching the trolley is his desire to kill the one, then that motivation is C-useful. And if he will not switch if we will regard him as culpable for switching in order to kill the one, then it would be C-useful not to regard him as culpable.

Now I realize that this is not an argument that will convince the skeptics. For not every belief that it would be useful to hold is true. In Kavka's Toxin Puzzle, the fact that it would be useful to believe that one will drink the toxin tomorrow does not mean that one can so believe. Beliefs aim at truth, not usefulness.<sup>23</sup>

So consider these analogs of the ill-motivated trolley switcher. We may need people to carry out various tawdry but morally permissible and even morally compelling tasks. We may need CIA assassins. We may need executioners for murderers. And so on. The MP prevents us from compelling people to perform these tasks. So suppose that the only volunteers we get for the jobs of assassin or executioner are people who will enjoy the jobs. Should we regard the executioner who enjoys executing convicted murderers, or the CIA operative who enjoys assassinating terrorists, as having acted *culpably* in executing or assassinating if their acts were morally permissible and they were aware of the facts that made the acts permissible?

I would contend that the answer should be "no." I believe that the temptation to say otherwise stems from confusing an assessment of character with an assessment of culpability. The executioner and assassin who enjoy their jobs may have character flaws that make them quite unsavory people. But that does not show their morally permissible acts of execution and assassination to be culpable. So long as they act on the intentions in question only when the acts are morally permissible—or would be morally permissible if what they believed to be true were true<sup>24</sup>—they should not be blamed or punished. And if they should not be blamed or punished, that must be because they have not acted culpably.<sup>25</sup> (Likewise, if a dosage of morphine is proper for a terminal patient even though it will hasten his death, then if the doctor who knows this nevertheless administers it only because he believes in euthanasia, he should not be deemed culpable for doing so even if euthanasia is otherwise impermissible. It only requires that the use is necessary to justify the act, and it is culpable if the actor foresees that the use is necessary to justify the act.

### VII. When is Risking Violating the MP Culpable?

What risk of violating the MP are we permitted to take without being culpable? Assume, for example, that the agent is attempting to promote C (or attempting to act within his prerogative without reducing C). He estimates that his act is 80% likely to do so without violating the MP. But is that probability sufficient to avoid culpability? Consider, by analogy, whether an 80% probability of not punishing a criminal more than he deserves (including the risk of not punishing him if he is innocent) is sufficient to render us non-culpable in punishing him? Holly Smith argues that in assessing whether to acquire additional information in order to avoid breaking a promise, we should count the promise itself, as opposed to the harm to the promisee if the promise is broken, as having zero weight.<sup>29</sup> Assuming Smith is correct, should we treat the MP, as opposed to the harm to the one used, similarly? I cannot see how we could do that and still regard the MP as a deontological constraint on C. For if we assess only the underlying harm to the one used in deciding how to act in light of our estimate of the probabilities, we are engaged in precisely the decisionmaking that C, stripped of the MP constraint, requires. So we must give the deontological constraint of the MP a value apart from the harm of the using. Because we always run the risk of violating the MP—which includes punishing people more than they deserve when doing so furthers C—and of breaking promises, we need a theory for assigning such a value.

Here is the approach that I believe makes sense. First, the objective, God’s-eye view of deontological constraints looks like this: If the benefits of an act exceed its costs, it furthers C and is permissible. However, one counts as C-benefits only those that are not caused by usings (MP violations, including undeserved punishments) or caused by breaches of promises. On the other hand, one *does* count the harms to those used and harms to the promisees from breaches. Put differently, an act is objectively permissible if its benefits, excluding those causally derived from usings or breaches of promises, exceed all of its costs, including those caused by usings and breaches of promises. In *Trolley*, for example, one counts as a benefit the saving of the five workers, but one excludes as benefits the saving of lives produced by harvesting organs from the dead worker. The benefits of using him cannot be part of the calculus; only the harm of his death counts. Moving from the objective view to the subjective, culpability-determining estimates of the actor is now straightforward. The actor estimates the probability that his act will be permissible by estimating the probabilities and magnitudes of benefits not produced by usings or breaches of promises and the probabilities and magnitudes of all harms. If the net of permissible benefits over all harms, discounted by the actor's estimates of their probabilities, is positive, his act is not culpable (even if, from the God's-eye objective perspective, it will turn out to be impermissible). The key, I believe, to solving the problem raised by Smith is excluding all benefits derived from deontological violations but not excluding the harms.

### VIII. Other Deontological Constraints?

Is the MP the only deontological constraint on the furthering of C? That is hard to say, because C itself has not been specified. I believe that most acts that we believe are impermissible and legitimately punished, at least if committed culpably, are either violations of C or violations of the MP. But I cannot rule out the possibility that there are some acts that are impermissible in this strong sense but that violate neither C nor the MP.

First, let me explain why most acts that we believe are wrongful, if not violations of the MP, are violations of C. If I knowingly drive dangerously and injure or kill someone, or damage her property, I will not have violated the MP. I would have happily driven the same way had she not existed. The impermissibility of my act must come from its setting back C. For if it furthered C despite the harm to the victim and did not use the victim, it is hard to see wherein its impermissibility lies. Of course, if I did not believe my act was furthering C but believed instead it was setting back C, then my act was culpable. But if it furthered C, then even if culpable, it was permissible. So again, most acts we believe are wrongful will be so either because they violate the MP or C.

Perhaps some acts of deception, for example, could change the deceived's behavior in ways that promote C but not by "using" the deceived in the ways the MP prohibits. I confess that I have a difficult time thinking of such a case. The MP protects people from the unconsented-to use of their bodies, labor, and talents to promote C, but it does not protect them from being prevented from performing acts that set back C, where the baseline for gauging such a setback does not rely on the use of the person's body, labor, or talent. If deceiving someone does not violate the MP and is a means of preventing him from setting back C, then the deception is C-permitted and not MP-proscribed. If, on the other hand, the deception furthers C by getting the deceived to use his body, labor, and talent to further C, then it is MP-prohibited if the deception vitiates the deceived's consent. So the question is, are there acts of deception that promote C but do not violate the MP? If not, then C and the MP together handle all cases of deception.<sup>30</sup>

What, then, about killings or other uses of force that promote C but do not violate the MP because they do not promote C by using the victim's body, labor, or talents? This might be thought possible. Suppose C is maximizing utilitarianism. And suppose that if Al kills Barb, everyone else will gain more utility than will be lost by Barb's death. Killing Barb does not use her as a means. Rather, it eliminates her as a source of utility and disutility.

Utilitarianism of either the total or average variety would thus have space for a deontological constraint on killing to promote C in addition to the constraint provided by the MP. However, in a moral realm that recognizes deontological constraints such as the MP, utilitarianism seems a very unlikely candidate for C. Rather, deontological constraints fit more easily with a more egalitarian or prioritarian form of C. And an "eliminative" killing would probably be a violation of C because it is inconsistent with the impartial concern that is at the core of such forms of C.<sup>31</sup> Therefore, C and the MP may together exhaust strong (enforceable) morality.

### IX. Conclusion

Michael Moore has taken many heterodox positions in his writings, but his position that deontological constraints are agent-relative and intention-focused is quite an orthodox one. Most of the explication of the Doctrine of Double Effect makes the intention of the actor central to the doctrine, and Moore is right in step with this.

I believe, however, that Moore and the orthodoxy are mistaken. The MP that I hold to be the quintessential deontological constraint on the pursuit of C demotes the role of the actor's intention. Facts, not the actor's intentions, determine his act's permissibility. And the actor's beliefs, not his intentions, are the determinants of whether he is culpable in acting. Intentions may reflect our character but not the moral status or culpability of our acts.<sup>32</sup>

## 4. Omissions and Holdings

The view covers omissions as well as acts. Jonathan Parry, adapting a case of Tadros's, describes a man who slips on ice and slides toward a trolley that will otherwise paralyse five people (Parry 2023, [p.]; Tadros 2011, [p. 122]). His body would slow the trolley enough to save the five, and he would be paralysed. A passer-by could easily stop him, and refrains so that his body will stop the trolley. That seems wrong, though it is permissible to let a man be hit when two trolleys threaten him and five others and she can stop only one. Quong can separate the cases only by giving the sliding man a prior right to be rescued; Parry argues that the five's claims would extinguish such a right, and concludes that the principle must help determine rights rather than merely follow them (Parry 2023, [p.]).

The Spending View explains the case directly. Letting the man suffer the loss so that his body serves settles, for him, whether it serves at that cost, just as imposing the loss would. Were the stopping supplied by something else, she could pull him back and the five would still be saved, so his paralysis is the cost of what his body supplies. It is the hospital's rule by omission: no one asks him, and his body serves the five at his cost all the same. So premise 4 covers omissions, which an account on which the wrong is compelling someone to serve cannot do, since failing to aid compels no one (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.], against Tadros). It follows that the good his body would supply cannot count against rescuing him, and the ordinary duty of easy rescue stands. In the two-trolley case nothing of the man's is supplied, and the numbers decide. Alexander's view has this structure: rescuing such a man is not wrong though it saves fewer, since it sets back the overall good "only by saving someone from being an involuntary means" of promoting it (Alexander 2016, [p.]).[^10]

The view extends to holdings without strain. Quong presses a pair of cases against Ramakrishnan: a swimmer who reaches the only piece of driftwood first, and one who steals another's life jacket (Quong 2020, [p.]). The first seems permissible and the second not, and a principle about persons alone cannot say why. The Spending View can. Taking the life jacket spends its owner, since another jacket would spare her. Winning the race to the driftwood spends no one, since nothing of the loser's supplies anything.

The view also avoids the costs Parry finds in Quong's extension of the principle to property. On Quong's view, turning a trolley toward a man with his own wrench uses him, so that when fifteen people are at risk, the only permissible rescue may be to turn the trolley onto three others instead (Parry 2023, [p.]). On the Spending View, the owner dies of the trolley, not as the cost of the wrench's service: had another tool turned the switch, he would have died all the same. Nor does acquisition impose sweeping new constraints on everyone else, as Parry fears (Parry 2023, [p.]): holdings constrain others only where the owner's loss would be the cost of their service, and the duties that bound the principle grow with holdings.[^11]

The bound does real work here. Someone who uses a stranger's vase as a hammer to free a stuck switch, and breaks it, imposes on the owner a loss that is the cost of what the vase supplies, since another hammer would have spared it. But the loss falls within what the owner owes under a duty of easy rescue, so the act does not spend her. It is permitted, and she is owed redress.

## 5. Objections

The most serious objection comes from a looping trolley. In *Loop*, a trolley is headed for five people. An agent can divert it onto a side track that loops back toward them, and on the loop stands one man whose body will stop the trolley, killing him; if the trolley went straight, he would be safe (Choo 2025, [p.]). Most people judge that diverting is permissible, and Frederick Choo argues that the judgment is robust: it survives controls for order effects, most participants accept it when no other case is shown first, and the philosophers who hold it see that the man is used, so it is no illusion of inattention (Choo 2025, [p.]). I grant all of this.

The view nonetheless forbids diverting. Relative to not diverting, the five survive only because his body stops the trolley, and a stopper placed before him would spare him. That is the footbridge case's structure. Frances Kamm tries to separate the cases: in diverting, she says, the agent removes the first threat and creates "a *new problem*", the trolley looping back, which his body merely prevents (Kamm 2007, 94–95). But the agent has a single goal, to save the five, and diverting serves it only through his body. Otsuka and Choo reject Kamm's description on this ground (Otsuka 2008, [p.]; Choo 2025, [p.]). The comparison with the agent's alternatives keeps the six-behind case permissible, since there the diversion saves the five by itself.[^12]

The view thus overrides a robust intuition, and I should say why it should prevail. Otsuka links *Loop* to a variant of the footbridge case in which a lever moves a pole that topples a man into the trolley's path, a case whose wrongness is, in his words, "a moral fixed point" for non-consequentialists (Otsuka 2008, [p.]). He goes by two routes, each through one intermediate case. In the first, the diverted trolley, as it circles back, itself trips the pole. This differs from *Loop* only in moving the man into the trolley rather than the trolley into the man, a difference Kamm herself argues is insignificant, and it is "morally indistinguishable" from the toppling case, since in both "you push a lever that causes someone to fall from the bridge" (Otsuka 2008, [p.]). In the second, opening a drawbridge sends the trolley up a ramp into a man on a footbridge overhead; it differs from the toppling case in the same way, and appears "completely morally indistinguishable" from *Loop* (Otsuka 2008, [p.]). Otsuka doubts that any principle separates these cases (Otsuka 2008, [p.] n. 25).

Choo answers Otsuka's general claim: those who judge *Loop* permissible see the use, and trust that judgment over the principles that condemn it (Choo 2025, [p.]). Neither point reaches the chain, which needs no one to miss the use and sets the verdict on *Loop* against a verdict on a case, not a principle. So one set of firm judgments must yield, and the principle that explains the wider set, and has an independent rationale, forbids diverting. I concede the cost; this is the one case where the view overrides a robust intuition. Others reach the same verdict: Otsuka, Alexander, Walen, Liao, McMahan and, on reflection, Ramakrishnan (Alexander 2016, n. 8; Walen 2016, [p.]; Liao et al. 2012, [p.] n. 3; Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.] n. 36).[^13]

A second objection is that the view permits too much, since it seems to allow driving over a man who lies across the only road to people in need of rescue. The view is silent on that case, not permissive. He supplies nothing; what the driver needs is his absence. Other constraints forbid running him over. Quinn condemns it under his account of doing and allowing, since the driver intends an action of the car that will kill the man (see Liao and Barry 2020, [p.]), and Walen now defends a claim to the space one occupies (Walen 2022, [p.]).

A third objection concerns motive. Parry describes a rescuer who is willing to lose an arm to stop a sliding man, sees five people further down the track, and refrains so that the man's body will stop the trolley (Parry 2023, [p.]). Refusing a rescue that would cost an arm is normally permitted, yet this refusal seems wrong.

The view can explain the case if one assumption holds. Suppose the permission not to give is a standing to favour oneself that is used in choosing, as premise 3's talk of the question being hers to settle suggests. Then, where conduct would otherwise spend someone, the permission justifies it only if the agent acts on it. The rescuer refuses for what the man's body will supply, not to spare her arm, so her permission does not justify the refusal; nor can the five's rescue, since the good his body supplies is disabled. Agent-neutral justifications justify whatever the motive, so motive matters only here. The view therefore disagrees with Liao and Barry about an enemy who turns a trolley from five people onto one only because he hates the one: he acts permissibly, if viciously (Liao and Barry 2020, [p.]).[^14]

Finally, the view may seem absolutist. It is not. Duties grow with the stakes, and the principle yields where duty yields: if a person can owe her life to save a million, taking it does not spend her. Whether she can is a question about the content of duty that the view inherits and does not settle.

## 6. Conclusion

I have argued that the means principle forbids spending persons, not using them. A replacement test, with supply judged against the agent's alternatives, sorts the opening pair, the canonical pairs, and the cases that defeat tests by absence. It gives Quinn's idea a precise form, and it repairs the rationale his successors offered, which fails when aimed at use and succeeds when aimed at spending. It extends to omissions and holdings, and it forbids diverting in *Loop*, at a cost I have stated.

The view is not the whole of the ethics of harm. It is silent about people who are merely in the way, and it takes duties of rescue as given. Within its scope, it says what the means principle protects. What separates *Smoke* from *Wedge* is not what the firefighter does with the man's body, but whether his death is the price of what it gives.

## 1. The most serious problem, stated plainly

**P2 is false as written, so the displayed argument is unsound (clear gap).** P2 says that to treat something as a resource for others is to treat the question "whether a good it can supply them is worth what supplying it costs it" as a question for others to settle. Using a sleeping stranger as a pillow is a paradigm of treating her as a resource, yet it costs her nothing. So there is no cost to weigh, and P2 does not count it.

The essay already scopes harmless use out (¶1.5), but the premise does not.

**Fix:** restrict P1 and P2 to treatment *at the person's expense*:
- P1: "The Means Principle exists to keep morality from treating persons as resources for others at their own expense."
- P2: "To treat something as a resource for others at its expense is to treat the question whether ... as a question for others to settle."

The restricted P1 stays close to common ground. Quong's version is a principle about *harming* (file 09 L119), and harmless uses fall under other rights: Ramakrishnan's principle concerns rights infringements in general (file 01 L15), but the view does not claim that territory. With the restriction the argument keeps its validity and loses the counterexample.

## 2. Reconstruction (strongest form the outline supports)

- (P1) The Means Principle exists to keep morality from treating persons as resources for others at their own expense.
- (P2) To treat something as a resource for others at its expense is to treat the question whether a good it can supply them is worth what supplying it costs it as a question for others to settle.
- (P3) Beyond what a person owes, that question is hers to settle, and she settles it by agreeing or refusing. Within what she owes, her duty has already settled it.
- (P4) Others settle it for her exactly when they impose on her, or let her suffer, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good.
- (C) So the principle should hold that the good a person's contribution would supply cannot justify imposing on her, or letting her suffer, the loss that supplying it would cost her, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear. That is, it should forbid spending her.

**Suppressed premises the outline needs:**
- **S1 (function to content).** The best version of a principle is the one that does its job, no more and no less. It is implicit in ¶3.1. Fix: state it in one clause there. It is also the brief's own standard.
- **S2 ("for the sake of" is objective).** "For the sake of that good" means "where that good is what would justify the loss", not the agent's motive. Without this, P4 and C equivocate with §5(c). Fix: define it in ¶2.2 (F5).
- **S3 ("settle" as act, not attitude).** P2 speaks of *treating* the question as others', P4 of *settling* it. Read objectively, to settle it is to act on a justification that presupposes the question is theirs. Checked: no change needed once S2 is stated.

**Mismatch between the displays (F6).** Display 1 is a disabler ("cannot justify"); the old C was a prohibition ("forbid ... for the sake of that good"). Under S2 they are equivalent. The C above is reworded to match Display 1 exactly, so the essay shows one view, not two.

## 3. Validity

With S1–S3 explicit, C follows:
1. From P1 and S1, the principle should forbid exactly treatment of persons as resources at their expense.
2. P2 says what that treatment is.
3. P3 says when the question is hers.
4. P4 says when others settle it.

The bound in C comes from P3's second clause. **Checked.**

## 4. Premise by premise

- **P1.** Common ground (Ramakrishnan L215; Alexander L79; Quinn's Kantian ideal). The only risk is the harmless-use problem, fixed by "at their own expense" (§1). **Fixed.**
- **P2.** The log and the tree illustrate it. One risk is that the felled tree might be taken for timber, which would make it a resource. The illustration must stipulate that it is left where it falls. **Minor wording at draft.**
- **P3.** Inherited. The load-bearing step is non-hollowness: a permission to refuse that others could override by taking would protect nothing.
  - **Referee will press:** the hybrid (Schefflerian) reply that the permission protects only her freedom from having to *do* the giving.
  - The outline states the step but not the permission's *point*, which is what makes the Schefflerian reply fail. Fix (F7): one sentence in ¶3.2, using inherited sources.
    - The point of the permission is that a person is "a free agent leading her own independent life" (Walen 2016, file 14 L301), with "a sphere of independence where her decisions are sovereign" (Quong, file 09 L135).
    - A permission that others could override by taking would leave her life and body at their disposal.
    - Credit Mack for the argument.
- **P4, "if".** Spending settles the question for her: she bears the cost of what she supplies, and the supply is counted as worth it by others. **Checked.**
- **P4, "only if".** Candidate counterexamples, all checked:
  - harmless use: no cost, so outside P2 once it is restricted;
  - coercion into giving: this is spending too, since her loss is the cost of her supply imposed through her own agency;
  - a person merely in the way: nothing supplied, so outside, and scoped out;
  - letting die where there is no duty to aid: her loss is not the cost of supply, since no one would have borne the cost of aiding her anyway.

  None survives. The deep challenge to "exactly" is the steelman in §6.
- **The replacement test.** Stress cases, each varying one feature, all checked (full list in §7):
  - two bodies, either of which would stop the trolley;
  - two bodies that stop it only together;
  - the agent herself as the only other source;
  - deterrence, where the good comes from the loss itself (terror bombing);
  - a stopper placed onto the person, where he is in the way, not a supplier.

  Each gives the verdict the view should give.
- **The comparison with alternatives, respect by respect.** Stress cases, all checked:
  - six behind the man who would *also* die if the trolley went straight: their survival is disabled, the five's is not, so diverting is permitted, like the diversion case;
  - two behind the man;
  - a loop in which the returning trolley would reach only one of the five.

  In the last case only the supplied part of the good is disabled: four lives still justify diverting. This is a precise result worth a footnote in §5 (F11).

## 6. The strongest objection, and the reply

**Steelman.**
- "Whether something of hers 'serves' is fixed by the replacement test, and the replacement test is causal structure. So the rationale says the permission protects her against losses standing in a certain causal relation to the good. That is Ramakrishnan's arbitrary causal structure in the language of giving.
- From her point of view there is no difference. The side-effect victim also loses her life, without consent, for others' good.
- Why should the permission track the structure, rather than the size of the loss and her lack of consent?"

This is the objection a hostile specialist raises first, since it goes to the essay's crux. Steinhoff's challenge and Ramakrishnan's worry are two faces of it.

**Candidate reply** (to be sharpened in ¶3.5 and ¶3.9: F4):
1. The permission not to give is a limit on others' claims to what she has and is. Beyond what she owes, they have no claim to her body's service or her holdings.
2. When she is spent, others act as if they had such a claim: they take what her body supplies at its cost to her.
   When she is harmed as a side effect, they claim nothing of hers. They pursue their ends by means that are theirs to use, and the only question is whether the harm to her is outweighed: the ordinary question about harming, weighed like any other.
3. So the two victims have different complaints. The side-effect victim complains of being harmed, and that complaint is weighed. The spent victim complains of being *taken from*, and the value of what was taken cannot answer that complaint (beyond duty).
4. The causal structure matters because it fixes which complaint she has. Giving is itself a structural notion: to give is to be the source of what another receives. A moral notion with a causal condition is not arbitrary on that account.

**Status:** this defeats the objection rather than conceding it, but it must be *in the text*, compressed to two sentences in ¶3.5 and one in ¶3.9.

## 8. Other findings

- **F2 (clear gap; accuracy and fairness).** ¶1.3 says "the criteria lack a reason". That is false of Quong, who grounds his criterion in independence under distributive justice (file 09 L129–139), and of Walen. Fix: the best criterion (Ramakrishnan's) has no reason behind it (L237), and the reason Quong gives attaches to *use*, which the opening pair shows is the wrong target.
- **F3 (clear gap; §5(c)).** "A permission is exercised only by acting on it" is overstated as a general claim. A spiteful donor who declines to give to charity acts permissibly despite her motive. Fix, narrowing:
  - The claim applies only where conduct would otherwise be spending.
  - There, an agent-relative permission justifies only if it is acted on, because it is a standing to favour oneself, used in choosing.
  - Agent-neutral justifications justify whatever the motive.
  - State it conditionally ("if permissions are powers exercised in choosing, as P3's 'hers to settle' suggests"), and footnote the spiteful case.

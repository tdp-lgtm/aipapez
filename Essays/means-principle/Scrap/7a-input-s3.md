## 3. Why Spending Matters

The replacement test sorts the cases. But why should the means principle track it? Ramakrishnan doubts that such a principle has a deeper explanation, and Uwe Steinhoff doubts that any rationale for it succeeds (Steinhoff 2019). If a principle's best version is the one that does its job, no more and no less, the question is what the means principle's job is. My answer is the following argument.

> **The argument.**
>
> (P1) The means principle exists to keep morality from treating persons as resources for others at their own expense.
>
> (P2) To treat something as a resource for others at its expense is to treat the question whether a good it can supply them is worth what supplying it costs it as a question for others to settle.
>
> (P3) Beyond what a person owes, that question is hers to settle, and she settles it by agreeing or refusing; within what she owes, her duty has already settled it.
>
> (P4) Others settle it for her exactly when they impose on her, or let her suffer, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good.
>
> (C) So the principle should hold that the good that a person's contribution would supply to others cannot justify imposing on her, or letting her suffer, the loss that supplying it would cost her, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear. That is, it should forbid spending her.

Premise 1 is common ground: Alexander states the principle's "core idea" as that "we are not resources for others' benefit without our consent" (Alexander 2016, [p.]). Premise 2 is the analysis given with the log and the tree. Premise 3 is inherited, and I defend it below. Premise 4 is where the view does its work: section 2 supplied the test, and the rest of this section argues that only spending settles the question for her.

Premise 3 rests on a permission that nearly every non-consequentialist accepts, an agent-relative prerogative in the usual terms: no one need give a kidney, or her life, to others, even when they would gain more than she loses. Premise 3 adds that this permission not to give is more than a permission to refuse. Imagine a rule that let a hospital take a kidney from anyone whose kidney would save a life, while leaving everyone free to refuse to donate. No one would say that the rule respects the permission: every refusal stands, and the kidney is taken anyway.[^8] The permission's point explains why. As Walen puts it, a person is "a free agent leading her own independent life", whom morality "cannot treat ... as though she may be pressed into duty to serve others at great expense to herself" (Walen 2016, [p.]). Whether her body serves others at a cost to her is part of that life, and a permission that others could override by taking would leave the question at their disposal, settling only who does the taking. Within what she owes, her duty has already settled it; that is Quinn's exception for strong moral obligations. Mack and Alexander pair the permission with the principle in the same way (Mack 2005, [p.]; Alexander 2016, [p.]).[^9]

Quong argues that no rationale of this kind can work (Quong 2020, [p.]). Read as the claim that "if Y is not duty bound to bear some cost, C, for the sake of goal, G, then it is wrong to impose C on Y for the sake of G", the rationale condemns the diversion case, since the one on the side track is not duty bound to die for the five. Read as Tadros's claim that the wrong is to "compel [a victim] to act for the sake of some end that she is permitted to reject for herself", it faces a dilemma. If compelling means imposing unowed costs, this is the first reading again. If it means using her body or property without her consent or duty, the explanation "amounts to saying: harmful using is distinctively wrong because it involves harmful using".

Both readings assume that the permission not to give limits what a person must bear. It does not. It limits what she must supply and what she must do. The diversion case imposes a cost on the one, but no cost of supply, so the first reading is not the rationale I have given. Nor is my explanation circular, since the replacement test speaks of sources, goods and costs, never of use; this also meets Liao and Barry's charge that such rationales end up sounding "very much like the MP" (Liao and Barry 2020, [p.]). Quong's own rationale has the right structure, since rights over a fair share of the world exist "to block" usefulness "from serving as a reason" (Quong 2020, [p.]). But its object is too wide. What should be blocked, as *Smoke* shows, is the good a person's contribution supplies, as a reason to impose on her the cost of supplying it.

Premise 4 says that others settle the question for her exactly when they spend her. Why only then? The permission protects two things: what she must do, and what of hers must serve. Others can override the first only by making her act, as coercion does; they override the second exactly by spending her, as the hospital's rule does. A side-effect harm does neither. Its loss is not the cost of anything of hers serving: either nothing of hers serves, as in the diversion case, or the loss would fall on her all the same were what serves supplied from elsewhere, as the smoke falls in *Smoke*. So it falls under the ordinary constraint against harming, which weighs harms against goods. Why not all harms? Because, in Mack's terms, the permission lets each person "devote *herself*" to her own ends, and the matching restriction forbids others to devote her "energy, attention, bodily parts, and so on" to theirs (Mack 2005, [p.]); a side-effect harm devotes nothing of anyone. The complaints differ accordingly. The person spent is taken from, and the value of what was taken cannot answer that complaint; the person harmed as a side effect is harmed, and that complaint is weighed. This also answers Steinhoff's question whether using a person's body is worse than discarding it (Steinhoff 2019, [p.]): it is, when the use is at the cost of what she supplies. His own alternative, a right to decide "in pursuit of which ends her body is to suffer adverse side effects" (Steinhoff 2019, [p.]), would make the diversion case as hard to justify as the footbridge case.

The distinction answers Judith Jarvis Thomson's argument against turning the trolley. Her bystander could turn the trolley onto himself, onto one workman, or not at all. If he turns it onto the workman because he would rather not die, he makes "the one workman pay the cost of his good deed because he doesn't feel like paying it himself", which is worse than stealing money for Oxfam (Thomson 2008, 364). Since an ordinary bystander would not turn the trolley onto himself, she concludes, he may not turn it onto the one (Thomson 2008, 365–366). The analogy fails. The thief makes another's money serve his good deed; turning the trolley makes nothing of the workman's serve. The argument slides from serving to doing: others may turn a trolley onto her that she need not turn onto herself (compare Tadros 2011, [p.]; Cullity 2018, [p.] n. 45).

A last worry is Ramakrishnan's. A principle of this kind makes permissibility turn on a person's "location in causal space", which strikes him as "a terribly arbitrary feature of his situation" (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). It does so in a fine-grained way: his principle lets a person's position prevent "a would-be defeater" of a justification, as in the case of twenty behind, and does not forbid harming someone who is a necessary means when medicine is short (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). The view explains the grain. What matters is whether she is made to give, and the test for being made to give has this shape: the comparison with alternatives removes the case of twenty behind, and replacement removes the cases of shortage. Giving is a structural notion: to give is to be the source of what another receives. A moral notion with a causal condition is not arbitrary on that account. Whether you have stolen from me depends on where what you have came from, and theft is no arbitrary wrong.

## 4. Omissions and Holdings

The view covers omissions as well as acts. Jonathan Parry, after Tadros, describes a man who slides on ice into the path of a trolley that will otherwise paralyse five people (Parry 2023, [p.]; Tadros 2011, [p. 122]). His body would slow the trolley, saving the five, but paralysing him. A passer-by could easily stop him, but she refrains so his body will stop the trolley. That refusal seems wrong. Compare two trolleys, one toward the man, one toward the five, where a passer-by can stop only one; letting the man be hit is permissible. Quong's principle permits a failure to save a victim "unless the victim has a right to be rescued or provided with resources" (Quong 2020, [p.]). Parry argues that the sliding man has no such right, because the five's claims extinguish it (Parry 2023, [p.]). Put the two together: on Quong's principle, nothing condemns the passer-by's refusal, since there is no right for it to violate, yet it is wrong. What is needed is a principle on which the five's claims do not count against rescuing him.

The Spending View is such a principle. By letting the man suffer the paralysis so his body serves, the passer-by settles for him whether serving is worth that cost, as imposing the loss would. If something else supplied the stopping, she could pull him back and the five would still be saved, so his paralysis is the cost of what his body supplies. This is the hospital's rule from section 3, applied to an omission: no one asks him, and his body serves the five at his cost. So premise 4 covers omissions. An account that locates the wrong in compulsion cannot cover omissions, since failing to aid compels no one (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.], against Tadros). The good his body would supply cannot count against rescuing him, and the ordinary duty of easy rescue stands. In the two-trolley case, nothing of the man's is supplied, and the numbers decide. Alexander's view has this structure: rescuing such a man is not wrong, though it saves fewer, since it sets back the overall good "only by saving someone from being an involuntary means" of promoting it (Alexander 2016, [p.]).[^8]

The view extends to holdings too. Quong presses two cases against Ramakrishnan: a swimmer who reaches the only driftwood first, and one who steals another's life jacket (Quong 2020, [p.]). The first seems permissible, the second not, and a principle about persons alone cannot say why. The Spending View can: taking the life jacket spends its owner, since another jacket would spare her, while winning the race to the driftwood spends no one, since nothing of the loser's supplies anything.

The view avoids Parry's difficulties with Quong's extension of the principle to property. On Quong's view, using a man's wrench to work the switch turning a trolley toward him counts as using him. So if fifteen people can be saved by turning the trolley toward him with his wrench, or toward three others with an unowned branch, Quong must require the second, killing three instead of one (Parry 2023, [p.]). On the Spending View, the owner dies of the trolley, not as the cost of the wrench's service, since he would have died had another tool turned the switch.

## 5. Objections

The most serious objection comes from a looping trolley. In *Loop*, a trolley is headed for five people. An agent can divert it onto a side track looping back toward them. One man stands on the loop, and his body will stop the trolley, killing him; if the trolley went straight, he would be safe (Choo 2025, [p.]). Most people judge diverting permissible, and Frederick Choo argues the judgment is robust. It survives controls for order effects, holding even when shown first. The philosophers who hold it see the man is used, so the judgment is no illusion of inattention (Choo 2025, [p.]). I grant all of this.

The view nonetheless forbids diverting. Relative to not diverting, the five survive only because his body stops the trolley, and a stopper before him would spare him. That is the footbridge case's structure. Frances Kamm tries to separate the cases: in diverting, the agent removes the first threat and creates "a *new problem*", the trolley looping back, which his body merely prevents (Kamm 2007, 94–95). But the agent has a single goal, to save the five, and diverting serves it only through his body. Otsuka and Choo reject Kamm's description on this ground (Otsuka 2008, [p.]; Choo 2025, [p.]). Judging supply against the agent's alternatives keeps diverting permissible when people stand behind the man, since there the diversion saves the five by itself.[^9]

The view overrides a robust intuition, so I must say why it should prevail. Otsuka's argument runs through a chain of cases, each differing from the last in one respect that seems morally irrelevant. It starts from a case that most people condemn. In the toppling case, pushing a lever moves a pole that topples a man from a bridge into the path of a trolley bound for five, and his body stops the trolley, killing him. That this is wrong is, in Otsuka's words, "a moral fixed point" for non-consequentialists (Otsuka 2008, [p.]).

Now change how the lever works. Pushing it diverts the trolley onto a loop, and as the trolley circles, it triggers the pole, which topples the man into its path. Otsuka calls this case "morally indistinguishable" from the toppling case, since in both "you push a lever that causes someone to fall from the bridge" (Otsuka 2008, [p.]). Now change one more thing: the man stands on the loop, so the diverted trolley runs into him rather than he into it. The result is *Loop*, and what has changed is only whether the man is moved into the trolley or the trolley into the man. Kamm herself has argued, of a similar case, that this makes no moral difference (Otsuka 2008, [p.]). Otsuka doubts that anyone can separate these cases (Otsuka 2008, [p.] n. 25).

Choo answers Otsuka's general claim: those who judge *Loop* permissible see the use, and trust that judgment over the principles that condemn it (Choo 2025, [p.]). Neither of Choo's points answers the chain. The chain needs no one to miss the use, and it sets the verdict on *Loop* against a verdict on another case, not against a principle. So one set of firm judgments must yield. Keeping the verdict on *Loop* while condemning the toppling case would need a principled difference; none has been found. It means giving up the verdict on the toppling case or the principle that explains it, the footbridge case, and the other pairs of section 2. Giving up the verdict on *Loop* costs less, all the more since the principle that forbids diverting has an independent rationale (section 3). I concede the cost; this is the one case where the view overrides a robust intuition, though other philosophers accept the same verdict.[^10]

A second objection is that the view permits driving over a man lying across the only road to people needing rescue. The view is silent on that case, not permissive. He supplies nothing; what the driver needs is his absence. Other constraints forbid running him over: Quinn's account of doing and allowing condemns it, since the driver intends the car's lethal action (see Liao and Barry 2020, [p.]), and Walen now defends a claim to the space one occupies (Walen 2022, [p.]).

A third objection concerns motive. Parry describes a rescuer willing to lose an arm to stop a sliding man. She sees five people further down the track and refrains, so his body will stop the trolley. Her refusal seems wrong, yet a refusal made only to keep her arm would be permissible, and the two are the same conduct (Parry 2023, [p.]).

The view can tell the refusals apart. Premise 3 explains why. It gives her the standing to settle, by agreeing or refusing, whether a good she can supply is worth its cost to her. So what the permission not to give protects is her answer to that question, and it justifies an act only when the act is itself that answer. The rescuer had answered it in favour of giving, since she was willing to lose her arm; she refuses for what the man's body will supply. So her permission does not justify the refusal, and nor can the five's rescue, since the good his body supplies is disabled. Had she refused to keep her arm, her permission would have justified the refusal. A prerogative points the same way: it permits her to give her interests extra weight, and weight she withholds cannot justify what she does. Agent-neutral justifications succeed whatever the motive, so motive matters only where conduct would otherwise spend someone. The view disagrees with Liao and Barry about an enemy who turns a trolley from five onto one only because he hates the one: on the view, he acts permissibly, though his motive is vicious (Liao and Barry 2020, [p.]).[^11]

Finally, the view may seem absolutist, but it is not. Duties grow with the stakes, and the principle yields where duty yields. If a person can owe her life to save a million, taking it does not spend her. Whether she can is a question about duty's content, which the view inherits and does not settle.

# Spending Persons

**Abstract.** [To be written last, about 150 words.]

## 1. Introduction

Compare two cases, both set in a burning house.

*Smoke.* Five people are trapped in an attic that is filling with smoke. The only way out is a trapdoor that falls shut unless something holds it open. An unconscious neighbour lies directly beneath it. His body is the only thing within reach to prop the trapdoor against, and propping it against him does him no harm. But once the trapdoor opens, the smoke will pour down onto him, and there is no time to move him. A firefighter props the trapdoor against him. The five climb down, and he dies of the smoke.

*Wedge.* Everything is the same, except that a roof vent draws the smoke away, so none will reach him, and the trapdoor is too heavy to prop. It stays open only if something is wedged beneath it, and the only thing there is his body, which the trapdoor's weight will crush. The firefighter wedges him beneath it. The five climb down, and he dies.

It seems permissible to prop the trapdoor in *Smoke* and wrong to wedge it in *Wedge*. Yet the difference is hard to locate. In both cases the firefighter uses his body to hold the trapdoor open, and in both she kills him rather than letting him die; five are saved either way. So the difference lies neither in using him, nor in harming him, nor in killing him. Nor does it lie in what she intends. In *Wedge* she need intend only that his body hold the trapdoor; she would be glad if he survived its weight.[^1]

The verdict in *Smoke* also has support that owes nothing to theory. Without the prop, *Smoke* would be a familiar diversion, like turning a runaway trolley from five people onto one, which most people judge permissible (Otsuka 2008, [p.]). And the prop changes nothing for him. Had a beam been within reach, she would have used it, and he would have died of the smoke regardless. A use that makes no difference to what happens to him cannot turn a permissible killing into a wrongful one.

Not everyone accepts this verdict. Jonathan Quong's version of the means principle forbids harming someone when doing so "involves using Y's body or other things over which Y has a rightful prior claim", absent duty or consent (Quong 2020, [p.]). He counts as a violation a case with the structure of *Smoke*: a woman on a side track is killed by a diverted trolley whose switch works only while her body presses a button (Quong 2020, [p.] n. 27). I will argue that he need not.

The principle's core idea is Warren Quinn's. His version of the doctrine of double effect rests on "the strong moral presumption that those who can be usefully involved in the promotion of a goal only at the cost of something protected by their independent moral rights ... ought, prima facie, to serve the goal only voluntarily", unless they "have or would have strong moral obligations" to serve (Quinn 1989, [p.]). Later work has developed the idea in two separate ways. Some have sought a criterion for when a person is used. Quong asks whether the agent's act "could succeed in the absence of Y and Y's rightful property" (Quong 2020, [p.]); Ketan Ramakrishnan asks whether a person will be useful to others only if her rights are infringed (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). Others have sought a rationale: persons may not be made to serve ends beyond what they owe. Neither line is complete on its own. Ramakrishnan takes his criterion to be primitive. Quong's rationale for his criterion attaches to use, which *Smoke* shows to be the wrong target. The rationale others have sought, for its part, comes with no criterion (section 3).

I argue that the means principle forbids spending persons, not using them. To spend a person is to impose on her, or let her suffer, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear. Whether a loss is such a cost can be tested by replacement: ask whether the good could be had without the loss if what she contributes came from some other source. In *Wedge*, a beam wedged under the trapdoor would spare the man, so his death is the cost of what his body supplies; in *Smoke*, a beam would not. And what a person supplies is judged against the agent's alternatives, not against her absence. The view breaks with Quong's, which condemns harmful use as such, and goes beyond Ramakrishnan's, which it matches for harms to persons. The idea is Quinn's, and the rationale is his successors' (section 3). My contribution is the test, which makes the idea precise, together with an argument that it is the right test and an account of what follows from it.

The principle is one constraint among several, silent about people merely in the way. I take duties of rescue as given, offer no theory of property, and set aside harmless uses, since the principle concerns losses. Section 2 states the view and its test, section 3 defends it as the right version of the principle, section 4 extends it to omissions and holdings, and section 5 answers objections, chiefly one from looping trolley cases.

## 2. Using and Spending

The view I defend says that the means principle forbids spending persons. The difference between a resource and a casualty shows what spending is. A log burned for heat is a resource: those who burn it decide whether its heat is worth its being burned. A tree felled because it stands where a road will go, and left where it falls, is a casualty: nothing it supplies is weighed, because it supplies nothing. The log is a resource because others decide whether its service is worth what that service costs it. That is what persons are said not to be. Quinn's ideal is that each person be treated "as existing only for purposes that he can share" (Quinn 1989, [p.]), and Quong gives each person "a sphere of independence where her decisions are sovereign" (Quong 2020, [p.]).

Stated in full, the view is this:

> **The Spending View.** The good that a person's contribution would supply to others cannot justify imposing on her, or letting her suffer, the loss that supplying it would cost her, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear.

A person's contribution is whatever of hers serves others: her body, its parts and position, her time and labour, her holdings. A loss is a setback she would not suffer if the agent acted otherwise. To spend her is to impose such a loss on her, or let her suffer it, for the sake of the good her contribution supplies, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear.[^2] "For the sake of" concerns justification, not motive: the good is what would be offered to justify the loss.

The view needs a test for when a loss is the cost of supplying a good. I propose a replacement test: her loss is the cost of her supplying a good when the good could be had without that loss if her contribution came from some other source. Take the footbridge case, in which the only way to stop a trolley bound for five people is to push a large man from a bridge into its path. If something else, such as a heavy weight, supplied the stopping, the five could be saved and he could live. So his death is the cost of what his body supplies, and pushing him spends him. The test varies what the person contributes, never the person: she stays where she is, and only the source of her contribution changes.[^3]

The familiar tests vary the person instead. Quinn asked whether an agent would still need his victims' cooperation if they had shelters or medicines of their own (Quinn 1989, [p.]). His successors, Quong among them, ask what the agent could achieve in the victim's absence (see Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.] n. 21). Absence tests fail, as Ramakrishnan himself shows. In one of his cases, a doctor can devise a cure for five people with a deadly disease by running harmless tests on a woman. She has a heart attack, and he leaves it untreated so that the tests can run (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). She also happens to lie on a pill that would cure the five. So the five could be saved in her absence, and an absence test finds nothing amiss. Yet leaving her to die seems plainly wrong. The replacement test condemns it, since the doctor could save the five and treat her if the information her body yields came from another source. The replacement test is counterfactual too, but it never removes her, so it never has to say what would fill the place she leaves empty.[^4]

The view has a second feature, as important as the first: judging supply against the agent's alternatives. What a person supplies is measured against what the agent could otherwise do, not against her absence. The reason is that the view concerns justification. A justification is a reason for one option rather than another. So her contribution supplies a good only if it makes the act better for others than an alternative.[^5] Take the diversion case, and suppose six people stand behind the man on the side track, so that his body shields them from the diverted trolley. If the agent does not divert, the trolley never reaches the side track, so the six are safe either way; their safety is therefore no respect in which diverting is better. What makes diverting better is the rescue of the five, and nothing of his supplies that. Diverting is permissible. When the side track loops back to the five, by contrast, the whole improvement comes from his body (section 5). Michael Otsuka explains this contrast by intention, and Ramakrishnan discusses a similar case (Otsuka 2008, [p.]; Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]).

Judging supply against the agent's alternatives also corrects Larry Alexander, who counts only benefits "excluding those causally derived from usings" (Alexander 2016, [p.]). The six's escape comes from the man's body, so Alexander leaves it out. He then reckons the diversion as if the trolley would run on into the six, and so forbids it (Alexander 2016, n. 8 (verify)). That measures the diversion against the man's absence, which the agent cannot choose.

Together the two features sort the canonical pairs. The footbridge case is spending. The diversion case is not, since nothing of the one supplies the five's rescue. Ramakrishnan's own pair has the shape of the opening pair, but concerns letting die. A man's legs hold open the door of a room where five people are dying of a virus, and a doctor gives his scarce medicine to the five rather than to the man (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). If something else held the door, the man would still go without, since there is not enough medicine for him and the five, so the doctor acts permissibly. The patient left untreated so that the tests can run, by contrast, is spent. Using a person is thus neither necessary nor sufficient for spending her: the man at the door, like the man in *Smoke*, is used without being spent, and the patient is spent though the tests on her are harmless.

Absence tests also err in the other direction, forbidding what is permissible, as a case from S. Matthew Liao and Christian Barry shows (Liao and Barry 2020, [p.]). A woman defending herself and nineteen others with a flamethrower kills their attacker and a bystander standing behind him. The bystander happens to stand in front of a door. Had he not been there, fifty people would have come through it, and her flames would have killed them too. Measured against his absence, then, his presence costs her nothing, since without him she could not permissibly fire at all. So a view that weakens a bystander's claim only when his presence costs the defender something gives his claim full weight here, and forbids her act. Yet the act seems permissible. Judging supply against the agent's alternatives gets it right: the bystander supplies nothing relative to her alternative, since the fifty are safe if she does not fire.

A reader may suspect that the view is Ramakrishnan's principle renamed. His principle says that a justification for infringing a person's claim C is "substantially weaker" than it otherwise would be when it rests on the fact that she "will be useful to others ... only if C is infringed" (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). For harms to persons the two views agree closely. They differ in four ways. First, the view gives a reason for judging supply against the agent's alternatives, and that reason yields his verdict when people stand behind the man on the side track (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). Second, it is explained by a permission almost everyone accepts (section 3), where he takes his principle as primitive. Third, it covers holdings, which Quong presses against him (section 4). Fourth, where his principle discounts the justification, the view disables it: up to a bound set by duty, the good she supplies cannot count at all.

## 3. Why Spending Matters

The replacement test sorts the cases. But why should the means principle track it? Ramakrishnan and Uwe Steinhoff both doubt that the means principle has any deeper rationale (Steinhoff 2019). The best version of a principle does its job, no more and no less; the question is what that job is. My answer is the following argument.

> **The argument.**
>
> (P1) The means principle exists to keep morality from treating persons as resources for others at their own expense.
>
> (P2) To treat something as a resource for others at its expense is to leave to those others the question whether a good it can supply them is worth what supplying that good costs it.
>
> (P3) Beyond what a person owes, that question is hers to settle, and she settles it by agreeing or refusing; within what she owes, her duty has already settled it.
>
> (P4) Others settle it for her exactly when they impose on her, or let her suffer, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good.
>
> (C) So the principle should hold that the good that a person's contribution would supply to others cannot justify imposing on her, or letting her suffer, the loss that supplying it would cost her, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear. That is, it should forbid spending her.

Premise 1 is common ground: Alexander states the principle's "core idea" as "we are not resources for others' benefit without our consent" (Alexander 2016, [p.]). Premise 2 is the analysis of the log and the tree: those who burn the log settle whether its heat is worth its being burned. Premise 3 is inherited; I defend it below. Premise 4 does the view's work: section 2 supplied the test, and this section argues that only spending settles the question for her.

Premise 3 rests on a permission nearly every non-consequentialist accepts: the agent-relative prerogative. No one need give a kidney, or her life, to others, even if they gain more than she loses. Premise 3 adds that this permission not to give is more than a permission to refuse.

Imagine a rule that leaves everyone free to refuse a kidney donation, but lets a hospital take one anyway, refused or not, from anyone whose kidney would save a life.[^6] No one would say the rule respects the permission. The permission's point explains why. As Walen puts it, a person is "a free agent leading her own independent life", whom morality "cannot treat ... as though she may be pressed into duty to serve others at great expense to herself" (Walen 2016, [p.]). Whether her body serves others at her cost is part of that life. A permission others could override by taking would leave the question for them to settle, not her. Within what she owes, her duty has already settled the question; that is Quinn's exception for strong moral obligations. Mack and Alexander pair the permission with the principle (Mack 2005, [p.]; Alexander 2016, [p.]).[^7]

Quong argues that no rationale of this kind can work (Quong 2020, [p.]). In its simplest form, the rationale says: "if Y is not duty bound to bear some cost, C, for the sake of goal, G, then it is wrong to impose C on Y for the sake of G". That condemns the diversion case, since the one on the side track is not duty bound to die for the five. Tadros's version says instead that the wrong is to "compel [a victim] to act for the sake of some end that she is permitted to reject for herself". Quong presses a dilemma against it. If compelling her means imposing on her a cost she is not duty bound to bear, Tadros's version collapses back into the simplest form and inherits its problem. If it means using her body or property without her consent or duty, the explanation is circular: it "amounts to saying: harmful using is distinctively wrong because it involves harmful using".

Both versions of the rationale assume that the permission not to give limits what a person must bear. It does not: it limits what she must give, that is, what she must do and what of hers must serve others. The diversion case imposes a cost on the one, but nothing of his serves the five, so the simplest form is not my rationale. Nor is my explanation circular. The replacement test picks out spending without mentioning use: it asks only whether the good could be had without her loss if something else supplied what she supplies. Spending is wrong because it overrides the permission not to give, which no one defines in terms of use. This meets Liao and Barry's charge that such rationales sound "very much like the MP", the means principle itself (Liao and Barry 2020, [p.]). Quong's own rationale has the right structure, since rights over a fair share of the world exist "to block" usefulness "from serving as a reason" (Quong 2020, [p.]). But its object is too wide. What should be blocked, as *Smoke* shows, is the good a person's contribution supplies, as a reason to impose on her the cost of supplying it.

Premise 4 says others settle the question for her exactly when they spend her. This is because the permission protects two things: what she must do, and what of hers must serve. Others override what she must do only by making her act, as coercion does. They override what of hers must serve exactly by spending her, as the hospital's rule does. A side-effect harm does neither. Its loss is not the cost of anything of hers serving. Either nothing of hers serves, as in the diversion case, or the loss would fall on her all the same were what serves supplied from elsewhere, as the smoke falls in *Smoke*. So it falls under the ordinary constraint against harming, which weighs harms against goods.

The permission does not protect against every harm. In Mack's terms, it lets each person "devote *herself*" to her own ends, and the matching restriction forbids others to devote her "energy, attention, bodily parts, and so on" to theirs (Mack 2005, [p.]). A side-effect harm devotes nothing of anyone. The complaints differ accordingly: the person spent is taken from, and the value of what was taken cannot answer that complaint. The person harmed as a side effect is harmed; that complaint is weighed.

The distinction answers Judith Jarvis Thomson's argument against turning the trolley. Her bystander could turn the trolley onto himself, onto one workman, or not at all. If he turns it onto the workman because he would rather not die, he makes "the one workman pay the cost of his good deed because he doesn't feel like paying it himself", which is worse than stealing money for Oxfam (Thomson 2008, 364). Since an ordinary bystander would not turn the trolley onto himself, she concludes, he may not turn it onto the one (Thomson 2008, 365–366). The analogy fails. The thief makes another's money serve his good deed, but turning the trolley does not make anything of the workman's serve the good deed. Without the analogy, what remains is an inference from what he would not do to himself to what he may not do to another, and it fails: others may turn a trolley onto her even though she need not turn it onto herself (compare Tadros 2011, [p.]; Cullity 2018, [p.] n. 45).

A last worry is Ramakrishnan's. A principle of this kind makes permissibility turn on a person's "location in causal space", which he finds "a terribly arbitrary feature of his situation" (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). It does so in a fine-grained way, as Ramakrishnan notes of his own principle. That principle lets a person's position prevent "a would-be defeater" of a justification, as when people stand behind the man on the side track. But it does not forbid letting someone die who is a necessary means to others' survival when medicine is short, as with the man whose legs hold the door (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]).

The view explains this fine grain. What matters is whether she is made to give, and the view's two features have exactly this shape. Judging supply against the agent's alternatives removes the case where people stand behind the man on the side track. The replacement test removes the cases of shortage. Giving is a structural notion: to give is to be the source of what another receives. A moral notion with a causal condition is not thereby arbitrary. Whether you have stolen from me depends on where what you have came from, and no one thinks that makes theft arbitrary.

## 4. Omissions and Holdings

The view covers omissions as well as acts. Jonathan Parry, after Tadros, describes a man who slides on ice into the path of a trolley that will otherwise paralyse five people (Parry 2023, [p.]; Tadros 2011, [p. 122]). His body would slow the trolley enough to save the five, and he would be paralysed. A passer-by could easily stop him, but she refrains so that his body will stop the trolley. That seems wrong, by contrast with a case of two trolleys, one heading for a man and one for five others, where she can stop only one: there, letting the man be hit is permissible. Quong's principle does not prohibit failures to save a victim "unless the victim has a right to be rescued or provided with resources" (Quong 2020, [p.]). Parry argues that the sliding man has no such right, because the five's claims would extinguish it (Parry 2023, [p.]). So Quong's principle cannot condemn the passer-by. What is needed is a principle on which the five's claims do not count against rescuing him.

The Spending View is such a principle. By letting the man suffer the paralysis so that his body serves, the passer-by settles for him whether serving is worth that cost, as imposing the loss would. If something else supplied the stopping, she could pull him back and the five would still be saved, so his paralysis is the cost of what his body supplies. This is the hospital's rule from section 3, applied to an omission: no one asks him, and his body serves the five at his cost. So premise 4 covers omissions. An account that locates the wrong in compulsion cannot cover omissions, since failing to aid compels no one (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.], against Tadros). The good his body would supply therefore cannot count against rescuing him, and the ordinary duty of easy rescue stands. In the two-trolley case, nothing of the man's is supplied, and the numbers decide. Alexander's view has this structure: rescuing such a man is not wrong, though it saves fewer, since it sets back the overall good "only by saving someone from being an involuntary means" of promoting it (Alexander 2016, [p.]).[^8]

The view extends to holdings too. Quong presses two cases against Ramakrishnan: a swimmer who reaches the only driftwood first, and one who steals another's life jacket (Quong 2020, [p.]). The first seems permissible and the second not, and a principle about persons alone cannot say why. The Spending View can: taking the life jacket spends its owner, since another jacket would spare her, while winning the race to the driftwood spends no one, since nothing of the loser's supplies anything.

The view also avoids the difficulties Parry finds in Quong's extension of the principle to property. On Quong's view, using a man's wrench to work the switch turning a trolley toward him counts as using him. So if fifteen people can be saved by turning the trolley toward him with his wrench, or toward three others with an unowned branch, Quong must require the second, killing three instead of one (Parry 2023, [p.]). On the Spending View, the owner dies of the trolley, not as the cost of the wrench's service, since he would have died had another tool turned the switch.

## 5. Objections

The most serious objection comes from a looping trolley. In *Loop*, a trolley is headed for five people. An agent can divert it onto a side track looping back toward them. One man stands on the loop, and his body will stop the trolley, killing him; if the trolley went straight, he would be safe (Choo 2025, [p.]). Most people judge diverting permissible, and Frederick Choo argues that the judgment is robust. It survives controls for order effects, holding when no other case is shown first. The philosophers who hold it see that the man is used, so the judgment is no illusion of inattention (Choo 2025, [p.]). I grant all of this.

The view nonetheless forbids diverting. Relative to not diverting, the five survive only because his body stops the trolley, and a stopper before him would spare him. That is the footbridge case's structure. Frances Kamm tries to separate the cases. She argues that in diverting, the agent removes the first threat and creates "a *new problem*", the trolley looping back, which his body merely prevents (Kamm 2007, 94–95). But the agent has a single goal, to save the five, and diverting serves it only through his body. Otsuka and Choo reject Kamm's description on this ground (Otsuka 2008, [p.]; Choo 2025, [p.]). Judging supply against the agent's alternatives keeps diverting permissible when people stand behind the man, since there the diversion saves the five by itself.[^9]

The view overrides a robust intuition, so I must say why it should prevail. Otsuka's argument against the intuition runs through a chain of cases, each differing from the last in one respect that seems morally irrelevant. It starts from a case that most people condemn. In the toppling case, pushing a lever moves a pole that topples a man from a bridge into the path of a trolley bound for five, and his body stops the trolley, killing him. That this is wrong is, in Otsuka's words, "a moral fixed point" for non-consequentialists (Otsuka 2008, [p.]).

Now change how the lever works. Pushing it diverts the trolley onto a loop, and as the trolley circles, it triggers the pole, which topples the man into its path. Otsuka calls this case "morally indistinguishable" from the toppling case, since in both "you push a lever that causes someone to fall from the bridge" (Otsuka 2008, [p.]). Now change one more thing: the man already stands on the loop, so the diverted trolley runs into him rather than he into it. The result is *Loop*, and what has changed is only whether the man is moved into the trolley or the trolley into the man. Kamm herself has argued, of a similar case, that this makes no moral difference (Otsuka 2008, [p.]). Otsuka doubts that anyone can separate these cases (Otsuka 2008, [p.] n. 25).

Choo answers Otsuka's general claim: those who judge *Loop* permissible see the use, and trust that judgment over the principles that condemn it (Choo 2025, [p.]). Neither of Choo's points answers the chain. The chain needs no one to miss the use, and it sets the verdict on *Loop* against a verdict on another case, not against a principle. So one set of firm judgments must yield. Keeping the verdict on *Loop* while condemning the toppling case would need a principled difference; none has been found. It would therefore mean giving up either the verdict on the toppling case or the principle that explains it together with the footbridge case and the other pairs of section 2. Giving up the single verdict on *Loop* costs less, all the more since the principle that forbids diverting has an independent rationale (section 3). I concede the cost; this is the one case where the view overrides a robust intuition, though other philosophers accept the same verdict.[^10]

A second objection is that the view permits driving over a man lying across the only road to people needing rescue. The view is silent on that case, not permissive. He supplies nothing; what the driver needs is his absence. Other constraints forbid running him over: Quinn's account of doing and allowing condemns it, since the driver intends an action of the car that will kill the man (see Liao and Barry 2020, [p.]), and Walen now defends a claim to the space one occupies (Walen 2022, [p.]).

A third objection concerns motive. Parry describes a rescuer willing to lose an arm to stop a sliding man. She sees five people further down the track and refrains so that his body will stop the trolley. Her refusal seems wrong, yet a refusal made only to keep her arm would be permissible, and the two are the same conduct (Parry 2023, [p.]).

The view can tell the refusals apart on one assumption: that the permission not to give justifies an act only when the act is the agent's answer to the question that premise 3 makes hers. Premise 3 supports the assumption. It gives her the standing to settle, by agreeing or refusing, whether a good she can supply is worth its cost to her, so what the permission protects is her answer to that question. The rescuer had answered it in favour of giving, since she was willing to lose her arm; she refuses instead for what the man's body will supply. So her permission does not justify the refusal, and nor can the five's rescue, since the good his body supplies is disabled. Had she refused to keep her arm, her permission would have justified the refusal. The nature of a prerogative points the same way: it permits her to give her own interests extra weight, and weight she does not give them cannot justify what she does. Agent-neutral justifications succeed whatever the motive, so motive matters only where conduct would otherwise spend someone. The view therefore disagrees with Liao and Barry about an enemy who turns a trolley from five onto one only because he hates the one: on the view, he acts permissibly, though his motive is vicious (Liao and Barry 2020, [p.]).[^11]

Finally, the view may seem absolutist, but it is not. Duties grow with the stakes, and the principle yields where duty yields. If a person can owe her life to save a million, taking it does not spend her. Whether she can is a question about duty's content, which the view inherits and does not settle.

## 6. Conclusion

I have argued that the means principle forbids spending persons. The replacement test, together with judging supply against the agent's alternatives, sorts the opening pair, the canonical pairs and the cases that defeat tests by absence. It gives Quinn's idea a precise form and repairs the rationale his successors offered, which fails when aimed at use and succeeds when aimed at spending. It extends to omissions and holdings, and it forbids diverting in *Loop* at a stated cost.

The view is not the whole of the ethics of harm. It is silent about people who are merely in the way, and it takes duties of rescue as given. Within its scope, though, the conclusion stands: what separates *Smoke* from *Wedge* is not what the firefighter does with the man's body, but whether his death is the cost of what it supplies.

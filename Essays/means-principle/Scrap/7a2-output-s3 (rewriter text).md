## 3. Why Spending Matters

The replacement test sorts the cases. But why should the means principle track it? Both Ramakrishnan and Uwe Steinhoff doubt any deeper rationale for the means principle (Steinhoff 2019). The best version of a principle does its job, no more and no less; the question is what that job is. My answer follows.

> **The argument.**
>
> (P1) The means principle exists to keep morality from treating persons as resources for others at their own expense.
>
> (P2) To treat something as a resource for others at its expense is to leave to those others the question whether a good it can supply them is worth what supplying that good costs it.
>
> (P3) Beyond what a person owes, that question is hers to settle, and she settles it by agreeing or refusing; within what she owes, her duty has already settled it.
>
> (P4) Others settle it for her exactly when they impose on her, or let her suffer, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good.
>
> (C) So the principle should hold that the good that a person's contribution would supply to others cannot justify imposing on her, or letting her suffer, the loss that supplying it would cost her, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear. That is, it should forbid spending her.

Premise 1 is common ground: Alexander states the principle's "core idea" as "we are not resources for others' benefit without our consent" (Alexander 2016, [p.]). Premise 2 is the analysis of the log and the tree: those who burn the log settle whether its heat is worth its being burned. Premise 3 is inherited; I defend it below. Premise 4 does the view's work: section 2 supplied the test, and this section argues that only spending settles the question for her.

Premise 3 rests on a permission nearly every non-consequentialist accepts: the agent-relative prerogative. No one need give a kidney, or her life, to others, even if they gain more than she loses. Premise 3 adds: this permission not to give is more than a permission to refuse.

Imagine a rule that leaves everyone free to refuse a kidney donation, but lets a hospital take one anyway, refused or not, from anyone whose kidney would save a life.[^6] No one would say the rule respects the permission. The permission's point explains why. As Walen puts it, a person is "a free agent leading her own independent life", whom morality "cannot treat ... as though she may be pressed into duty to serve others at great expense to herself" (Walen 2016, [p.]). Whether her body serves others at her cost is part of that life. A permission others could override by taking would settle the question for them, not for her. Within what she owes, her duty has already settled the question; that is Quinn's exception. Mack and Alexander pair the permission with the principle (Mack 2005, [p.]; Alexander 2016, [p.]).[^7]

Quong argues that no rationale of this kind can work (Quong 2020, [p.]). In its simplest form: "if Y is not duty bound to bear some cost, C, for the sake of goal, G, then it is wrong to impose C on Y for the sake of G". That condemns the diversion case, since the one on the side track is not duty bound to die for the five. Tadros's version says instead that the wrong is to "compel [a victim] to act for the sake of some end that she is permitted to reject for herself". Quong presses a dilemma against it. If compelling her means imposing on her a cost she is not duty bound to bear, Tadros's version collapses back into the simplest form and inherits its problem. If it means using her body or property without her consent or duty, the explanation is circular: it "amounts to saying: harmful using is distinctively wrong because it involves harmful using".

Both horns assume that the permission not to give limits how much a person may be made to bear. It does not. It limits what she must give: what she must do, and what of hers must serve others. In the diversion case, a cost falls on the one, but nothing of his serves the five, so the simplest form is not my rationale. Nor is my explanation circular. The replacement test picks out spending without mentioning use: it asks only whether something else could have supplied the good without her loss. Spending is wrong because it overrides the permission not to give, which no one defines in terms of use. This meets Liao and Barry's charge that such rationales sound "very much like the MP", the means principle itself (Liao and Barry 2020, [p.]). Quong's own rationale has the right structure, since rights over a fair share of the world exist "to block" usefulness "from serving as a reason" (Quong 2020, [p.]). But its object is too wide. What should be blocked, as *Smoke* shows, is the good a person's contribution supplies, as a reason to impose on her the cost of supplying it.

Premise 4 says others settle the question for her exactly when they spend her. They override what she must do by making her act, as coercion does. They override what of hers must serve by spending her, as the hospital's rule does. A side-effect harm does neither. Its loss is not the cost of anything of hers serving. Either nothing of hers serves, as in the diversion case, or the loss would fall on her all the same were what serves supplied from elsewhere, as the smoke falls in *Smoke*. So it falls under the ordinary constraint against harming, which weighs harms against goods.

The permission does not protect against every harm. In Mack's terms, it lets each person "devote *herself*" to her own ends, and the matching restriction forbids others to devote her "energy, attention, bodily parts, and so on" to theirs (Mack 2005, [p.]). A side-effect harm devotes nothing of anyone. The complaints differ: the person spent is taken from, and no value can answer that complaint. The person harmed as a side effect is harmed; that complaint is weighed.

The distinction answers Judith Jarvis Thomson's argument against turning the trolley. Her bystander could turn the trolley onto himself, onto one workman, or not at all. If he turns it onto the workman because he would rather not die, he makes "the one workman pay the cost of his good deed because he doesn't feel like paying it himself", which is worse than stealing money for Oxfam (Thomson 2008, 364). Since an ordinary bystander would not turn the trolley onto himself, she concludes, he may not turn it onto the one (Thomson 2008, 365–366). The analogy fails. The thief makes another's money serve his good deed, but turning the trolley does not make anything of the workman's serve the good deed. Without the analogy, only the inference from what he would do to himself survives, and that inference is false: others may turn a trolley onto her even though she need not turn it onto herself (compare Tadros 2011, [p.]; Cullity 2018, [p.] n. 45).

A last worry is Ramakrishnan's. A principle of this kind makes permissibility turn on a person's "location in causal space", which he finds "a terribly arbitrary feature of his situation" (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). It does so in a fine-grained way, as Ramakrishnan notes of his own principle. That principle lets a person's position prevent "a would-be defeater" of a justification, as when people stand behind the man on the side track. But it does not forbid letting someone die who is a necessary means to others' survival when medicine is short, as with the man whose legs hold the door (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]).

The view explains this fine-grainedness. What matters is whether she is made to give, and the view's two features have exactly this shape. Judging supply against the agent's alternatives removes the case where people stand behind the man on the side track. The replacement test removes the cases of shortage. Giving is structural: to give is to be the source of what another receives. A moral notion with a causal condition is not thereby arbitrary. Whether you have stolen from me depends on where what you have came from, and no one thinks that makes theft arbitrary.

## 1. Introduction

Compare two cases, both set in a burning house.

*Smoke.* Five people are trapped in an attic that is filling with smoke. The only way out is a trapdoor that falls shut unless something holds it open. An unconscious neighbour lies directly beneath it. His body is the only thing within reach that the trapdoor can be propped against, and propping it against him does him no harm. But once the trapdoor opens, the smoke will pour down onto him, and there is no time to move him. A firefighter props the trapdoor against him. The five climb down, and he dies of the smoke.

*Wedge.* Everything is the same, except that a roof vent draws the smoke away, so none will reach him, and the trapdoor is too heavy to prop. It stays open only if something is wedged beneath it, and the only thing there is his body, which the trapdoor's weight will crush. The firefighter wedges him beneath it. The five climb down, and he dies.

It seems permissible to prop the trapdoor in *Smoke* and wrong to wedge it in *Wedge*. Yet the difference is hard to locate. In both cases his body holds the trapdoor open, he dies, and five people are saved; in both, the firefighter kills him rather than letting him die. So the difference lies neither in using him, nor in harming him, nor in killing him. Nor does it lie in what she intends: in *Wedge* she need intend only that his body hold the trapdoor, and she would be glad if he survived its weight.[^1] The verdict in *Smoke* also has support that owes nothing to theory. Without the prop, *Smoke* would be a familiar diversion, like turning a runaway trolley from five people onto one, which most people judge permissible (Otsuka 2008, [p.]). And the prop changes nothing for him: had a beam been within reach, the firefighter would have used it, and he would have died of the smoke all the same. A use that makes no difference to what happens to him cannot be what turns a permissible killing into a wrongful one.

Not everyone accepts this verdict. Jonathan Quong's version of the means principle forbids harming someone when doing so "involves using Y's body or other things over which Y has a rightful prior claim", absent duty or consent, and he counts as a violation a case with the structure of *Smoke*: a woman on a side track is killed by a diverted trolley whose switch works only while her body presses a button (Quong 2020, [p.]; [p.] n. 27). I will argue that he need not.

The idea at the heart of the principle is Warren Quinn's. His version of the doctrine of double effect rests on "the strong moral presumption that those who can be usefully involved in the promotion of a goal only at the cost of something protected by their independent moral rights ... ought, prima facie, to serve the goal only voluntarily", unless they "have or would have strong moral obligations" to serve (Quinn 1989, [p.]). Later work has split into two tracks. One seeks a criterion: Quong asks whether the agent's act "could succeed in the absence of Y and Y's rightful property" (Quong 2020, [p.]), and Ketan Ramakrishnan whether a person will be useful to others only if her rights are infringed (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). The other seeks a rationale: persons may not be made to serve ends beyond what they owe (Mack 2005; Tadros 2011; Alexander 2016; Walen 2022). Each track lacks what the other has. Ramakrishnan takes his criterion to be primitive, and Quong's reason for his attaches to use, which *Smoke* shows to be the wrong target; the rationale lacks a criterion, and Quong argues that it therefore condemns too much or explains nothing (Quong 2020, [p.]).

I argue that the means principle forbids spending persons, not using them. To spend a person is to impose on her, or let her suffer, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear. A loss is the cost of supplying a good when the good could be had without the loss if what she contributes came from some other source. In *Wedge*, a beam wedged under the trapdoor would spare the man, so his death is the cost of what his body supplies; in *Smoke*, as I noted, a beam would not. The view breaks with Quong's, which condemns harmful use as such, and goes beyond Ramakrishnan's, which it matches for harms to persons. The idea is Quinn's and the rationale his successors'; what is new is a test that makes the idea precise, an argument that it is the right test, and what follows from it.

The principle is one constraint among several, and it is silent about people who are merely in the way. I take duties of rescue as given, offer no theory of property, and set aside harmless uses, since the principle concerns losses. Section 2 states the view and its test, section 3 argues that it is the right version of the principle, section 4 extends it to omissions and holdings, and section 5 answers objections, chiefly one from looping trolley cases.

## 2. Using and Spending

The view I defend says that the means principle forbids spending persons. A log burned for heat is a resource: those who burn it decide whether its heat is worth its being burned. A tree felled because it stands where a road will go, and left where it falls, is a casualty: nothing it supplies is weighed, because it supplies nothing. The log is a resource because others decide whether its service is worth what that service costs it. That is exactly what persons are said not to be. Quinn's ideal is that each person be treated "as existing only for purposes that he can share" (Quinn 1989, [p.]), and Quong gives each person "a sphere of independence where her decisions are sovereign" (Quong 2020, [p.]).[^2]

Stated in full, the view is this:

> **The Spending View.** The good that a person's contribution would supply to others cannot justify imposing on her, or letting her suffer, the loss that supplying it would cost her, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear.

A person's contribution is whatever of hers serves others: her body, its parts and position, her time and labour, her holdings. A loss is a setback she would not suffer under the alternative. To spend her is to impose such a loss on her, or let her suffer it, for the sake of the good her contribution supplies, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear. "For the sake of" concerns justification, not motive: the good is what would be offered to justify the loss. A loss she is duty bound to bear for the good may be imposed, and so may a loss she agrees to bear.[^3]

The view needs a test for when a loss is the cost of supplying a good. I propose a replacement test: her loss is the cost of her supplying a good if, were what she contributes provided from some other source, the good could be had without that loss. Take the footbridge case, in which the only way to stop a trolley bound for five people is to push a large man from a bridge into its path. Were the stopping supplied by something else, such as a heavy weight, the five could be saved and he could live. So his death is the cost of what his body supplies, and pushing him spends him. The test varies what the person contributes, never the person: she stays where she is, and only the source of her contribution changes.[^4]

The familiar tests vary the person instead. Quinn asked whether an agent would still need his victims' cooperation if they had shelters or medicines of their own (Quinn 1989, [p.]); his successors, Quong among them, ask what the agent could achieve in the victim's absence (see Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.] n. 21). Absence tests fail. In one of Ramakrishnan's cases, a woman lies on the only pill that could cure five people of a deadly disease, and harmless tests on her could yield a second cure. She has a heart attack, and the doctor leaves it untreated so that the tests can run (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). The five could be saved in her absence, with the pill, so an absence test finds nothing amiss; yet leaving her to die seems plainly wrong. The replacement test condemns it, since were the information her body yields supplied by another source, the doctor could save the five and treat her. Is it just another counterfactual test? It is. But it varies only the source of her contribution, never her presence or her position, so it never has to say what would fill the place she leaves empty.[^5]

The view has a second feature: what a person supplies is judged against what the agent could otherwise do. A justification is a reason for one option rather than another, so her contribution supplies a good only if the good is a respect in which the act is better for others than an alternative. Suppose six people stand behind the man on the side track, so that his body will shield them from the diverted trolley. They are safe if the agent does not divert, so their safety is no respect in which diverting is better. What makes diverting better is the rescue of the five, and nothing of his supplies that: diverting is permissible. When the side track loops back to the five, by contrast, the whole improvement comes from his body (section 5). Michael Otsuka draws the contrast in terms of intention, and Ramakrishnan discusses a version with twenty behind (Otsuka 2008, [p.]; Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]).[^6] The comparison also corrects Larry Alexander, who counts only benefits "excluding those causally derived from usings" and so forbids this diversion, measuring it against the man's absence, which the agent cannot choose (Alexander 2016, [p.] and n. 8 (verify)).

Together the two features sort the canonical pairs. The footbridge case is spending; the diversion case is not, since nothing of the one supplies the five's rescue. Ramakrishnan's own pair has the shape of the opening pair, now for letting die. A man's legs hold open the door of a room where five people are dying of a virus, and a doctor gives his scarce medicine to the five rather than to the man (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). Were the door held by something else, the man would still go without, since there is not enough medicine for him and the five, so the doctor acts permissibly. The patient left untreated so that tests can run, by contrast, is spent. Using a person is thus neither necessary nor sufficient for spending her: the man at the door, like the man in *Smoke*, is used without being spent, and the patient is spent though the tests on her are harmless.

Two cases from S. Matthew Liao and Christian Barry show that the replacement test needs no stipulation about empty places (Liao and Barry 2020, [p.]). In one, a defender can stop an attack only by shooting a man off a roof onto the attacker; had he not been there, builders would have put a log there for her to shoot instead. An absence test says that his presence costs her an option and so weakens his claim, yet shooting him is as wrong as ever. In the other, a defender's flamethrower kills an attacker and a bystander behind him; had the bystander not stood in front of a door, fifty people would have come through it into the flames. An absence test says that his presence costs her nothing, so his claim is at full strength and the act forbidden, yet it seems permissible. The replacement test sorts both: the man on the roof supplies the stopping and would be spared were it supplied otherwise, and the bystander supplies nothing relative to the defender's alternative, since the fifty are safe if she does not fire.[^7]

A reader may suspect that the view is Ramakrishnan's principle renamed. On his principle, a justification for infringing someone's claim that holds because she "will be useful to others ... only if C is infringed" is "substantially weaker" than it otherwise would be (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). For harms to persons the two agree closely, but they differ in four ways. First, the view grounds its comparison with the agent's alternatives, and his treatment of cases where a person's position prevents "a would-be defeater" of a justification falls out as a special case (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). Second, it is explained by a permission almost everyone accepts (section 3), where he takes his principle as primitive. Third, it covers holdings, which Quong presses against him (section 4). Fourth, it disables the justification within a bound set by duty, where his principle discounts it.

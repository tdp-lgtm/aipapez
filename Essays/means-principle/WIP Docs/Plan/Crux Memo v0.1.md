## 0. Verdict in brief

1. **The crux is resolved well enough to plan.** Spending is morally deep because it is the one way of imposing a loss that settles, *for* a person, the trade-off between what she supplies to others and what supplying it costs her. The limits of beneficence assign that trade-off to her (beyond what she owes). Mere harm settles no trade-off of hers. (Rationale R, §2.)
2. **The test is not gerrymandered.** Read "supplies" relative to the agent's alternatives and "cost" by an existential replacement test. Both readings fall out of R. The old "positive improvement, not collateral-damage prevention" clause is replaced by the alternatives reading, with the same verdicts (§1, §3).
3. **Loop is a strength, not a bullet.** In Thomson's own Loop the five's bodies stop the trolley if it goes straight, so it never reaches the one. Hence *not* diverting would spend the five (for the one) just as diverting would spend the one (for the five). When every option spends someone, the protected claims conflict and are weighed, as in Switch; the numbers favour diverting. The view condemns only the *asymmetric* loop (the one safe if the trolley goes straight), which it treats like Bridge (with Walen 2014; against Kamm). This replaces Sketch A's plan to bite the bullet (§4).
4. **Bonus: when intentions matter.** R implies that a prerogative is authority over one's *own* trade-off, exercised only by acting on it. So refusing aid in order to exploit someone's plight is not an exercise of the prerogative. That delivers Parry's *Costly Icy Trolley* and Christensen's *Streets* verdicts without making the principle intention-based (§5). Sketch B's result now comes free, in one paragraph.
5. **Originality, re-assessed after Stage 2 reading.** Several components are *not* new, and the essay must say so:
   - Quinn 1989 ties the DDE to unowed service, with an exception for strong obligations (file 12, abridged; verify #6).
   - Tadros 2011 and Alexander 2016 bound the MP by enforceable duty (verify #5).
   - Quong 2020 has reason-blocking bounded by rescue duties (file 09 L135–139).
   - Mack 2005 argues that a prerogative needs matching restrictions (verify #12).
   - Costa 1987 and Walen 2014 give the symmetry treatment of Loop (file 07 L201–217).
   - Added after writing §2: Tadros 2011 already says side-effect victims "do not contribute to the pursuit of the goal" and are not "required to expend [their] life" (file 03 L277), and he answers Thomson 2008 by limiting her argument to using (L85–101). Cullity 2018 fn 45 makes the same limitation ("stand-in"). Thomson 2008 (file 13, pp. 364–366) is the over-general form of P4. So P4–P5 are *not* new as a family of argument. What is new is the scope result: the argument's correct object is *supplying at a cost*, which reaches omissions and excludes uses whose harm is not the price of the use. This is the fix that lets the family escape Quong's dilemma.

   What remains new (subject to reading #1–#5 and #7):
   - (a) The spending criterion (a loss that is the cost of supplying a good, judged relative to the alternatives) as the MP's content, replacing "use."
   - (b) Deriving that criterion from the *object* of the prerogative over giving. This yields the MP's exact shape and no more, which answers both Mack-style over-generalization and Quong's dilemma.
   - (c) The extension to holdings through cost of supply. This dissolves Parry's Three-Track and normative-externality objections and Quong's bitten bullets (Overdetermined, Switch with Button).
   - (d) Omissions without prior rights (Parry's Icy Trolley) come out as a consequence of the criterion, not an add-on.
   - (e) The symmetric/asymmetric Loop result follows from (a) and (d), with no separate symmetry thesis.
   - (f) An independent check: research ethics already draws the line.

## 1. The principle, restated

**Display (draft).** *The fact that a person's contribution would supply a good to others cannot be a reason for imposing on her a loss that is the cost of her supplying it, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear.*

**Terms.**
- **Contribution:** anything of hers that a good can come from. This covers her body and its powers, position, and time; her capacities; and her holdings.
- **Supplies (relative to alternatives):** her contribution supplies good G under option O iff (i) G obtains under O partly because of her contribution, and (ii) G is a respect in which O is better for others than the agent's alternatives.
- **Loss:** a setback she suffers under O and would not suffer under the alternative. The notion is comparative, so a Pareto improvement imposes no loss.
- **Cost of supplying (existential replacement):** loss L is the cost of her supplying G iff, were her contribution to G provided by some other source (anywhere), the agent could secure G without imposing L.
- **Spending:** imposing a loss that is the cost of her supplying a good, by act or omission, where that good figures in the justification. This is an objective matter: it concerns what can justify, not what the agent intends.
- **Owes:** her duties of rescue, justice, and liability fix what supply at what cost can be required of her. Consent can extend beyond that.

**Structure.** The principle is a *disabler*: the supplied good is excluded as a reason for imposing the cost. It is bounded by duty. It is not a discount (Ramakrishnan's *Utility*, file 01 L153–157) and not an unbounded absolute. Note that Quong's MP is also "absolute" only within a scope already priced by rescue duties (file 09 L147–159), so the structural difference from Quong is smaller than Sketch A implied. Say so.

**Why each clause is needed** (checked on cases in §3):
- The alternatives clause handles *Tracks* without an ad hoc exception. Billy's body keeps the twenty safe, but they are safe anyway if the agent does not divert, so their safety is no respect in which diverting is better. By contrast, in *Loop* the whole improvement over not diverting comes from the one's body.
- The existential replacement handles the trapdoor Bridge. A "replace at her own location" reading would wrongly permit both trapdoor Bridge and Loop (see the stress-test log in `Thesis Candidates v0.1.md`).
- The comparative notion of loss excludes Pareto cases, such as a loop in which the one dies whatever the agent does.

## 2. Rationale R: why spending is deep

**Premises.**
- **P1 (function).** The MP's job is to keep morality from treating persons as resources for others. Sources: Quinn's "Kantian ideal" (file 12: persons "existing only for purposes that he can share"; verify #6 for the page); Ramakrishnan's "people are not resources for others' benefit" (file 01 L215); Quong's independence (file 09 L135).
- **P2 (what resource-treatment is).** To treat something as a resource is to settle *for it* the trade-off between what it supplies and what supplying costs it: to count the value of its supply as a reason for imposing that cost. A log burned for heat is a resource. A tree felled because it stands where the road will go is a casualty of the road: nothing it supplies is being traded against its loss.
- **P3 (the prerogative over giving).** The limits of beneficence assign to each person the trade-off between what she supplies to others and what supplying it costs her, beyond what she owes. She need not give her kidney, or her life, even when others would gain more.
- **P4 (non-hollowness).** That assignment would be hollow if others could settle the same trade-off against her whenever others would gain more. A permission to refuse that others may override by taking protects nothing the prerogative is for. (This is Mack's style of argument, verify #12, and the standard complaint against a hybrid view with prerogatives but no restrictions.)
- **P5 (exact shape).** Others settle her trade-off against her exactly when they count the good her contribution supplies as a reason for imposing on her the cost of supplying it: that is, when they spend her. Side-effect losses and scarcity losses settle no trade-off of hers, because nothing of hers is being given.

**Conclusion.** No spending beyond duty or consent: the Means Principle.

**Corollaries.**
- It covers omissions, because a trade-off can be settled by letting her lose.
- It covers holdings exactly where the owner's loss is the cost of their service.
- It is bounded by duty because the prerogative is: the trade-off is hers only beyond what she owes.
- It is objective, because it restricts what can justify.

**Passing the standing tests.**
- **Quong's dilemma** (file 09 L125–127).
  - *Horn 1:* "If Y is not duty bound to bear C for G, it is wrong to impose C for G." This fails because it condemns Switch (L125). R's premise is narrower: it concerns *supplying* G at cost C, not bearing C for G. The limits of beneficence govern what one must supply. Switch imposes no cost of supply, and Betty's death there raises no beneficence question at all; it is a question of whether the agent's act is justified on balance. So Quong dismissed the duty rationale too fast: its correct form concerns what one must give, not what one must bear.
  - *Horn 2 (tautology).* The prerogative over giving is recognized independently of the MP (it is a thesis about the demands of beneficence), so explaining the MP by it is not circular.
- **Ramakrishnan's worry** that the principle is "ultimately a principle about causal structure" (file 01 L269). The structure matters because it fixes *whose* trade-off is being settled. The replacement test is simply how we tell whether a loss is being exchanged for something the victim gives.
- **Mack-style over-generalization** (a prerogative supports restrictions on all harming). The prerogative's object is the supply/cost trade-off, and harms that settle no such trade-off fall outside it. Evidence: my prerogative not to divert a trolley onto myself does not imply that others may not divert it onto me (Switch). What I may decline to give, others may not take; what I may decline to *do* to myself, others may sometimes do to me as a side effect.

**Independent standing** (evidence that the line is not an artifact of trolley cases).
- **Research ethics.** Placebo controls are acceptable where no proven treatment exists, and, under a further condition, where the trial "does not require participants to forgo treatment they would otherwise receive" (Millum & Grady 2013, as reported in search summary; verify #11). The Declaration of Helsinki's placebo paragraph allows the exception only if participants face no "additional risks of serious or irreversible harm as a result of not receiving the best proven intervention" (verify #18). Randomizing a scarce experimental drug is defended as fair allocation that also yields data (search 52). This is *Room* versus *Heart Attack* in institutional form: the control patient under scarcity loses out; the control patient denied available treatment is spent. It also shows the view's significance beyond trolleys.
- **Restitution** (optional, a footnote at most). Beneficiaries of a *permissible* spending (within duty: *Heirloom Tool*; necessity cases) owe the spent person restitution. Beneficiaries of a side-effect harm owe nothing to the victim as beneficiaries. This needs legal sources before use.

## 4. Loop

- **The stipulation.** Thomson's own Loop specifies that the five "are thin, but thick enough so that although all five will be killed if the trolley goes straight, the bodies of the five will stop it, and it will therefore not reach the one." This is quoted by Walen 2014 (file 07 L189) from Thomson 1986, p. 102 (verify #10 against the 1985 *Yale Law Journal* text). Cullity's version says the same: "The trolley will kill the five and then stop if it is not diverted" (file 11 L253). So if the trolley is not diverted, the one survives *only because the five's bodies stop it*.
- **Spending analysis.**
  - Diverting spends the one: his body supplies the five's rescue, and a stopper placed before him would spare him.
  - Not diverting spends the five: their bodies supply the one's safety, and a stopper before them would spare them.
  - So every option spends someone.
- **What the principle says when every option spends someone.** The disabler excludes supplied goods as reasons *for* imposing costs. It does not silence the spent parties' own claims against being spent. When those claims conflict (each option overrides someone's), they are weighed, and numbers count among claims of the same kind, as they do in Switch. The doing/allowing difference still applies: diverting spends by act, not diverting spends by omission. So the result is exactly Switch's: diverting is permissible. The analogy is Foot's trolley driver, who can only choose whom he kills and should kill fewer (verify: Foot 1967, or Thomson 1985's discussion of the driver).
- **The asymmetric loop.** Here the one would be safe if the trolley went straight, even without the five. Not diverting spends no one; diverting spends the one; so diverting is forbidden, like Bridge. Walen 2014 reaches the same verdict (file 07 L223, Fig. 2), and notes that Kamm treats it like Loop (L235, citing *Intricate Ethics* p. 122; verify #9).
- **Hypothesis about divided intuitions.** Reports of a roughly 50:50 split on Loop (Hauser, via file 07 fn 32 and file 11 fn 48, citing Otsuka p. 95), and the context effects found by Liao et al. 2012 (verify #13), fit a view on which the verdict turns on whether the one is himself protected by the five. The standard telling leaves that detail implicit. Offer this only as a hypothesis, flagged as such.
- **Credit and difference.** Costa 1987 (via file 07 fn 34) and Walen 2014 give the symmetry treatment within the DDE and the RCP respectively. The Spending View derives it from the definition of spending plus its coverage of omissions: not diverting *is* a spending, and no separate appeal to "interdependence" is needed. Choo 2025 must be read for objections to symmetry-based defences (verify #3). If Choo shows that asymmetric loops still elicit permissibility intuitions, the view bites that bullet, with Walen and against Kamm, on the strength of the parity with trapdoor Bridge.
- **Mutual-shield generalization (handle with care).** In a symmetric Bridge, the man on the bridge would himself be killed by the trolley unless the five's bodies stopped it. Pushing there is not an MP violation: as far as the MP goes, it is like Switch. Personal-force reactions or other principles may still weigh against pushing. Use this only if it survives referee scrutiny; it is a genuine novel prediction but a risky one.

## 5. Intentions: the prerogative point (one paragraph in the essay)

*Costly Icy Trolley* (file 06 L121). Rescuing Billy would cost Sarah her arm, and that gives her an undisabled reason, so the objective principle permits her to refrain. Parry holds that refraining *in order that* Billy's body stop the trolley is wrong.

R explains why:
- A prerogative is authority over one's *own* trade-off, and it is exercised only by acting on it: declining for the arm's sake.
- Declining for the sake of what the five gain through Billy's body is not an exercise of the prerogative.
- The only other available justification, the five's rescue, is disabled.

So intentions matter exactly where the justification is a prerogative, and nowhere else. The same analysis covers Christensen's *Streets*. Contrast Quong's *Murders Averted*, where the justification is agent-neutral, so motive is irrelevant. This answers Parry's and Tadros's case for an intention-based MP without conceding it.

## 6. Objections to plan for

- **O1 Relabeling** (the criterion is Ramakrishnan's *Utility* renamed). Reply: R. Also show where the two come apart:
  - holdings (Life Jacket/Driftwood; Decoy House);
  - losses imposed without a prior claim (the principle helps fix rescue claims rather than presupposing them; *Utility* needs "an independent account of the claims we have," file 01 fn 33);
  - structure (disabling within duty vs discount);
  - symmetric Loop (check what *Utility* says).
- **O2 Quong's dilemma.** Reply: §2.
- **O3 Mack-style generalization.** Reply: §2.
- **O4 Loop / Choo.** Reply: §4.
- **O5 Harmless use** (reading a diary; using a sleeper as a pillow). These are outside the MP; they are wrongs of trespass or autonomy. Quong and Ramakrishnan also restrict the MP to harm.
- **O6 Catastrophe.** Duties scale with stakes, so the principle yields where duty yields. Whether a person can owe her life to save a million is a question the principle inherits, not one it must settle.
- **O7 Property.** Quong's unity demand is met (file 09 L111). Parry's externality objection (file 06 L247–259) is answered: acquiring property changes others' protections only where the owner's loss is the cost of the holding's service, and duties grow with holdings.
- **O8 Liao & Barry.** Their "person in the path" cases, malicious-reason cases, and the charge that victim-centred views presuppose the MP (verify #2). Read first.
- **O9 Eliminative harms** (Cave Exits; removing obstacles). These are not spending, so the principle is silent and ordinary weighing governs. This is in good company (Ramakrishnan, Quong). Letting someone die so that her body can be moved is a hard case; keep it out of the essay unless a referee raises it.
- **O10 Consent readings** (Kahn 2024, file 08). Consent is an exception, not the criterion.

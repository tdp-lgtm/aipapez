## 1. Thesis

**One sentence (P/Q/R).** I argue that the Means Principle forbids *spending* persons, not using them: imposing on someone, by act or omission, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good, beyond what she owes. This is against the prevailing criteria, which locate the principle's target in the use of a person's body or property, measured against her absence (Quong 2020), or in a bare relation of usefulness (Ramakrishnan 2016). The grounds: spending is the test for being made to serve others that the familiar argument for the principle has lacked. With it, that argument escapes its standing objections, and the principle gets right the cases on which the rival tests fail.

**Credit sentence (in §1, right after the thesis).** The reason the essay gives for the principle is not new. It is the familiar thought that the principle protects people from being made to serve others beyond what they owe (Quinn; Tadros 2011; Mack 2005; Alexander 2016; Walen 2022). What is new is a test for when that happens, and the test is what the familiar thought has lacked.

**Compressed form (for the abstract and first paragraph).** The Means Principle forbids spending persons, not using them.

**Full form (Display 1, in §2).**

> **The Spending View.** The good that a person's body, time, or holdings would supply to others cannot justify imposing on her, or letting her suffer, the loss that supplying it would cost her, beyond what she owes them or agrees to bear.

Two glosses follow immediately in running prose:
- **The replacement test.** Her loss is the cost of her supplying a good if, were what she contributes provided from some other source, the good could be had without that loss.
- **Supplying is judged against what the agent could otherwise do.** Her contribution supplies a good only if the good is part of what makes the act better for others than the agent's alternatives. This is not an added clause. The principle limits what can justify an act, and a justification is always a reason for doing one thing *rather than another*.

**Contribution type.** A new distinction (using vs spending) with a precise test. It also makes a diagnosis: the familiar rationale failed for want of a criterion, not because its idea was wrong. One spine: spending is both the right criterion and the thing the inherited rationale needs.

**What the essay does not claim** (stated in §1, in its own sentences):
- It does not offer a new reason why the principle matters. It takes a familiar one and supplies the missing piece.
- It does not account for all constraints on harming. Harming someone who stands in the path of the agent's means is not spending. Examples: driving over a person lying on the only road; firing through a bystander; a train that must run over a trapped man. The principle is silent there, and other constraints must do the work. So must the doing/allowing distinction and the wrongs of harmless use.
- It does not defend the limits of beneficence or settle the content of rescue duties. It takes them as inputs.
- It does not settle what counts as a person's rightful holdings.

## 2. The move

The analytic move: **separate what a person is made to supply from what she is made to bear, and identify supply by replacing her contribution and comparing the agent's alternatives.**
- In the footbridge case, the man's body supplies the stopping of the trolley, and his death is what supplying it costs him. A stopper placed before him would spare him.
- In the diversion case, the man on the side track supplies nothing: the five are saved by the trolley's leaving their track. His death is a cost of the diversion, which he bears.

Why this is the move the literature needs:
- **The rationale track** (Quinn, Tadros, Mack, Alexander) has the right idea: the principle protects people from being made to serve. But it has no test for "made to serve" that does not presuppose the principle. So Quong's dilemma catches it: read as a limit on what one must *bear*, it condemns the diversion case; defined as harmful use, it is circular.
- **The criterion track** (Quong, Øverland, Walen, Alexander) measures the victim against her *absence* or against the agent's entitlements. That misfires on Liao & Barry's cases, on Walen's own counterexamples, and on the case of six people standing behind the man on the side track. And it has no explanation.

The test does both jobs:
- It identifies supplying through sources, goods and costs, never "means" or "use". So the rationale is no longer circular.
- It excludes the diversion case, so the rationale is not too broad.

## 3. Why this is the sharpest version

- **One principle, one test, one bound.** To settle trolley cases, Quong's version needs:
  - use;
  - a counterfactual absence test;
  - a rescue proviso for omissions;
  - an account of property.

  Ramakrishnan's needs a prior account of claims, and admits no explanation. The Spending View needs the replacement test, the comparison with the agent's alternatives, and the duty bound. Its verdicts on omissions, holdings and Loop follow from these.
- **Derived, not listed.** Each clause answers to something prior:
  - the function (P1);
  - the permission not to give (P3);
  - the contrastive structure of justification (the alternatives gloss).

  This meets the custodian's brief that the version be "responsive to what the Means Principle is supposed to do." The functions the corpus assigns the principle (provided-corpus notes, F1–F6) are all served:
  - puzzling pairs sorted;
  - omissions covered;

- **Claims only what the literature leaves open** (Literature Map v0.2 §5). It credits the rest up front, which protects the originality score instead of risking it.
- **Weaker modality where it suffices.** The thesis says the principle *forbids spending*. It does not say that spending is the only wrong in the vicinity.

## 4. Central argument (Display 2, in §3)

> **The argument.**
> (P1) The Means Principle exists to keep morality from treating persons as resources for others.
> (P2) To treat something as a resource for others is to treat the question whether a good it can supply them is worth what supplying it costs it as a question for others to settle.
> (P3) Beyond what a person owes, that question is hers to settle, and she settles it by agreeing or refusing; within what she owes, her duty has already settled it.
> (P4) Others settle it for her exactly when they impose on her, or let her suffer, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good.
> (C) So the Means Principle should forbid imposing on a person, or letting her suffer, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear: it should forbid spending her.

**Where each premise is defended** (named in the text right after the display):
- **P1** is common ground: Quinn's ideal of persons as existing only for purposes they can share; Ramakrishnan's "people are not resources for others' benefit" (file 01 L215); Alexander's "we are not resources for others' benefit" (file 25 L79). §1 and §3, cited, not argued.
- **P2** is in §2: the log and the tree.
- **P3** is in §3 and is *inherited*: the permission not to give, and why it must be more than a permission to refuse. Credit: Mack (file 19 L117–167), Alexander's corollary (file 25 L83, fn 16 at L95), Walen 2022's "patient-analog" (file 22 L47), and Tadros (file 03 L277).
- **P4** is the essay's contribution, defended twice:
  - in §2, by the test and its cases;
  - in §3, by *doing versus serving*, which shows that nothing but spending settles the question for her: side-effect harms do not, and neither does the bystander's own refusal in Thomson's case.

**Argument labels.** P1–P4 only (the essay refers back to them). No other labels.

### §2 Spending (1,300)

- **Resources and casualties (P2).** A log burned for heat vs a tree felled because it stands where the road will go, in 3–4 sentences. This is the essay's one illustration: it restates P2 and argues nothing.
- **Display 1 and its glosses.** Define in plain words: "contribution" (body, its powers and position, time, holdings) and "loss" (comparative: a setback she would not suffer under the alternative).
- **Feature 1: replacement, not absence.**
  - The footbridge: a stopper placed before the man would spare him, so his death is the cost of what his body supplies.
  - Why not absence: removing the person changes more than her contribution, because something else would then stand where she stood. Replacing only her contribution holds everything else fixed.
  - Liao & Barry show that absence tests reverse their verdicts depending on what fills the empty place (a log, a crate, nothing) (file 29 L165–213). One sentence here; the cases follow below.
- **Feature 2: supply judged against what the agent could otherwise do.**
  - The ground: the principle limits what can justify an act, and a justification is a reason for one option *rather than another*.
  - Illustration: six people stand behind the man on the side track, and his body would shield them from the diverted trolley. If the agent does not divert, they are safe anyway. So their safety is no part of what makes diverting better, and his body supplies nothing that figures in the justification. Permissible.
  - Contrast *Loop*, where the whole of the improvement over not diverting comes from his body.
  - Credit: Otsuka reached this contrast through intentions (file 27 L93–105), and Ramakrishnan's *Tracks* is the same shape (file 01).
  - Correct Alexander, whose accounting forbids diverting in the six-behind case (file 25 L57).
- **The classic pairs** (2–3 sentences each):
  - the footbridge (spending) vs the diversion (nothing supplied);
  - *Room* (his legs hold the door, but his death comes from the shortage of medicine) vs *Heart Attack* (her untreated attack is the cost of the data her body supplies).
- **The stress test** (one paragraph). Three of the cases Liao & Barry use against the victim-centred views, told plainly:
  - a man on a roof whose place a log would otherwise fill;
  - a man blocking a door behind which fifty are safe either way;
  - a man whose weight on a button keeps a diversion from reaching ten.

  The test gets each right, with no stipulated counterfactual. Walen's two counterexamples to his own 2014 baseline go in a footnote (file 14 L161–217).
- **Using without spending, and spending without using.**
  - Using without spending: *Room*; a wrench used to turn the trolley toward its owner; a body that holds down a switch button.
  - Spending without using: letting someone go untreated so that her body yields data.
  - So the principle's target is not use.
- **Pre-emptive defence against deflation** ("this is Ramakrishnan renamed"). Grant the closeness for harms to persons. Then name the differences:
  - the contrastive ground (just shown);
  - the explanation (§3);
  - omissions and holdings (§4);
  - disabling up to duty versus his discount.

### §3 Why spending matters (1,200)

- **Display 2**, with the defence locations.
- **The inherited premise (P3).**
  - A person need not give her kidney, or her life, even when others would gain more.
  - This permission must be more than a permission to refuse. If others could take whenever the gain was large enough, whether she gives would not be hers to decide.
  - Credit Mack, Alexander (the corollary), Walen 2022 and Tadros, in the text and a footnote. Say plainly that this premise is theirs.
- **Quong's dilemma** ("attack one premise"). Restate it validly, with a quote (file 09 L125–127).
  - **Horn one** reads the rationale as "if Y is not duty bound to bear C for G, it is wrong to impose C on Y for G", which condemns the diversion case. The premise to deny: that the permission not to give limits what a person must *bear*. It limits what she must supply, and what she must do.
  - **Horn two** says the rationale defines "compelled to serve" as harmful use, so it is circular. It fails because the test identifies supplying by sources, goods and costs, without mentioning use.
- **Doing versus serving** (the defence of P4's "exactly").
  - The permission not to make great sacrifices protects two things: what a person must *do*, and what *of hers* must serve.
  - Others cannot override the first without making her act; coercion does that, and is forbidden for that reason. They override the second exactly by spending her. A side-effect harm does neither.
  - **Thomson 2008**: a bystander who would not turn the trolley onto himself may not turn it onto another (file 13, pp. 364–366). Her argument runs through the first thing protected: what the bystander may decline is an act of his own. So it yields no restriction on turning the trolley onto the one.
  - Credit Tadros's reply ("service of a goal" vs "side effect", file 03 L85–101) and Cullity (fn 45). The addition here: the precise distinction, made available by the test.
- **Steinhoff's challenge.** Why is using worse than discarding (file 16 L107–109)?
  - Spending settles, for her, the question the permission leaves to her. A side-effect harm does not.
  - His matched pairs involve people in the path of the agent's means, which other constraints cover (§5).
  - His best-controlled case, in which a sensor pointed at the side-track man's body works the switch, comes out as he says it should: permissible (L145–147).
- **The arbitrariness worry** (Ramakrishnan, file 01 L267–277). The replacement test matters morally because it tells us whether a person is made to give, rather than whether she merely loses out.
  - Independent evidence that this line is not an artifact of trolley cases: research ethics draws it, in the words "forgo treatment they would otherwise receive" (verify #11). This is the two trials of §1.

### §4 Omissions and holdings (600)

- **Omissions.** Parry's sliding man vs two independent trolleys, told plainly (file 06 L67–105; Parry adapts it from Tadros 2011, p. 122, fn 13).
  - Parry shows that on Quong's view the man lacks the prior rescue right the principle is meant to protect.
  - The test: the good his body would supply is disabled as a reason against rescuing him, so an ordinary duty of easy rescue stands. The victim of the second trolley supplies nothing, so the numbers count.
  - Credit the structure to Alexander: a duty to rescue "is not canceled by the setback to C" (file 25 L151). Note that he denies general duties of easy rescue (L161).
  - **Modularity sentence:** the principle creates no duty to aid; it removes one reason against meeting it. So its reach in omission cases depends on which rescue duties there are. Footnote Hecht 2023 (file 15 L29, L75) on how contested that is.
- **Holdings.**
  - Quong's unity demand, the life jacket vs the driftwood (file 09 L101–111), is met. The jacket's owner drowns as the cost of what her jacket supplies; the loser of the race for the driftwood supplies nothing.
  - Parry's wrench cases (file 06 L171–195): the owner does not die as the cost of the wrench's service, so there is no three-track absurdity.
  - Parry's externality objection (L231–259) gets one sentence: acquiring something changes others' protections only where the owner's loss would be the cost of the thing's service.
  - Footnote: Walen's tightness test (file 22 L79) reaches similar verdicts on holdings by another route.
- **The bound.** Easy rescue: a vase smashed to reach a stuck switch is spent within duty, and redress is owed (one or two sentences).

### §5 Objections (1,100)

- **(a) Loop** (≈450). This is the dedicated objection.
  - **Stipulation block.** In the version the debate and the studies concern, the one would be safe if the trolley went straight (Choo fn 12, file 28 L102; Liao et al. fn 2, file 17 L121).
  - **Concede-and-scope.** Grant Choo's empirical premise in full: when Loop is shown first, most people judge diverting permissible (file 28 L153; report the means, not percentages, for Liao et al.). The question that remains is normative.
  - **Verdict:** forbidden. Judged against not diverting, the one's body supplies the whole of the good, and a stopper placed before him would spare him.
  - **Kamm's rescue fails.** She calls the looping trolley "a new problem" that his body merely prevents (file 21 L61–65, pp. 94–95; the p. 94 boundary is inferred because the conversion lacks markers for pp. 92–94). This fails for the reason Otsuka and Choo give: the agent has one goal, saving the five (file 27 L91, L105; file 28 L267–275).
  - **Why accept the verdict:**
    - (i) Otsuka's argument that Loop cannot be told apart from a loop that topples a man from a bridge, or from a ramp (file 27 L107–125, L145–151). Choo does not engage it.
    - (ii) If diverting were permitted, the protection against being made to serve would lapse exactly where the whole benefit comes from the victim's body.
  - **Concede the cost.**
  - **Footnotes:** Thomson's own, symmetric case (file 23 L133–149), where the principle applies in both directions (Costa 1987, via Otsuka fn 7; verify #21), and Liao et al.'s order effects.
- **(b) The person in the path** (≈150). "The view permits driving over the man on the road." Reply: it says nothing about that case. Other constraints forbid it: doing and allowing where a new threat is created; claims to the space one occupies (Walen 2022, file 22 L59–61). The principle does not claim to be the whole ethics of harming. Close with a flat verdict.
- **(c) Intentions** (≈300).
  - A rescuer who would lose an arm refuses to save a sliding man. For the arm's sake the refusal is permissible; refusing *so that* his body stops the trolley is wrong (Parry, file 06 L121).
  - How the view explains it: P3 makes the permission not to give an authority over one's *own* sacrifice. That authority is exercised only by acting on it, and a refusal made to exploit another's plight is no exercise of it. The only other justification, the five's rescue, is disabled.
  - Otherwise the principle is objective: Liao & Barry's malicious diverter is acknowledged as a disagreement (file 29 L143–163).
  - Footnote: Walen 2022's forfeiture view (file 22 L107–123) and Christensen's *Streets* (file 05).
- **(d) Catastrophe** (≈150). Duties grow with the stakes, so the principle yields where duty yields. Whether a person can owe her life to save a million is a question the principle inherits, not one it must settle.

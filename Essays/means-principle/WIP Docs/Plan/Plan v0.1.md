## 1. Thesis

**One sentence (P/Q/R).** I argue that the Means Principle forbids *spending* persons, not using them: making someone bear a loss that is the cost of what she supplies to others, for the sake of what she supplies, beyond what she owes. This is against the prevailing views, which locate its target either in the use of a person's body or property (Quong 2020) or in the bare causal structure of her usefulness (Ramakrishnan 2016). The ground is that the limits of beneficence leave it to each person to decide whether what she can give is worth what giving it would cost her, and spending is exactly the way of making that decision for her.

**Compressed form (for the abstract and first paragraph).** The Means Principle forbids spending persons, not using them.

**Full form (Display 1, in §2).**

> **The Spending View.** The good that a person's body, time, or holdings would supply to others cannot justify imposing on her, or letting her suffer, the loss that supplying it would cost her, beyond what she owes them or agrees to bear.

With two glosses in running prose, immediately after the display:
- *Cost of her supplying it*: the replacement test. Her loss is the cost of her supplying a good if, were what she contributes provided from some other source, the good could be had without that loss.
- *Supplying*: judged against the agent's alternatives. Her contribution supplies a good only if the good is part of what makes the act better for others than what the agent could otherwise do.

**Contribution type.** A new distinction (using vs spending) that also dissolves a confusion. The confusion is that the duty-based rationale for the principle fails. It fails only when duties are read as limits on what a person must *bear*; read as limits on what she must *supply*, it succeeds. One spine: spending is both the right criterion and the thing the right rationale explains.

**What the essay does not claim** (stated in §1, in its own sentences):
- It does not give an account of all constraints on harming. The doing/allowing distinction, harms that remove an obstacle, and harmless uses (wrongs of trespass or of bodily and sexual integrity) are left to other principles.
- It does not defend the limits of beneficence. It assumes, with the principle's defenders, that they exist.
- It does not settle what counts as a person's rightful holdings. It needs only that some things are hers.

## 2. The move

The analytic move is the distinction between what a person is made to **supply** and what she is made to **bear**.
- In the footbridge case the man's body supplies the stopping of the trolley, and his death is what supplying it costs him.
- In the diversion case the man on the side track supplies nothing: his death is a cost of the diversion, which he bears.

The limits of beneficence are limits on supplying. A person need not give her kidney or her life for others, even when they would gain more. The limits are not limits on bearing: bearing the incidental costs of other people's permissible pursuits is governed by the balance of harms, as the diversion case shows.

The two halves of the literature need exactly this move.
- **The criterion literature** (Ramakrishnan, Quong, Walen) has a test with no explanation.
- **The rationale literature** (Quinn, Tadros, Alexander, Cullity) has an explanation. It falls to Quong's dilemma because it lacks an independent test.

The replacement test is that independent test. It identifies supplying without mentioning "use," so the explanation is not circular. It also leaves the diversion case out, so the explanation is not too broad.

## 3. Why this is the sharpest version

- **One principle, one test, one bound.** Quong's version needs use, a counterfactual absence test, a rescue proviso for omissions, and an account of property to settle trolley cases. Ramakrishnan's needs a prior account of claims and admits no explanation. The Spending View needs the replacement test and the duty bound. Rescue rights, the property results, and the Loop verdicts all follow from it.
- **It is derived, not listed.** Each clause comes from the function (P1) or the permission not to give (P3), which answers the custodian's brief that the version be "responsive to what the Means Principle is supposed to do." The functions the corpus assigns to the principle (Reading Notes F1–F6) are each served:
  - F1, the puzzling pairs: sorted by the replacement test.
  - F2, doing and allowing: omissions are covered.
  - F3, limits on lesser-evil justification and prerogatives: the principle is what gives the permission not to give its force.
  - F4, liability to be used: this is owing a supply.
  - F5, war, punishment, strikes, research: terror bombing and placebo controls fall under it.
  - F6, status: persons are not resources.
- **Weaker modality where it suffices.** The thesis says the principle *forbids spending*. It does not say that spending is the only wrong in the vicinity.

## 4. Central argument (Display 2, in §3)

> **The argument.**
> (P1) The Means Principle exists to keep morality from treating persons as resources for others.
> (P2) To treat something as a resource for others is to treat the question whether a good it can supply them is worth what supplying it costs it as a question for others to settle.
> (P3) Beyond what a person owes, that question is hers to settle, and she settles it by agreeing or refusing; within what she owes, her duty has already settled it.
> (P4) Others settle it for her exactly when they impose on her, or let her suffer, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good.
> (C) So the Means Principle should forbid imposing on a person, or letting her suffer, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear: it should forbid spending her.

**Where each premise is defended** (named in the text right after the display):
- **P1:** common ground, with citations: Quinn's ideal of persons as existing only for purposes they can share; Ramakrishnan's "people are not resources for others' benefit" (file 01 L215); Quong's independence. Stated in §1 and §3, not argued at length.
- **P2:** §2. The log burned for heat versus the tree felled because it stands where the road will go (the essay's one illustration). Then persons.
- **P3:** §3. The permission not to give, and why it must be more than a permission to refuse. If others could take whenever the gain was large enough, the decision would not be hers. This is the argument Thomson 2008 (pp. 364–366) runs too broadly, and that Tadros 2011 and Cullity 2018 restrict to using. The essay's addition is that its correct scope is supplying.
- **P4:** §2. The replacement test, and checking that it sorts *Room* and *Heart Attack* and the diversion and footbridge cases.

**Argument labels.** P1–P4 only (the essay refers back to them). No other labels.

### §1 Introduction (700)

- **Hook (paired-cases opening, with "Compare:").** Two clinical trials.
  - In the first, there is too little of a new drug for everyone. Patients are assigned by lottery, and those who do not receive it serve as controls.
  - In the second, a proven drug is available for all, but some patients are given a placebo so that they can serve as controls.
  - Research ethics accepts the first design and forbids the second, except where going untreated risks no serious harm. (verify #11, #18)
  - In both, patients go untreated and their data serve others. What is the difference?
- **Bridge to the literature.** The Means Principle is meant to explain differences like this. The literature's version of the pair is Ramakrishnan's *Room* and *Heart Attack*, told in 2–3 sentences each in §2, or here if space allows.
- **The two tracks** (one paragraph).
  - The criterion track: Quong's use of body or property; Ramakrishnan's usefulness relation, which he suspects is primitive, "ultimately a principle about causal structure" (file 01 L269).
  - The rationale track: Quinn, Tadros, Alexander, Cullity, which Quong's dilemma defeats.
  - Each fails for want of the other.
- **Thesis, compressed**, then the scoped statement, naming the two nearest rivals (Quong; Ramakrishnan) in the same paragraph. The honest closeness line: for harms to persons the test is close to Ramakrishnan's. What is new is the explanation, and what the explanation brings with it.

### §2 Using and spending (1,000)

- **Resources and casualties (P2).** The log and the tree, in 3–4 sentences. The one illustration; it restates and does not argue.
- **Display 1** and its two glosses (the replacement test; supply relative to alternatives). Define the terms in plain words:
  - "contribution": body, its powers and position, time, capacities, holdings;
  - "loss": comparative.
- **The pairs:**
  - The footbridge (spending) vs the diversion (nothing supplied).
  - *Room* (his legs supply access, but his death comes from the shortage of medicine, so it is not the cost) vs *Heart Attack* (her untreated attack is the cost of the data her body supplies).
  - The man whose body shields twenty others from a diverted trolley. The twenty are safe either way, so their safety is not a respect in which diverting is better. This shows why "supply" is judged against the alternatives.
- **Using without spending, and spending without using.**
  - Using without spending: *Room*; a wrench used to divert a trolley toward its owner; a body that holds down a failsafe button.
  - Spending without using in the ordinary sense: letting someone die so that her body yields data.
  - So the principle's target is not use.

### §3 Why spending matters (1,200)

- **Display 2 and the defense locations.**
- **P3.** The permission not to give (kidney, life), and why it must be more than a permission to refuse. If others may take whenever the gain is large enough, the decision whether to give is not hers but theirs. (Non-hollowness; Mack 2005 makes a general version: verify #12, or footnote only.)
- **Thomson's argument** (file 13, pp. 364–366). She holds that a bystander may not make another "pay the cost of his good deed" when he would not pay it himself, and concludes that the bystander may not divert. Quote it.
  - Tadros (file 03 L85–101) and Cullity (file 11 fn 45) reply that the argument applies at most to using. Credit them.
  - The essay's addition: the argument's correct scope is *supplying*. The man on the side track pays a cost of the good deed but gives nothing. The man on the footbridge gives something, and his death is its cost.
- **Quong's dilemma** (the "attack one premise" architecture). Restate it validly with a quote (file 09 L125–127).
  - Horn one rejects "if Y is not duty bound to bear C for G, it is wrong to impose C on Y for G" because it condemns the diversion case.
  - Deny the premise that the limits of duty are limits on bearing. They are limits on supplying, and the diversion case imposes no cost of supply.
  - Horn two (tautology) fails because the replacement test identifies supplying without mentioning use, and P3 is a claim about beneficence, not about harming.
- **Why not all harms** (the generalization worry). My permission not to divert a trolley onto myself does not imply that others may not divert it onto me. What I may decline to give, others may not take. What I may decline to do to myself, others may sometimes do to me as a side effect.
- **The arbitrariness worry answered.** The replacement test matters morally because it tells us whether a person is being made to give. Evidence that the line is not an artifact of trolley cases: the two trials. The patient who would otherwise be treated is made to give; the patient under scarcity loses out. Research ethics draws exactly this line, in the words "forgo treatment they would otherwise receive" (verify #11).

### §4 Omissions and holdings (850)

- **Rescue without prior rights.** Parry's sliding man vs two independent trolleys (told plainly; source: Parry 2023, after Tadros 2011; file 06 L67–105).
  - Parry shows that Quong needs a right to rescue that the man lacks, and calls for a "quasi-causal difference ... more fine-grained than counterfactual dependence" (L105).
  - The replacement test is that difference. If his stopping power came from elsewhere, he would be rescued, so letting him slide spends him. The two-trolley victim supplies nothing.
  - The principle thus helps fix rescue rights instead of presupposing them.
- **Holdings.**
  - Quong's unity demand (life jacket vs driftwood; file 09 L101–111) is met. The jacket's owner drowns as the cost of what her jacket supplies; the loser of the driftwood race supplies nothing.
  - Parry's wrench cases (L171–195) come out right. The owner of the wrench does not die as the cost of the wrench's service, so diverting toward him is an ordinary side-effect case, and there is no three-track absurdity.
  - Parry's externality objection (L231–259) is answered. Acquiring something changes others' protections only where the owner's loss would be the cost of the thing's service, and what one owes grows with what one holds.
- **The bound.**
  - Easy rescue: a vase smashed to reach a stuck switch is spent within duty, and redress is owed.
  - Liability: a person liable to be killed to stop her own attack does not thereby owe the use of her suffering to extract information from another (after Tadros 2011; told plainly, one sentence; verify the case source in the corpus).

### §5 The loop (800) — the dedicated objection section

- **The objection in its strongest form.** Choo 2025 (verify #3): diverting in Loop seems permissible, every version of the principle forbids it, and no defence succeeds. Quote him. Note the divided intuitions: a roughly 50:50 split (Hauser via Walen fn 32 and Cullity fn 48) and context effects (Liao et al. 2012, verify #13).
- **Stipulation block.** Thomson's own case (verify #10; cf. file 07 L189): if the trolley goes straight, the five's bodies stop it, so it "will therefore not reach the one."
- **Analysis.**
  - Diverting spends the one.
  - Not diverting spends the five: their bodies supply his safety, and a buffer before them would spare them.
  - Every option spends someone. The principle excludes each supplied good as a reason for imposing its cost, but the spent parties' claims remain and conflict.
  - Conflicting claims of the same kind are weighed, and the numbers count, as in the diversion case. So diverting is permissible in Thomson's case.
- **The asymmetric loop.** If the one would be safe were the trolley to go straight, not diverting spends no one, so diverting is forbidden. It is on a par with the footbridge. Walen 2014 agrees (file 07 L223); Kamm disagrees (verify #9). Otsuka's position must be checked (verify #4).
- **Credit and concession.** Costa 1987 and Walen 2014 reach the symmetric verdict by other routes (file 07 fn 34, L201–217). Concede that anyone with a firm intuition that asymmetric loops are permissible will find the view costly. Reply by parity with a trapdoor footbridge.
- **Hypothesis** (flagged as such). The standard telling leaves implicit whether the one is threatened, which fits the divided intuitions.
- **Mutual shields.** Leave out unless space allows; hold for a referee response.

### §6 Two further objections (550)

- **(a) Intentions.** A rescuer who would lose an arm refuses to save a sliding man. For the arm's sake the refusal is permissible; in order that his body stop the trolley it is wrong (Parry 2023, file 06 L121). The same holds for Christensen's *Streets*, told plainly (file 05 L211). How the view explains this:
  - P3 makes the permission not to give an authority over one's *own* sacrifice.
  - It is exercised only by acting on it.
  - A refusal made to exploit another's plight is no exercise of it.
  - The only other justification, the five's rescue, is disabled.
  - So motive matters exactly where a permission not to give is relied on. This keeps the principle objective for acts; compare Quong's view that a killer's bad motive does not make a justified defensive killing wrong (file 09 L299–305).
- **Footnote placeholder:** Liao & Barry's charge that victim-centred views presuppose the principle, and their malicious-reason cases (verify #2). The objective view answers the malicious-reason cases as Quong does; the prerogative point covers the rest. Promote to the text only if #2 shows the charge bites.
- **(b) Catastrophe.** Duties grow with the stakes, so the principle yields where duty yields. Whether a person can owe her life to save a million is a question the principle inherits, not one it must settle. This contrasts with Ramakrishnan's discount and with absolute versions.

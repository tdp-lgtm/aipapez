## 0. Verdict in brief

1. **Keep the core idea; change what the essay claims for it.** The core idea: the Means Principle forbids *spending* persons, not using them.
2. **The rationale is not new in kind.**
   - Tadros 2011, Mack 2005, Alexander 2016 and Walen 2022 each hold that the principle protects people from being made to serve others beyond what they owe.
   - Alexander says outright that the principle and the permission not to help are two sides of one thing (file 25 L83, and fn 16 at L95).
   - So the essay must present the rationale as inherited and credit it up front.
3. **What is new is the criterion.** The new reading strengthens it. The criterion is a way of telling when a person is made to serve that does not presuppose the principle.
   - It sorts the test cases that defeat every rival criterion: Liao & Barry's six, Walen's two, and *Six Behind One*.
   - It is exactly what the inherited rationale lacks. Quong's dilemma defeats that rationale only because it has no such criterion.
4. **So the essay's centre of gravity moves.** It moves from "a new rationale for the principle" to "the criterion the best rationale needs, and the version of the principle that results."
5. **Loop is no longer a point in the view's favour.** The literature and all the survey data concern the asymmetric version, and there the view forbids diverting. The essay should accept that verdict briefly, and defend it by argument rather than by data. Thomson's original, symmetric case goes to a footnote.
6. **Recommendation: Option A (§4).** Options B and C are genuine alternatives that trade breadth for depth.

## 2. Originality, recalibrated

**No longer claimed:**
- the rationale family, or the link between the permission not to help and the principle;
- the structure for omissions;
- the symmetric Loop verdict;
- the verdicts on holdings taken one by one.

**Claimed:**

- **(a) The criterion.** A loss counts as spending when it is the cost of what the person supplies. Two features fix this:
  1. **Replacement, not absence.** We ask what would happen if her *contribution* came from another source, not what would happen if *she* were absent.
  2. **Supply judged against the agent's alternatives.**

  The second feature is not an ad hoc clause. The principle limits what can justify an act, and justifications are contrastive: a reason for doing A is a way in which A beats the other options. So "the good she supplies" must be a way in which the act beats the agent's alternatives. This one point explains why every absence-based baseline fails: the agent cannot choose the victim's absence, so comparing with it measures nothing that could justify the act. (Quong and Øverland use absence baselines; Alexander counts benefits "causally derived" from use.)

  The criterion is close to Ramakrishnan's usefulness relation for harms to persons, and the essay says so.
- **(b) The fit.** This criterion is the account of "being made to serve" that the Tadros–Mack–Alexander rationale has lacked, and it does not presuppose the principle. With it the rationale escapes Quong's dilemma, and its scope comes out right:
  - omissions are in (*Heart Attack*; the sliding man);
  - *Room*, the wrench, the button and *Six Behind One* are out.
- **(c) The corrections that follow:**
  - Alexander on *Six Behind One*;
  - Quong's bitten bullets (*Switch with Button*, *Overdetermined*, *Three-Track*);
  - the victim-centred views on Liao & Barry's cases;
  - Kamm on Loop.
- **(d) Doing versus serving, stated precisely.** The permission not to make great sacrifices protects two things: what a person must *do*, and what *of hers* must serve others.
  - Others can violate the second without her doing anything, and they do so exactly by spending her.
  - A side-effect harm does neither: she is not made to act, and nothing of hers serves.
  - That is why the permission requires a restriction on spending and none on side-effect harm. This answers Thomson and Steinhoff.
  - Tadros's contrast between serving a goal and being harmed as a side effect partly anticipates this. The claim here is the precise version, which the criterion makes available.

**Verdict: OPEN, narrowed.** The niche is now "the criterion the rationale needs," not "the rationale."

**Other directions considered and rejected:**

**Verdict: keep the core idea; revise the essay's claims and weighting.** There are three ways to do that.

## 4. The options

### Option A (recommended): the criterion the rationale needs ("Spending Persons," refocused)

**Thesis.** The Means Principle forbids spending persons, not using them: imposing on someone, by act or omission, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear. This gives the familiar view that the principle protects people from being made to serve others its missing piece: an independent test. The test gets right the cases on which the rival tests fail.

**Loop, in outline.**
- The version the debate concerns is the asymmetric one. The permissibility data are strong; report them fairly (Choo).
- The view forbids diverting. Relative to not diverting, the one's body supplies the whole of the good, and a stopper placed before him would spare him. Kamm's analysis ("a new problem" that his body merely prevents) fails for the reason Otsuka and Choo give: the agent has one goal, saving the five.
- **Why accept the verdict:**
  - (i) Otsuka's argument that Loop cannot be told apart from a loop that topples a man from a bridge, or from a ramp. Choo does not engage it.
  - (ii) If diverting were permitted, the protection against being made to serve would lapse exactly where the whole benefit comes from the victim's body.
- Concede the cost.

**Strengths.** Best fit to the brief (a new version of the principle, derived from what the principle is for). Its originality rests on the part that survived the reading. High significance: it settles live disputes on three fronts.

**Risks.**
- Crowding. Each case gets 2–3 sentences, and some cases move to footnotes.
- The doing-versus-serving joint in §3 must be argued, not asserted.

### Option B: the criterion alone ("What the Victim Gives")

**Thesis.** Every victim-centred version of the principle needs a baseline for what the victim contributes. The standard baselines each fail on known cases:
- her absence (Quong, Øverland, Walen 2014);
- the agent's entitlements (Walen 2016);
- the causal history of the benefit (Alexander).

The right baseline is the agent's own alternatives, with her *contribution* replaced rather than her person removed. The reason: the principle restricts justifications, and justifications are contrastive.

**Strengths.** The sharpest originality per word. Very testable. Engages Liao & Barry, Walen, Øverland, Quong, Alexander, Otsuka, Kamm and Ramakrishnan closely.

**Risks.**
- Narrower significance.
- Thinner on "what the principle is supposed to do," which the brief stresses. The rationale shrinks to a paragraph.
- Judges may read it as a technical repair.

### Option C: the rationale made precise ("What the Permission Not to Help Protects")

**Thesis.** The permission not to make great sacrifices for others protects two things: what a person must *do*, and what *of hers* must serve. Others can violate only the second without her acting, and they do so exactly by spending her. So the permission requires one restriction on others, the Means Principle, and nothing broader. This answers:
- Thomson 2008 (her argument runs through the "doing" side);
- Mack (his restrictions are too broad);
- the hybrid view that has the permission without restrictions;
- Steinhoff.

**Strengths.** High significance (in general normative theory: why permissions bring restrictions, and which ones). It answers "what the principle is for" most directly.

**Risks.**
- The most anticipated of the three (Tadros, Mack, Alexander, Walen 2022).
- It needs sources not on disk (Scheffler 1982; Kagan 1989).
- The criterion gets little space, so there are fewer demonstrations.

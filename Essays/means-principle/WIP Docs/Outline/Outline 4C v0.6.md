## §1 Introduction (650; 5 ¶)

- **¶1.1 The pair.** "Compare two cases."
  - *Smoke.* A house is burning, and five people are trapped in an attic that is filling with smoke. The only way out is a trapdoor that falls shut unless something holds it open. An unconscious neighbour lies directly beneath it. His body is the only thing in reach to prop it against, and propping it against him does him no harm. But once the trapdoor opens, the smoke will pour down onto him, and there is no time to move him. The firefighter props it against him. The five climb down, and he dies of the smoke.
  - *Wedge.* The same, except that a roof vent draws the smoke off, so none will reach him, and the trapdoor is too heavy to prop. It stays open only if something is wedged beneath it, and the only thing there is his body, which its weight will crush. She wedges him, and the five climb down.
  - Verdict, with one light hedge: permissible in *Smoke*; wrong in *Wedge*.
- **¶1.2 The puzzle, and the burden.**
  - In both cases his body holds the trapdoor, he dies, five are saved, and he is killed rather than let die. So neither use, nor harm, nor killing marks the difference.
  - Support for *Smoke* that needs no theory. Without the prop, *Smoke* is the diversion case: a threat to five, let out onto one. The prop changes nothing for him: had a beam been in reach she would have used it, and he would have died all the same. Adding a use that makes no difference to what happens to someone cannot turn a permissible act into a wrong one.
  - The burden: Quong's version forbids harming that "involves using [someone's] body" (file 09 L119), and he accepts that a case of this structure is a violation (L219–233). The essay argues that he need not.
- **¶1.3 Quinn's idea, and two tracks.**
  - The idea is Quinn's. Those "usefully involved in the promotion of a goal only at the cost of something protected by their independent moral rights ... ought, prima facie, to serve the goal only voluntarily", except where they have "strong moral obligations" to serve (file 12 L250–254; verify #6).
  - Two tracks have developed it since.
    - Criteria: Quong's use-plus-absence test; Ramakrishnan's usefulness relation, which he takes as primitive (file 01 L237) and fears is "ultimately a principle about causal structure" (L269).
    - Rationales: being made to serve beyond what one owes (Tadros 2011; Mack 2005; Alexander 2016); and Quong's independence under distributive justice (file 09 L135–137), which attaches to use.
  - Each track fails for want of what the other has. Quong's dilemma catches the rationale (L125–127).
- **¶1.4 Thesis.**
  - Compressed: the principle forbids spending persons, not using them.
  - Scoped: it forbids imposing on someone, or letting her suffer, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear. In *Wedge* a beam would spare him; in *Smoke* it would not.
  - Rivals: against Quong; beyond Ramakrishnan, whose relation is close for harms to persons.
  - Credit: the idea is Quinn's, and the rationale is his and his successors' (Tadros, Mack, Alexander, Walen 2022). What is new is the test that makes the idea precise, and what follows from it.
- **¶1.5 Scope and roadmap.**
  - Not all constraints on harming: driving over a man lying on the only road is not spending.
  - Rescue duties are inputs; there is no theory of property; harmless use (a sleeping stranger used as a pillow) is out, since the principle concerns losses.
  - Roadmap: §2 the test; §3 why it is the right one; §4 omissions and holdings; §5 objections, chiefly *Loop*.

## §2 Using and Spending (1,300; 10 ¶)

- **¶2.1 Resources and casualties (P2).**
  - A log burned for heat is a resource: those who burn it decide whether its heat is worth its being burned. A tree felled because it stands where the road will go, and left where it falls, is a casualty: nothing it supplies is weighed.
  - That is what it is for something to be a resource for us: whether its service is worth what it costs it is ours to decide. It is also exactly what persons are said not to be. Quinn's persons exist "only for purposes that [they] can share" (file 12 L297). Quong's have "a sphere of independence where [their] decisions are sovereign" (file 09 L135).
  - Note: Kamm's contrast between substitution and subordination (file 20, pp. 165–166).
- **¶2.2 Display 1** (the Spending View), then plain definitions:
  - *contribution:* body, its parts and position, time, labour, holdings;
  - *loss:* a setback she would not suffer under the alternative;
  - *for the sake of that good:* where that good is what would justify the loss. This concerns justification, not motive, except in ¶5.6;
  - *the bound:* what she owes; what she agrees to bear.
  - Note: consent is an exception, not the test (Kahn, file 08 L9).
- **¶2.3 The replacement test.** Her loss is the cost of her supplying a good if, were what she contributes provided from some other source, the good could be had without the loss.
  - Footbridge: a stopper before him would spare him.
  - The pair: a beam would spare him in *Wedge*, not in *Smoke*.
  - The test replaces the contribution, not the person.
- **¶2.4 Why not absence.**
  - Quinn tested his idea by asking whether the agent would still need the victims' cooperation if they had shelters or medicines of their own (file 12 L238–244). His successors ask what the agent could do in the victim's absence (Quong; "Opportunism", file 01 L121).
  - Absence tests fail. Ramakrishnan's patient lies on the only pill that would cure five, so the five could be saved in her absence. Yet leaving her heart attack untreated so that tests on her can run is still wrong (file 01 L127–129). Holding her presence fixed, the replacement test condemns it.
  - *Is the test just another counterfactual?* Yes, but it varies only the source of her contribution, never her presence or position, so it never has to say what fills an empty place.
  - Note: Walen abandons his own absence baseline (file 14 L161–217).
- **¶2.5 Judged against what the agent could otherwise do.**
  - A justification is a reason for one option rather than another. So what she supplies must be a respect in which the act is better for others than an alternative, judged respect by respect.
  - Six people stand behind the man on the side track, and his body will shield them from the diverted trolley. They are safe if the agent does not divert, so their safety is no respect in which diverting is better: permissible. In *Loop*, by contrast, the whole improvement comes from his body (§5).
  - Credit: Otsuka, who draws the contrast by intention (file 27 L93–105); Ramakrishnan's twenty-behind case (file 01 L201).
  - Note: the several-alternatives rule.
- **¶2.6 Correcting Alexander.** His accounting excludes "benefits ... causally derived from usings" (file 25 L167), and so forbids the six-behind case (L57). It measures against the man's absence, which the agent cannot choose.
- **¶2.7 The canonical pairs.**
  - Footbridge vs diversion.
  - Ramakrishnan's pair, told plainly: the legs holding the door (file 01 L133) are not spending; the untreated patient (L35) is.
  - This is the opening pair's distinction, now for letting die.
- **¶2.8 The stress test.** Two of Liao & Barry's cases against the victim-centred views:
  - a man on a roof whose place a log would otherwise fill: forbidden;
  - a man blocking a door behind which fifty are safe either way: permissible.

  The test sorts both with no stipulation about empty places. The absence-based views fail on them (file 29 L165–213). Note: Øverland.
- **¶2.9 Use is neither necessary nor sufficient.**
  - Using without spending: *Smoke*; the legs holding the door; the wrench (§4).
  - Spending without harmful use: the untreated patient. The tests are harmless; being left untreated so that they can run is what kills her.
- **¶2.10 "Ramakrishnan renamed?"** The test is close to his relation for harms to persons, under which the justification becomes "substantially weaker" (file 01 L153). Four differences:
  1. the contrastive ground, of which his would-be defeater (L275) is a special case;
  2. an explanation from an independently accepted permission (§3), where he takes the principle as primitive (L237);
  3. holdings (Quong's objection to him, file 09 L111);
  4. a disabler bounded by duty, not a discount.

## §3 Why Spending Matters (1,300; 9 ¶)

- **¶3.1 Transition and Display 2.**
  - The test sorts the cases, but why should the principle track it? (Ramakrishnan's worry; Steinhoff's scepticism.) If a principle's best version is the one that does its job, no more and no less, the question is what its job is.
  - Display 2:
    - P1: the principle exists to keep morality from treating persons as resources for others at their own expense.
    - P2: to treat something as a resource for others at its expense is to treat the question whether a good it can supply them is worth what supplying it costs it as a question for others to settle.
    - P3: beyond what a person owes, that question is hers to settle, and she settles it by agreeing or refusing; within what she owes, her duty has already settled it.
    - P4: others settle it for her exactly when they impose on her, or let her suffer, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good.
    - C: so the principle should hold that the good a person's contribution would supply cannot justify imposing on her, or letting her suffer, the loss that supplying it would cost her, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear. That is, it should forbid spending her.
  - Defence locations:
    - P1: common ground (¶2.1; Alexander L79).
    - P2: the analysis in ¶2.1.
    - P3: inherited (¶3.2).
    - P4: the test (§2), and why only spending (¶3.5–3.7).
- **¶3.2 P3, inherited, and why it must be more than a permission to refuse.**
  - No one need give her kidney, or her life, even when others would gain more.
  - *The kidney rule* (self-written). Imagine a rule that let a hospital take a kidney from anyone whose kidney would save a life, while leaving everyone free to refuse to donate. No one would say it respects the permission not to donate: every refusal stands, and the kidney is taken anyway. Note: compare Harris's "survival lottery" (verify).
  - Why the rule offends:
    1. The permission's point is that a person is "a free agent leading her own independent life" (Walen 2016, file 14 L301), with "a sphere of independence where her decisions are sovereign" (Quong, file 09 L135).
    2. Whether her body serves others at a cost to her is part of that life. A permission that others could override by taking would leave it at their disposal, and would decide only who does the taking.
  - Within what she owes, duty has settled the question. Quinn's exception for "strong moral obligations" (file 12 L253–254) is the bound.
  - Credit: Mack (file 19 L117–167); Alexander (file 25 L83, fn 16); Walen 2022 (file 22 L47); Tadros (file 03 L277). Note: Lazar (file 18 L9).
- **¶3.3 Quong's dilemma** (file 09 L125–127). As "if Y is not duty bound to bear some cost, C, for the sake of goal, G, then it is wrong to impose C on Y for the sake of G", the rationale condemns the diversion case. As "compelled to serve", defined through use, it is circular.
- **¶3.4 Deny the shared premise.**
  - The permission limits what she must supply and what she must do, not what she must bear. The diversion case imposes no cost of supply.
  - The second horn fails: the test speaks of sources, goods and costs, never of use. This also meets Liao & Barry's charge that such views "sound very much like the MP" (file 29 L291).
  - Quong's own rationale, rights that "block" usefulness as a reason (file 09 L135–137), has the right structure but the wrong object: use, not supply at a cost.
- **¶3.5 Doing versus serving.**
  - The permission protects what she must do and what of hers must serve. Others cannot override the first without making her act (coercion does, and is forbidden for that reason). They override the second exactly by spending her: the kidney rule is that override.
  - A side-effect harm does neither. Its loss is not the cost of anything of hers serving: either nothing of hers serves (the diversion case), or the loss would fall on her all the same were what serves supplied from elsewhere (the smoke in *Smoke*). So it leaves the permission's object untouched, and falls under the ordinary constraint against harming, which weighs harms.
  - *Why not all harms* (Mack, file 19 L261–277)? A side-effect harm overrides nothing the permission protects: others may divert a trolley onto her that she need not divert onto herself.
  - The complaints differ. The spent victim is taken from, and the value of what was taken cannot answer that. The side-effect victim is harmed, and that complaint is weighed.
- **¶3.6 Thomson 2008** (pp. 364–365).
  - Her argument: the bystander who would not turn the trolley onto himself may not make another "pay the cost of his good deed", as one may not steal money to give to Oxfam.
  - Reply: the thief makes another's money serve; the bystander's refusal concerns an act of his own. The argument slides from serving to doing.
  - Credit: Tadros (file 03 L85–101); Cullity (fn 45).
- **¶3.7 Steinhoff.**
  - His question: is using a body worse than discarding it (file 16 L107)?
  - Yes. Using her at the cost of what she supplies makes something of hers serve an end she may refuse.
  - His alternative, a right to decide "in pursuit of which ends her body is to suffer adverse side effects" (L119), would forbid the diversion case.
- **¶3.8 The arbitrariness worry** (file 01 L267–277). Why should "location in causal space" matter, and in so fine-grained a way: a would-be defeater prevented, a shortage (L275)?
- **¶3.9 Reply.**
  - The fine grain is what the test for being made to give requires: the contrast removes the twenty-behind case, and replacement removes the shortage cases.
  - Giving is a structural notion: to give is to be the source of what another receives. A moral notion with a causal condition is not arbitrary on that account.
  - Optional: research ethics (verify #11).

## §4 Omissions and Holdings (600; 5 ¶)

- **¶4.1 The sliding man** (Parry, file 06 L67; after Tadros 2011, p. 122).
  - A man slides toward a trolley bound for five. His body would stop it, and he would be paralysed. A passer-by could easily stop him, and refrains so that his body stops the trolley.
  - Contrast two trolleys, one bound for a man and one for five.
  - Parry: Quong needs a prior rescue right, which the five's claims extinguish (L89), so the principle must inform rights (L103).
- **¶4.2 The view.**
  - Letting him suffer the loss so that his body serves settles, for him, whether it serves at that cost, just as imposing the loss would. Stopping him was easy, and she declines so that his body will serve. That is the kidney rule by omission: no one asks him, and his body serves the five at his cost all the same. So P4 covers omissions. A compulsion account cannot say this, since failing to aid compels no one (Ramakrishnan on Tadros, file 01 L107–117).
  - The good his body would supply cannot count against rescuing him, so the duty of easy rescue stands. In the two-trolley case nothing of his is supplied.
  - Credit Alexander's structure (file 25 L151; p. 261 per Parry fn 12, verify).
  - Notes:
    - Alexander resists the verdict, denying any duty to shield others with one's body "even at little or no cost" (L161). That is a dispute about rescue duties, which are an input (Hecht, file 15 L75–77).
    - Harmless omissions spend nothing (file 01 L109).
- **¶4.3 Holdings.** Quong's unity demand (file 09 L99–111): taking a swimmer's life jacket spends her, since another jacket would spare her. Winning the race to the driftwood does not.
- **¶4.4 The wrench and externalities.**
  - Using the owner's wrench to turn the trolley toward him (file 06 L171–195): he dies of the trolley, not as the cost of the wrench's service, so there is no three-track absurdity.
  - Externalities (L231–259): holdings enlarge others' duties only where the owner's loss would be the cost of their service, and duties grow with holdings.
  - Note: Walen's tightness test (file 22 L79).
- **¶4.5 The bound.** A vase smashed to reach a stuck switch is spent within the duty of easy rescue; redress is owed.

## §5 Objections (1,000; 7 ¶)

- **¶5.1 *Loop*, and what Choo establishes.**
  - In the version at issue, the one would be safe if the trolley went straight (file 28 L102; file 17 L121).
  - Grant Choo everything he shows:
    - the judgment that diverting is permissible survives controls for order effects;
    - it holds when *Loop* is seen with no case before it (means 4.19/6 and 4.74/6; 81%; file 28 L153);
    - it is no illusion of inattention, since those who hold it see that the man is used (L193–195).
- **¶5.2 The verdict: forbidden.**
  - Relative to not diverting, the five's survival depends on his body, and a stopper before him would spare him. This is the footbridge's structure.
  - Kamm's rescue fails. She calls the looping trolley "a new problem" that his body merely prevents (file 21 L61–65, pp. 94–95), but the agent has one goal, and his body supplies all of it (file 27 L91, L105; file 28 L267–275).
  - §2's contrast keeps the six-behind case permissible.
  - Note: only the part of a good that is supplied is disabled. A loop that would return to only one of the five leaves four lives to justify diverting. The point is noted, not developed.
- **¶5.3 Why the theory should prevail here.**
  - Otsuka links *Loop* to his Bridge Case, a footbridge variant in which a lever moves a pole that topples a man into the trolley's path (file 27 L13). That it is wrong to topple him is "a moral fixed point" for non-consequentialists (L125). He goes by two routes, each with one case in between:
    1. *Loop-Bridge.* The diverted trolley, as it circles back, itself trips the pole (L107). It differs from *Loop* only in moving the man into the trolley rather than the trolley into the man, a difference Kamm herself argues is insignificant (her Lazy Susan case, L119). And it is "morally indistinguishable" from the Bridge Case, since in both "you push a lever that causes someone to fall from the bridge" (L125).
    2. *Ramp.* Opening a drawbridge sends the trolley up a ramp into a man on a footbridge overhead (L145). It differs from the Bridge Case only in that same way, and appears "completely morally indistinguishable" from *Loop* (L151).

    Otsuka doubts that any principle separates these cases (L159; L163 n. 25).
  - Choo answers Otsuka's general claim: those who judge *Loop* permissible see the use, and trust that judgment over the principles that condemn it (file 28 L185–195). Neither point reaches the chain. It needs no one to miss the use, and it sets the *Loop* verdict against a verdict on a case, not against a principle. Choo does not discuss *Loop-Bridge* or *Ramp*.
  - So one set of firm judgments must yield. The principle that explains the wider set, and has an independent rationale (§3), forbids diverting.
  - This is the one place the view overrides a robust intuition, and the cost is conceded. Company: Otsuka; Walen; Ramakrishnan on reflection (file 01, fn at L249); Liao; McMahan (file 17 L122).
  - Notes:
    - Walen 2016's retraction (file 14 L369–383), his later claim to space (file 22 L59–61), and his forfeiture view (L107–123);
    - Thomson's symmetric original (file 23 L133–149; Costa, verify #21);
    - the order effects (file 28 L137–151).
- **¶5.4 The person in the path.**
  - Objection: the view permits driving over a man lying on the only road to save others.
  - Reply: it is silent. He supplies nothing; what the agent needs is his absence.
  - Other constraints forbid it: doing and allowing where a new threat is created; a claim to the space one occupies (note to ¶5.3); Quinn's and Kamm's accounts (per file 29 L101–141). The principle is one constraint among several.
  - Notes: Ramakrishnan's cave case (file 01 L227–233); Steinhoff's parking pair (file 16 L107–109).
- **¶5.5 Intentions: the case** (file 06 L121). A rescuer willing to lose an arm to save a sliding man sees five further down the track, and refrains so that his body stops the trolley. Refusing a rescue that costs an arm is permitted, yet this refusal seems wrong.
- **¶5.6 The explanation (conditional).**
  - Suppose the permission not to give is a standing to favour oneself, used in choosing, as P3's "hers to settle" suggests. Then, where conduct would otherwise spend someone, the permission justifies it only if it is acted on.
  - Her refusal, made for what his body will supply, is not acted on it, and the five's rescue is disabled. Agent-neutral justifications justify whatever the motive, so motive matters only here.
  - Liao & Barry's malicious diverter (file 29 L143–163) acts permissibly on the view. This is a stated disagreement.
  - Notes:
    - Christensen's passer-by (file 05 L209–211) is spending by omission: the homeless people's suffering supplies deterrence, she would otherwise help, and she acts on no permission of her own. So the view agrees that it is wrong.
    - A spiteful donor who declines to give to charity spends no one: the needy supply nothing, and their suffering is the object of her spite, not the cost of anything they give. So she acts permissibly, if viciously.
    - Tadros 2020.
- **¶5.7 Catastrophe.** Duties grow with the stakes, so the principle yields where duty yields. Whether one can owe one's life to save a million is inherited, not settled here. Contrast Ramakrishnan's discount (file 01 L153).

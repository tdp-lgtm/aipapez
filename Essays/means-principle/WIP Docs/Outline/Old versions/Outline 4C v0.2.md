## §1 Introduction (650; 5 ¶)

- **¶1.1 The pair.** "Compare two cases."
  - *Smoke.* A house is burning. Five people are trapped in the attic above a trapdoor that falls shut unless something holds it open. An unconscious neighbour lies directly beneath it. His body is the only thing in reach to prop it against, and propping it against him does him no harm. But once the trapdoor opens, the smoke gathered in the attic will pour down onto him, and there is no time to move him. The firefighter props it against him. The five climb down, and he dies of the smoke.
  - *Wedge.* The same, except that no smoke will reach him and the trapdoor is too heavy to prop. It stays open only if something is wedged beneath it, and the only thing there is his body, which its weight will crush. She wedges him, and the five climb down.
  - Verdict, with one light hedge: permissible in *Smoke*; wrong in *Wedge*.
  - Footnote: *Smoke* has the structure of Quong's "Switch with Button", which he counts a violation (file 09 L219–233).
- **¶1.2 The puzzle.** In both cases his body holds the trapdoor, he dies, five are saved, and he is killed rather than let die. So neither use, nor harm, nor killing marks the difference. Yet the most developed version of the principle forbids harming that "involves using [someone's] body" (Quong, file 09 L119), and both cases do that.
- **¶1.3 The two tracks.**
  - Criteria: Quong's use-plus-absence test; Ramakrishnan's usefulness relation, "ultimately a principle about causal structure" (file 01 L269).
  - Rationale: the principle protects people from being made to serve others beyond what they owe (Quinn; Tadros 2011; Mack 2005; Alexander 2016).
  - The criteria lack a reason (L237). The rationale lacks a test and falls to Quong's dilemma (file 09 L125–127).
- **¶1.4 Thesis.**
  - Compressed: the principle forbids spending persons, not using them.
  - Scoped: it forbids imposing on someone, or letting her suffer, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear.
  - Applied to the pair: in *Wedge* a beam would spare him; in *Smoke* it would not.
  - Rivals: against Quong; beyond Ramakrishnan, whose relation is close for harms to persons.
  - Credit sentence: the reason is inherited (Quinn, Tadros, Mack, Alexander, Walen 2022); the test is new.
- **¶1.5 Scope and roadmap.**
  - Not all constraints on harming: driving over a man lying on the only road is not spending.
  - Rescue duties are inputs, and there is no theory of property.
  - Nor harmless use: a sleeping stranger used as a pillow is wronged, if at all, on other grounds, since the principle concerns losses.
  - Roadmap: §2 the view and its test; §3 why it is the right target; §4 omissions and holdings; §5 objections, chiefly *Loop*.

## §2 Using and Spending (1,300; 10 ¶)

- **¶2.1 Resources and casualties (P2).** A log burned for heat is a resource: what it supplies is weighed against what supplying costs it. A tree felled because it stands where the road will go is a casualty: nothing it supplies is weighed. The principle keeps persons from being treated as the log (P1).
- **¶2.2 Display 1** (the Spending View), then plain definitions: contribution (body, its parts and position, time, labour, holdings); loss (a setback she would not suffer under the alternative); spending; the bound (what she owes; what she agrees to bear). Footnote: consent enters as an exception, not as the test. Kahn 2024 argues against consent accounts of the mere-means prohibition (file 08 L9).
- **¶2.3 The replacement test.** Her loss is the cost of her supplying a good if, were what she contributes provided from some other source, the good could be had without the loss.
  - Footbridge: a stopper placed before him would spare him.
  - The pair: a beam would spare him in *Wedge*, not in *Smoke*.
  - The test replaces the contribution, not the person.
- **¶2.4 Why not absence.** Absence tests ask what the agent could do were she absent. But something else would then stand where she stood, and the verdict turns on what it is. Liao & Barry: a man on a roof where a log would otherwise lie; a man on a platform where a crate would stand (file 29 L165–213). The replacement test holds her presence fixed.
  - *Is it just another counterfactual?* Yes, but it varies only the source of her contribution, holding fixed her presence, her position and the agent's act. So it never has to say what would fill an empty place.
  - Footnote: Walen abandons his own absence baseline for the same reason (file 14 L161–217).
- **¶2.5 Judged against what the agent could otherwise do.**
  - A justification is a reason for one option rather than another. So what she supplies must be a respect in which the act is better for others than an alternative.
  - Six people stand behind the man on the side track, and his body will shield them from the diverted trolley. They are safe if the agent does not divert, so their safety is no respect in which diverting is better: permissible. Contrast *Loop* (§5).
  - The comparison goes respect by respect. Diverting's overall advantage depends on his body, since without it the trolley would hit the six. But the respect in which diverting is better, the five's safety, does not.
  - Credit: Otsuka, who draws the contrast by intention (file 27 L93–105); Ramakrishnan's twenty-behind case (file 01 L201).
  - Footnote: the several-alternatives rule (4B §0).
- **¶2.6 Correcting Alexander.** His accounting excludes "benefits ... causally derived from usings" (file 25 L167), and so forbids the six-behind case (L57). It measures the benefit against the man's absence, which the agent cannot choose. Only her real alternatives measure what could justify her act.
- **¶2.7 The canonical pairs.**
  - Footbridge vs diversion: in the diversion the five are saved by the trolley's leaving their track, and the man on the side track supplies nothing.
  - Ramakrishnan's pair, told plainly:
    - a man whose legs hold open the door to five dies because the medicine goes to them (file 01 L133). Not spending.
    - a patient left untreated so that tests on her can run in time dies (L35). Spending.
  - The pair's distinction, now for letting die.
- **¶2.8 The stress test.** Liao & Barry's cases against the victim-centred views:
  - the man on the roof: forbidden;
  - a man blocking a door behind which fifty are safe either way: permissible;
  - a man whose weight on a button keeps a diversion from reaching ten: permissible.

  The test sorts all three with no stipulation about what fills an empty place. Øverland's view and Walen's first view do not (footnote).
- **¶2.9 Use is neither necessary nor sufficient.**
  - Using without spending: *Smoke*; the legs holding the door; the wrench (§4); a woman whose body holds down the switch's button on the side track, where the trolley kills her anyway. Quong counts that last case a violation (file 09 L219–233).
  - Spending without harmful use: the untreated patient.
- **¶2.10 "Ramakrishnan renamed?"** For harms to persons the test is close to his relation, under which the justification becomes "substantially weaker" (file 01 L153). Four differences:
  1. the contrastive ground, of which his would-be defeater (L275) is a special case;
  2. an explanation, where he offers none (L237);
  3. holdings (Quong's objection to him, file 09 L111);
  4. a disabler bounded by duty, not a discount.

## §3 Why Spending Matters (1,200; 9 ¶)

- **¶3.1 Transition and Display 2.** The test sorts the cases, but why should the principle track it? (Ramakrishnan's arbitrariness worry; Steinhoff's scepticism.) Display 2 (P1–P4 → C), with defence locations:
  - P1 is common ground (Ramakrishnan L215; Alexander L79);
  - P2 is §2;
  - P3 comes next;
  - P4 is §2 and ¶3.5–3.7.
- **¶3.2 P3, inherited.**
  - No one need give her kidney, or her life, even when others would gain more.
  - The permission must be more than a permission to refuse. If others could take whenever the gain was large enough, her refusal would settle only who takes.
  - Within what she owes, duty has settled the question: the bound.
  - Credit: Mack (file 19 L117–167); Alexander (file 25 L83, fn 16); Walen 2022 (file 22 L47); Tadros (file 03 L277).
- **¶3.3 Quong's dilemma** (file 09 L125–127). Read as "if Y is not duty bound to bear some cost, C, for the sake of goal, G, then it is wrong to impose C on Y for the sake of G", the rationale condemns the diversion case. Read as "compelled to serve", defined through use, it is circular.
- **¶3.4 Deny the shared premise.** The permission limits what she must supply and what she must do, not what she must bear. The diversion case imposes no cost of supply. And the second horn fails, because the replacement test speaks of sources, goods and costs, never of use.
  - This also meets Liao & Barry's charge that victim-centred views, pressed to say why using a body is worse, give answers that "sound very much like the MP" (file 29 L291). The test says what spending is without saying that it is wrong.
- **¶3.5 Doing versus serving.**
  - The permission protects what she must do and what of hers must serve.
  - Others cannot override the first without making her act; coercion does that, and is forbidden for that reason.
  - They override the second exactly by spending her.
  - A side-effect harm does neither, so the permission requires a restriction on spending only.
  - *Why not all harms, as Mack's argument suggests?* A side-effect harm overrides nothing the permission protects. That is why others may divert a trolley onto her that she need not divert onto herself. Footnote: Mack (file 19 L261–277).
- **¶3.6 Thomson 2008** (pp. 364–365).
  - Her argument: a bystander who would not turn the trolley onto himself may not make another "pay the cost of his good deed", just as one may not steal money to give to Oxfam.
  - Reply: the thief makes another's money serve. The bystander's refusal concerns his own act. The argument slides from serving to doing.
  - Credit: Tadros's reply (file 03 L85–101); Cullity (fn 45).
- **¶3.7 Steinhoff.**
  - His question: is using a body worse than discarding it (file 16 L107)?
  - Yes. Using her at the cost of what she supplies makes something of hers serve an end she may refuse.
  - His alternative entitlement, to decide "in pursuit of which ends her body is to suffer adverse side effects" (L119), would forbid the diversion case. His parking pair adds a person in the path (§5).
- **¶3.8 The arbitrariness worry** (file 01 L267–277). Ramakrishnan finds a person's "location in causal space" a strange thing to matter. It is stranger still in fine grain: a would-be defeater prevented; a shortage (L275).
- **¶3.9 Reply.** The fine grain is what the test for being made to give requires:
  - the contrast removes the twenty-behind case;
  - replacement removes the shortage cases.

  What looked arbitrary is what separates being made to give from losing out. Optional: research ethics (verify #11).

## §4 Omissions and Holdings (600; 5 ¶)

- **¶4.1 The sliding man.** A man slides toward a trolley bound for five; his body would stop it, and he would be paralysed. A passer-by could easily stop him, and refrains so that his body stops the trolley (Parry, file 06 L67; after Tadros 2011, p. 122). Contrast two trolleys, one bound for a man and one for five, where she can stop only one. Parry: Quong needs a prior rescue right, which the five's claims extinguish (L89), so the principle must inform rights (L103).
- **¶4.2 The view.** Letting him suffer the loss so that his body serves settles, for him, whether it serves at that cost, just as imposing the loss would. So P4 covers omissions. A compulsion account cannot say this, since failing to aid compels no one (Ramakrishnan on Tadros, file 01 L107–117). Harmless omissions spend nothing: in Ramakrishnan's drowning case (L109) she suffers no loss. The good his body would supply cannot count against rescuing him, so the duty of easy rescue stands. In the two-trolley case nothing of his is supplied, and the numbers count. Credit Alexander: a duty to rescue "is not canceled by the setback to C" (file 25 L151; p. 261 per Parry fn 12, verify). Modularity: rescue duties are an input. Footnote: Hecht (file 15 L75–77).
- **¶4.3 Holdings.** Quong's unity demand (file 09 L99–111). Taking a swimmer's life jacket spends her, since another jacket would spare her. Winning the race to the driftwood does not. One principle covers persons and holdings.
- **¶4.4 The wrench; externalities.**
  - Using the owner's wrench to turn the trolley toward him (file 06 L171–195): he dies of the trolley, not as the cost of the wrench's service. So there is no three-track absurdity.
  - Externalities (L231–259): holdings enlarge others' duties only where the owner's loss would be the cost of their service, and duties grow with holdings.
  - Footnote: Walen's tightness test (file 22 L79).
- **¶4.5 The bound.** A vase smashed to reach a stuck switch is spent within the duty of easy rescue; redress is owed.

## §5 Objections (1,100; 7 ¶)

- **¶5.1 *Loop*: stipulation and data.**
  - In the version the debate concerns, the one would be safe if the trolley went straight (file 28 L102; file 17 L121).
  - Grant the data: when *Loop* is shown first, most judge diverting permissible (file 28 L153: means 4.19/6 and 4.74/6; 81%).
  - The question is whether that judgment should stand.
- **¶5.2 Verdict: forbidden.** Relative to not diverting, the five's survival depends on his body, and a stopper before him would spare him.
  - Kamm: the looping trolley is "a new problem" that his body merely prevents (file 21 L61–65, pp. 94–95).
  - Reply: the agent has one goal, and judged against not diverting his body supplies all of it (file 27 L91, L105; file 28 L267–275).
  - §2's contrast keeps the six-behind case permissible.
- **¶5.3 Why accept it.**
  1. Otsuka's loop that topples a man from a bridge, and his ramp, cannot be told apart from *Loop*, and in them the use is plain (file 27 L107–125, L145–151). Choo does not engage this.
  2. §3: the protection would lapse exactly where the victim's body supplies the whole benefit.
  - Cost conceded, with company: Otsuka; Walen 2016 (file 14 L369–383); Ramakrishnan on reflection (file 01 fn at L249); Liao; McMahan (file 17 L122).
  - Footnotes: Thomson's symmetric original (file 23 L133–149; Costa, verify #21); the order effects (Choo L137–151).
- **¶5.4 The person in the path.**
  - Objection: the view permits driving over a man on the only road to save others.
  - Reply: it is silent. He supplies nothing; what the agent needs is his absence, not anything of his. The same holds for anyone merely in the way. Ramakrishnan's person blocking a cave exit is dislodged, not made to serve (file 01 L227–233).
  - Other constraints forbid the road case: doing and allowing where a new threat is created; a claim to one's space (file 22 L59–61); Quinn's and Kamm's accounts (per file 29 L101–141).
  - Verdict: the principle is one constraint among several.
- **¶5.5 Intentions: the case.** A rescuer willing to lose an arm to save a sliding man sees five further down the track, and refrains so that his body stops the trolley (file 06 L121). Refusing a rescue that costs an arm is permitted, yet this refusal seems wrong.
- **¶5.6 The explanation.**
  - P3 makes the permission not to give an authority over one's own sacrifice, exercised only by acting on it.
  - A refusal made for what his body will supply is no exercise of it, and the five's rescue is disabled. So motive matters only where a permission of this kind is relied on.
  - Liao & Barry's malicious diverter (file 29 L143–163) acts permissibly on the view: a stated disagreement.
  - Footnote: Walen 2022 (file 22 L107–123); Christensen (file 05 L209–211); Tadros 2020.
- **¶5.7 Catastrophe.** Duties grow with the stakes, so the principle yields where duty yields. Whether one can owe one's life to save a million is inherited, not settled here. Contrast Ramakrishnan's discount and Walen's threshold outside "the space of rights" (file 22 fn 17).

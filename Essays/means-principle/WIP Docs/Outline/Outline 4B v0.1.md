## 0. Settled at 4B: terms and the several-alternatives rule

These fix the content of Display 1. The essay states them in plain words in §2, with no symbols.

- **Contribution.** Something of hers that a good can come from: her body (its parts, weight, position), her time and labour, her holdings.
- **Supplying (relative to an alternative A).** Her contribution supplies good G, relative to A, when both of these hold:
  - G is one of the respects in which the act is better for others than A;
  - G obtains under the act partly because of her contribution.

  This is judged respect by respect, not by the act's overall advantage. That is what keeps the six-behind-the-one case permissible. Without the man's body the diverted trolley would hit the six, so diverting's *overall* advantage depends on him. But the six are safe under the alternative too, so their safety is not one of the respects in which diverting is better.
- **Loss.** A setback she suffers under the act and would not suffer under A. It is comparative, so a loss she would suffer either way is no loss relative to A.
- **Cost of supplying (the replacement test).** Her loss is the cost of her supplying G if, were her contribution provided from some other source, G could be had without that loss. "Some other source" is existential: any source, anywhere. Her presence and position are held fixed; only the source of the contribution varies.
- **Spending.** Imposing on her, or letting her suffer, a loss that is the cost of her supplying G, where G is relied on to justify the act over A.
- **Bound.** Beyond what she owes (duties of rescue, justice, liability) or agrees to bear.
- **Several alternatives.** An act that imposes on her a loss relative to some alternative A must be justified over A by reasons that do not consist in goods she supplies relative to A. This must hold for every such alternative, permissible or not.
  - *Test case, for a footnote:* one can stop the trolley with a woman's body; divert it onto two; or do nothing.
  - Pushing her beats diverting on an undisabled ground: the two survive, which does not depend on her.
  - But relative to doing nothing, pushing is justified only by the five's survival, which her body supplies.
  - So pushing is forbidden, while diverting onto the two remains permitted. This is the familiar deontological pattern, and the view inherits it; it is not a new cost.

## 1. Introduction (650)

**Section claim.** A simple pair of cases defeats the explanation that the principle forbids harmful *use*. The essay's view explains the pair, and gives the familiar rationale the test it has lacked.

1. **The pair** ("Compare:"). Self-written; final wording at 4C.
   - *First case:* a firefighter props a trapdoor open with an unconscious neighbour's body, which does him no harm. She has time to carry out the five upstairs or him, not both. She carries out the five, and he dies of the smoke.
   - *Second case:* there is time for everyone, but the trapdoor holds only if his body is wedged under it, which will crush him. She wedges him, and the five escape.
   - Verdicts, with one light hedge: the first seems permissible, the second not. A footnote credits the first case's model, Ramakrishnan's door-holding case (file 01 L133).
2. **The puzzle.**
   - In both cases she uses his body to save the five, and in both he dies. So "harming through use" cannot be what the principle forbids.
   - Quong's version forbids harming that "involves using Y's body or other things over which Y has a rightful prior claim" (file 09 L119). That is the natural reading, and the pair already strains it.
3. **The two tracks** (one paragraph).
   - *Criterion track:* Quong's use-plus-absence test; Ramakrishnan's usefulness relation, which he fears is "ultimately a principle about causal structure" (file 01 L269).
   - *Rationale track:* the principle protects people from being made to serve others beyond what they owe (Quinn; Tadros 2011; Mack 2005; Alexander 2016).
   - Quong's dilemma catches the rationale for want of a test (file 09 L125–127). The criteria lack a rationale.
4. **Thesis, compressed then scoped.**
   - *Compressed:* the principle forbids spending persons, not using them.
   - *Scoped:* it forbids imposing on someone, or letting her suffer, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear.
   - The nearest rivals are named in the same paragraph (Quong; Ramakrishnan), with the closeness line: for harms to persons the test is close to Ramakrishnan's.
5. **Credit sentence.** The reason is inherited; the test is new (Plan v0.4 §1).
6. **Scope sentences.**
   - Not all constraints on harming: the person in the path of the agent's means is left to other constraints.
   - Rescue duties are taken as inputs.
   - No theory of property.
7. **Roadmap** (4 sentences).

**Sub-arguments (2).**
- (a) The pair holds use and death fixed, so any account that keys the principle to use alone mis-sorts it. Example: the pair. Source: Quong's formulation (file 09 L119).
- (b) Each track lacks what the other has. Sources: Ramakrishnan L237 and L267–277 (no explanation; the arbitrariness worry); Quong L125–127 (the dilemma).

**Objections placed here:** the preemptive caveats (step 6).

## 2. Using and Spending (1,300)

**Section claim.** Spending is the relation the puzzling cases track. It is identified by replacing the victim's contribution and comparing the agent's alternatives, and so it succeeds where tests that compare with the victim's absence fail.

1. **Resources and casualties (P2).**
   - A log burned for heat is a resource: what it supplies is weighed against what supplying costs it.
   - A tree felled because it stands where the road will go is a casualty of the road: nothing it supplies is weighed.

   It restates P2; it argues nothing.
2. **Display 1** and its two glosses, with the terms of §0 in plain words.
3. **Replacement, not absence.**
   - (a) *Footbridge:* a stopper placed before the man would spare him, so his death is the cost of what his body supplies.
   - (b) *Why not absence:* removing the person changes more than her contribution, because something else then stands where she stood. Absence tests reverse their verdicts with what fills the place.
     - Liao & Barry: a man on a roof where a log would otherwise lie; a man on a platform where a crate would otherwise be (file 29 L165–213).
     - Walen's massive man who, were he absent, would be replaced by a moose (file 14 L161–217, footnote).
   - (c) *Micro-objection:* "the replacement test is a counterfactual too." Reply: it varies only the source of her contribution, holding her presence and position fixed. So the question it asks has an answer in exactly the cases where the absence question does not.
4. **Supply judged against what the agent could otherwise do.**
   - (a) *Ground:* the principle limits what can justify an act, and a justification is a reason for one option rather than another. So the good she supplies must be a respect in which the act beats an alternative.
   - (b) *Illustration:* six people stand behind the man on the side track, and his body would shield them from the diverted trolley. If the agent does not divert, they are safe anyway. So their safety is no part of what makes diverting better, and nothing he supplies figures in the justification. Permissible. Contrast *Loop*, where the whole of the improvement over not diverting comes from his body.
   - (c) *Credit and correction:*
     - Otsuka drew this contrast through intention (file 27 L93–105).
     - Ramakrishnan's twenty-behind case is the same shape (file 01 L201). He handles it as "preventing the obtaining of a would-be defeater" (L275), which the contrast explains.
     - Alexander's accounting forbids diverting in the six-behind case (file 25 L57). It measures the benefit against the man's absence, which the agent cannot choose.
   - (d) *Footnote:* the several-alternatives rule and its test case (§0).
5. **The cases sorted.**
   - (a) *The opening pair.* In the second case a beam in his place would spare him, so it is spending. In the first he would die of the smoke whatever held the trapdoor, so it is not.
   - (b) *Footbridge vs diversion.* In the diversion the five are saved by the trolley's leaving their track, so the man on the side track supplies nothing.
   - (c) *Ramakrishnan's pair, told plainly and credited.*
     - The man whose legs hold the door open dies because the medicine goes to five others (file 01 L133): not spending.
     - The patient left untreated so that tests can be run on her in time dies as the cost of what her body supplies (file 01 L35): spending.
6. **Stress test.** Liao & Barry's cases against victim-centred views (file 29 L165–213), told plainly:
   - the man on the roof: forbidden;
   - the man blocking a door behind which fifty are safe either way: permissible;
   - the man whose weight on a button keeps a diversion from reaching ten: permissible.

   The test gets each right, with no stipulation about what fills an empty place. The views they target (Øverland's; Walen's first) get some of them wrong (footnote).
7. **Using without spending, and spending without harmful use.**
   - (a) *Using without spending:*
     - the first opening case;
     - the door-holding legs;
     - a wrench used to turn the trolley toward its owner (§4);
     - a woman whose body holds down the button that makes the switch work, on the side track where the diverted trolley will kill her anyway. Quong counts this a violation (file 09 L219–233); on the view it is permissible.
   - (b) *Spending without harmful use:* the untreated patient. The tests are harmless; what kills her is being left untreated so that they can run.
   - (c) So the principle's target is not use.
8. **Pre-emptive defence against deflation** ("Ramakrishnan renamed"). Grant the closeness for harms to persons. Then name four differences:
   - the contrastive ground (step 4), of which his would-be-defeater point is a special case;
   - the explanation (§3), where he offers none (file 01 L237);
   - holdings (§4): his principle concerns persons only, which Quong presses with the life jacket (file 09 L111);
   - a disabler bounded by duty (§4), where he has a discount (the justification becomes "substantially weaker", L153).

**Sub-arguments (4):**
- replacement beats absence (step 3);
- contrast is required by justification (step 4);
- the test sorts the canonical and the stress-test cases (steps 5–6);
- use is neither necessary nor sufficient (step 7).

**Objections placed here:** deflation (step 8); "the test is just another counterfactual" (step 3c).

## 3. Why Spending Matters (1,200)

**Section claim.** The familiar rationale, the permission not to give, explains why the principle should forbid spending and nothing broader. The test is what lets it do so without circularity or overreach.

1. **Display 2**, with the defence locations named (Plan v0.4 §4).
2. **P3, inherited and credited.**
   - (a) A person need not give her kidney, or her life, even when others would gain more (the limits of beneficence; common ground).
   - (b) The permission must be more than a permission to refuse. If others could take whenever the gain was large enough, whether she gives would not be hers to decide: her refusal would only settle who performs the taking. Credit:
     - Mack (subordination "by others", file 19 L117–167);
     - Alexander (the MP and the prerogative as "logical corollar[ies]", file 25 L83, fn 16 at L95);
     - Walen 2022 (the victim's claim as the "patient-analog" of the claim not to make large sacrifices, file 22 L47);
     - Tadros (file 03 L277).
   - (c) Within what she owes, her duty has already settled the question. This is the source of the bound (§4).
3. **Quong's dilemma** ("attack one premise").
   - (a) *Reconstruct it validly, quoting* (file 09 L125–127):
     - The duty rationale says: "if Y is not duty bound to bear some cost, C, for the sake of goal, G, then it is wrong to impose C on Y for the sake of G". Read that way, it condemns the diversion case.
     - Read instead as "compelled to serve", defined through use, it is a tautology.
   - (b) *Deny the premise:* the limits of beneficence limit what a person must supply, and what she must do. They do not limit what she must bear. The diversion case imposes no cost of supply, so no question of beneficence arises there.
   - (c) *Horn two fails:* the replacement test identifies supply by sources, goods and costs. "Use" and "means" never appear in it.
4. **Doing versus serving** (the defence of P4's "exactly").
   - (a) The permission protects two things: what a person must *do*, and what *of hers* must serve.
   - (b) Others cannot override the first without making her act; coercion does that, and is forbidden for that reason. They override the second exactly by spending her. A side-effect harm does neither: she is not made to act, and nothing of hers serves.
   - (c) *Thomson 2008* (pp. 364–365).
     - Her model is the Oxfam donor who steals someone's money instead of giving his own. That is a case of serving: the stolen money serves.
     - Her target is the bystander who would not turn the trolley onto himself. That is a case of doing: what he may decline is an act of his own.
     - So her principle ("A must not kill B to save five if he can instead kill himself") slides from the second object to the first, and yields no restriction on turning the trolley onto the one.
     - Credit Tadros's reply (file 03 L85–101: bearing a cost "in the service of a goal" vs "simply as a side effect") and Cullity (fn 45).
   - (d) *Steinhoff.* He asks whether "using her body is worse than discarding her body" (file 16 L107).
     - Using her at the cost of supply makes something of hers serve an end she may refuse. Discarding does not.
     - The entitlement he offers instead, to determine "in pursuit of which ends her body is to suffer adverse side effects" (L119), would forbid the diversion case.
     - His parking pair mixes in a person in the path of the agent's means (§5b).
5. **The arbitrariness worry** (Ramakrishnan, file 01 L267–277).
   - (a) *His worry:* a principle about "causal structure", and "fine-grained" at that. His examples: the would-be defeater in his twenty-behind case; the shortage in the door-holding and six-heart-attacks cases (L275).
   - (b) *Reply:* the fine grain is exactly what the test for being made to give requires.
     - The contrast removes the twenty-behind case: their safety is no respect in which the act is better.
     - Replacement removes the shortage cases: the loss is not the price of what she gives.
     - So the features that looked arbitrary are the ones that separate being made to give from losing out.
   - (c) *Optional, only if #11/#18 arrive:* research ethics draws the same line (verify #11).

**Sub-arguments (4):**
- the inherited permission must be more than a permission to refuse (step 2b);
- the dilemma dissolves once the permission's object is supply (step 3);
- only spending overrides what the permission protects (step 4);
- the fine grain is principled (step 5).

**Objections placed here:**
- Quong's dilemma;
- Thomson's generalization;
- Steinhoff;
- Ramakrishnan's arbitrariness worry;
- Mack-style over-generalization (answered by step 4, with a footnote).

## 4. Omissions and Holdings (600)

**Section claim.** The scope that §3 derives covers letting suffer and holdings, and the duty bound shapes both.

1. **Omissions.**
   - (a) *The pair, told plainly* (Parry, file 06 L67–105; adapted from Tadros 2011, p. 122, per Parry fn 13):
     - A man slides toward a trolley bound for five. His body would stop it, and he would be paralysed. A passer-by could easily stop him, and refrains so that his body stops the trolley.
     - Contrast two trolleys: she can stop only one, one bearing down on a man, the other on five.
   - (b) *Parry's challenge:* on Quong's view the sliding man lacks the prior rescue right the principle is meant to protect, since the five's claims extinguish it as they do in the two-trolley case (L81–97; "extinguished", L89). So the principle must inform rights, not only track them (L103).
   - (c) *The view:* the disabler applies to the reasons that fix rescue duties. The five's gain from his body cannot count against rescuing him, so the ordinary duty of easy rescue stands. In the two-trolley case he supplies nothing, so the numbers count.
   - (d) *Credit and modularity:*
     - Alexander has this structure: a duty to rescue "is not canceled by the setback to C" when the setback comes only from the victim's not being used (file 25 L151; p. 261 per Parry fn 12, verify). His examples are special duties.
     - The view creates no duty to aid; it removes one reason against meeting a duty. Rescue duties are an input (footnote: Hecht 2023, file 15 L75–77).
2. **Holdings.**
   - (a) *Quong's unity demand* (file 09 L99–111). Two people in the open ocean:
     - One takes another's life jacket, and she drowns. The jacket supplies his survival, and her drowning is the cost of its doing so, since another jacket would spare her. Spending.
     - One reaches a piece of driftwood first. Nothing of hers supplies his survival. Not spending.

     One principle covers persons and holdings.
   - (b) *Parry's wrench cases* (file 06 L171–195):
     - Using the owner's wrench to turn the trolley toward him is not spending, since he dies of the trolley, not as the cost of the wrench's service.
     - So there is no three-track absurdity, in which Quong's view makes killing three the only permissible option.
   - (c) *Parry's externality objection* (L231–259). Acquiring something enlarges others' duties toward one only where one's loss would be the cost of the thing's service, and what one owes grows with what one holds. Footnote: Walen's tightness test (file 22 L79) reaches similar verdicts on holdings by another route.
3. **The bound.** Smashing a stranger's vase to reach a stuck switch spends it within the duty of easy rescue, and redress is owed.

**Sub-arguments (3):** the disabler shapes rescue duties (step 1c); one principle covers holdings with the right reach (step 2); the bound (step 3).

**Objections placed here:** Parry's rights-first challenge (step 1b); the externality objection (step 2c).

## 5. Objections (1,100)

**Opening recall:** the view's verdicts are now on the table. The case that tests them hardest is Loop.

1. **(a) Loop (≈450).**
   - *Stipulation block:* in the version the debate and the studies concern, the one would be safe if the trolley went straight (Choo fn 12, file 28 L102; Liao et al. fn 2, file 17 L121).
   - *Concede-and-scope:* grant Choo's empirical premise in full. When Loop is shown first, most people judge diverting permissible (file 28 L153: means 4.19/6 and 4.74/6; 81% in Greene's report). Report the means, not Liao et al.'s percentages.
   - *Verdict: forbidden.* Relative to not diverting, the five's survival obtains only because his body stops the trolley, and a stopper placed before him would spare him.
   - *Kamm's rescue fails.* She calls the looping trolley "a new problem" that his body merely prevents (file 21 L61–65, pp. 94–95). But the agent has a single goal, saving the five, and judged against not diverting, his body supplies all of it (Otsuka, file 27 L91, L105; Choo, file 28 L267–275). §2's contrast is the objective form of this point, and it keeps the six-behind case permissible.
   - *Why accept the verdict:*
     - (i) Otsuka's argument that Loop cannot be told apart from a loop that topples a man from a bridge into the trolley's path, or from a ramp that launches the trolley into a man on a footbridge (file 27 L107–125, L145–151). Choo's replies (§3.1–3.3) do not engage it.
     - (ii) Point back to §3; do not restate it. The protection would lapse exactly where the whole benefit comes from the victim's body.
   - *Concede the cost.* Name the company: Otsuka; Walen 2016 (file 14 L369–383); Ramakrishnan on reflection (file 01 fn at L249); Liao 2009 and McMahan 2009, as listed by Liao et al. fn 3 (file 17 L122).
   - *Footnotes:*
     - Thomson's own, symmetric case (file 23 L133–149), where the view applies in both directions (Costa 1987, via Otsuka fn 7, file 27 L41; verify #21);
     - the order effects (Liao et al. 2012) and Choo's reply (file 28 L137–151).
2. **(b) The person in the path (≈150).** "The view permits driving over a man lying on the only road to save others."
   - Reply: it says nothing about that case, because he supplies nothing: he is in the way.
   - Other constraints forbid it: doing and allowing where a new threat is created; a claim to the space one occupies (Walen 2022, file 22 L59–61); Quinn's and Kamm's accounts, as Liao & Barry report (file 29 L101–141). Steinhoff gets a clause only.
   - Flat verdict: the principle is one constraint among several, and its scope is spending.
3. **(c) Intentions (≈300).**
   - *The case* (Parry, file 06 L121): a rescuer willing to lose an arm to save a sliding man sees five people further down the track, and refrains so that his body stops the trolley.
   - *The problem:* the objective view permits refusing a rescue that costs an arm, yet this refusal seems wrong.
   - *The view's explanation:*
     - P3 makes the permission not to give an authority over one's *own* sacrifice. It is exercised only by acting on it: refusing for the arm's sake.
     - A refusal made for the sake of what his body will supply is no exercise of it.
     - The only other justification, the five's rescue, is disabled.
     - So motive matters exactly where the justification is a permission of this kind, and nowhere else.
   - *Elsewhere the view is objective.* Liao & Barry's malicious diverter (file 29 L143–163) acts permissibly. State this as a known disagreement.
   - *Footnote:* Walen 2022's forfeiture view (file 22 L107–123); Christensen's passer-by (file 05 L209–211); Tadros 2020 on intentions.
4. **(d) Catastrophe (≈150).**
   - Duties grow with the stakes, so the principle yields where duty yields.
   - Whether a person can owe her life to save a million is a question the principle inherits, not one it must settle.
   - Contrast Ramakrishnan's discount, and Walen's threshold outside "the space of rights" (file 22 fn 17).

**Sub-arguments:** one per objection, each closed with a flat verdict.

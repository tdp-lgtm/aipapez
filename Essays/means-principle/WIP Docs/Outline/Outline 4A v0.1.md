## 1. The spine in one paragraph

The Means Principle forbids spending persons, not using them.
- §1 poses the puzzle with a self-written pair: a firefighter uses an unconscious neighbour's body in both cases, and he dies in both, yet only one case seems wrong.
- §2 states the view. It shows that its test resolves the pair, and that it sorts the hard cases on which the rival tests fail.
- §3 argues that spending is what the principle should target: the familiar rationale needs exactly this test, and with it escapes its standing objections.
- §4 shows the resulting scope at work on omissions and holdings.
- §5 meets the objections, chiefly Loop.
- §6 restates the result and its scope.

## 3. Dependencies (why this order)

- **P2 before Display 1.** The log and the tree give "resource" its sense before the view is displayed.
- **Test before rationale.** §3's answer to Quong's second horn (circularity) needs a test that identifies supply without mentioning use, so §2 must define the test first.
- **Early payoff.** The opening pair is resolved in §2, not deferred, so the reader gets the payoff of §1's puzzle within the first quarter of the essay.
- **P3 before its uses.**
  - The permission not to give is stated in §3, before §4's duty bound and §5(c)'s account of motives, which both rely on it.
  - §5(c) needs P3 in its "authority" form ("hers to settle ... she settles it by agreeing or refusing"). Display 2's P3 already has that wording.
- **Scope before §5(b).** The person in the path is scoped out in §1 and answered in §5(b), so the objection meets a stated boundary rather than a surprise.

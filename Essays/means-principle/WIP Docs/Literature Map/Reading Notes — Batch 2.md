## 14 — Walen 2016, "The Restricting Claims Principle Revisited: Grounding the Means Principle on the Agent–Patient Divide," *Law and Philosophy* 35: 211–247

**Status:** read in full (L1–422).

**Thesis.** He keeps the restricting claims principle (RCP) but swaps its baseline. The old counterfactual baseline asked what the agent could permissibly do if the patient were absent. The new "toolkit baseline" asks whether the patient's claim concerns what the agent may count as hers to use (then it is *non-restricting*, hence strong) or what she may do with her toolkit (then it is *restricting*, hence weaker) (L11, L197, L229).

**Grounding.** The "agent–patient divide" (L273–301), resting on two fundamental agent-rights:
- (i) "a strong claim not to have to make large sacrifices for the sake of others" unless special duties apply (L283);
- (ii) a right to control property, "first property (their bodies) and second property" (L285).

Agents first divide the world's resources; patient-claims then restrict agents relative to that division (L299–301). Key sentence (L301): using another is hard to justify because "the other is a free agent leading her own independent life. Insofar as she has no special duties to others, morality cannot treat her as though she may be pressed into duty to serve others at great expense to herself."

**Why the counterfactual baseline was dropped** (L161–217):
- *Sidetrack Man Protecting Others*: his weight works another switch that saves ten. The counterfactual test wrongly makes his claim non-restricting.
- *Massive Man Displacing a Moose*: were he absent, a moose could be used. The counterfactual test wrongly makes his claim restricting.
- Theoretical point: the counterfactual baseline presupposes the toolkit baseline. It is "a convenient crutch for intuition, a heuristic" (L331).
- He grants that counterfactual "credit" and "burden" carry *some* slight weight (L217, L323).

**Property.**
- *Drug Owner* (L85–87, L231): the drug is his and he needs all of it to live, so A may not take it. Destroying it as a side effect of a fire break is permitted.
- *Trolley Switch Tool* (L251–257): using the sidetrack man's tool is permissible. The harm of being hit "is not directly connected to the interest we normally take to be protected by a property claim over an object like a tool." Keep the interests separate: a non-restricting but easily outweighed claim over the tool, and a restricting claim not to be hit.
- Variant (L261–265): he owns the switch and needs it daily to reach his doctor. Walen: the agent may not use it, since the ownership interest is "directly connected with his interest in his life." He rejects as "perverse" the argument that it would not matter because he will be dead from the trolley anyway.
- The toolkit is not static: "empowering claims" of those in need can put others' property into the agent's toolkit (the trolley company's switch), but they lose to non-restricting claims that protect substantial interests such as the massive man's life (L233–245).

**Objections he answers:**
- the theory leans on property theory (L305);
- property rules are too flexible, answered by "first property" having "natural law priority" (L307);
- property is prioritized over life (L317);
- circularity (L355–357). His reply: the explanation appeals to foundational principles of the space of rights plus the negative-externality analog, not to the MP.

**The dilemma for the MP** (L101–123):
- *Subjective reading:* intention-based wrongs are the wrong kind of wrong. The malicious bystander in Sidetrack does a justifiable act for a bad reason (L113–115).
- *Objective reading:* causal role has "no obvious moral significance." He quotes Scanlon: "being a means in this sense – being causally necessary – has no intrinsic moral significance" (L121, citing *Moral Dimensions* 29).
- The RCP claims to explain why causal role matters, via agent freedom.

**Appendix: retractions.**
- **Loop** (L369–383). He now holds that the RCP *and* the MP forbid diverting in Loop.
  - On the toolkit baseline, "given that the possibility of inaction is in an agent's toolkit, it is no longer possible to get conflicting non-restricting claims."
  - His dialog: the one can say "you must be relying on my serving as the means of stopping the trolley." The agent can say to the five: "I would not choose to have you be hit as a means of saving the one; if I could lower a ladder down so that you could get out of the way, I would feel obliged to do so ... Instead, you are asking me to kill the one, and I have a right not to kill on your behalf."
  - Conclusion: "The conflict with intuition is a bullet both theories have to bite ... the symmetry of the case masks a deeper asymmetry." He cites Thomson's "extra bit of track" line ('The Trolley Problem' [1986 reprint] p. 102) as a source of misleading intuition, and Otsuka against Kamm (fn 56).
  - **So Walen 2014's symmetric-Loop verdict, which the Crux Memo and Plan v0.2 cite as agreement, is RETRACTED here.**
- **Rescue Ahead** (L385–415). Dr. A's train will kill a man trapped on the track unless she stops, which aborts the rescue of five. The RCP now treats his claim as restricting, so continuing is permitted. The intuition to the contrary is attributed to squeamishness about direct physical force, and he offers *Collision Course* against the right-of-way explanation. Bullet bitten.

**→ SV.**
1. **Originality: property.** Walen 2016 already restricts property protection by the connection between the harm and the property interest (*Trolley Switch Tool* permitted; *Drug Owner* forbidden). So Parry's objections to Quong do not hit Walen, and the SV's property results are *not* unique. What the SV adds is a cleaner criterion ("cost of supplying" instead of "interest directly connected"). The two diverge on the switch-owner variant: the SV permits, because his death comes from the trolley and not from losing the switch; Walen forbids. Decide whether the SV's verdict is defensible. It turns on the comparative notion of loss.
2. **Originality: rationale.** Walen's ground (L283, L301) includes the no-large-sacrifice principle, stated as not being "pressed into duty to serve others at great expense." This is close to the SV's P3 (the permission not to give). His explanatory work, however, is done by the toolkit/property baseline, not by the sacrifice principle, and he frames the no-sacrifice point as an *agent*-right (freedom). The SV's distinctive move remains: the object of that right is *supplying at a cost*, and the criterion comes from that object rather than from property.
3. **Loop.** The SV's symmetric verdict now *disagrees* with Walen 2016, which is good for originality but needs an answer to his ladder argument. Draft answer: the ladder shows only that the agent does not *want* the five hit. It does not show that her refusal to divert can be justified without relying on their shielding the one. Remove the shielding (thin five, trolley loops on) and the one dies whatever she does, and her "right not to kill on your behalf" loses its force. So the reason against diverting draws its force from the five's unowed supply. Walen's toolkit view counts inaction as always within the agent's toolkit. The SV denies that inaction is neutral whenever the loss it imposes is the cost of the victim's supply (*Heart Attack*; the sliding man). **This is the sharpest contrast between SV and RCP.**
4. **Rescue Ahead.** Like the RCP, the SV does not classify it as spending: the trapped man supplies nothing. The SV can leave the verdict to a doing/allowing principle (creating a new threat vs redirecting one) rather than to squeamishness. It is a scope point for the essay.
5. Walen's malicious-bystander argument (L113–115) supports the SV's objectivity and its limited role for motive (Plan §6(a)).
6. Walen admits counterfactual "credit/burden" has some weight (L217). The SV's replacement test is a counterfactual, but it is anchored in the victim's supply, not in her presence. The moose case shows the difference: the massive man's presence blocks the moose, but what he *supplies* is his body's stopping power, so the SV condemns toppling him, rightly. The sidetrack man who protects others: his body supplies the ten's safety only relative to a diversion that would otherwise go toward them. Relative to the agent's actual alternatives (divert toward him or not at all) it supplies nothing, so the SV permits. **The SV handles both of Walen's counterexamples to the counterfactual baseline**, a good point for §2.

## 29 — Liao & Barry 2020, "A Critique of Some Recent Victim-Centered Theories of Nonconsequentialism," *Law and Philosophy* 39(5): 503–526

**Status:** read in full (L1–355). The JSTOR front matter confirms venue, issue and pages (L5–7).

**Targets.** Øverland's theory of moral obstacles (TMO), Walen's original RCP (extensionally equivalent to the TMO), and Walen's revised version, RCP(R) (L39).

**Challenge A: morally significant distinctions** (L101–141).
- *Flamethrower* (Andy is behind Tom): permissible. *Flamethrower 2* (Bob is in front of Tom, so the flame passes through Bob's spot): impermissible.
- Foot's *Car Rescue* (driving over an immovable person to save oneself and 19): impermissible. Quinn explains it by the intended action of the car over the victim's spot (pp. 298–301 in the reprint pagination); Kamm by a "direct side-effect of the means" causally upstream of the good (*Intricate Ethics* p. 147).
- The TMO permits all of these, since Andy and Bob are both moral obstacles.

**Challenge B: the cost of denying that intentions matter** (L143–163).
- *Enemy Trolley* is a "may act" case in which the enemy diverts in order to kill Luke. Liao & Barry: impermissible.
- *Enemy 10,000 to 1* is a "must act" case, so the enemy must divert whatever his motive.
- Victim-centred views cannot condemn *Enemy Trolley*; Kamm's view cannot either (fn 29).

**Challenge C: specifying the counterfactual** (L165–213).
- *Flamethrower 3*: Andy blocks a door; were he absent, 50 would come through and die from the flame. The TMO says he is no obstacle, so killing him is impermissible. Wrong.
- *Rifle 2*: Frank is on a roof; were he absent, a log would be there. The TMO says he is an obstacle, so shooting him is permissible. Wrong.
- *Standard Trolley 2*: Joey's weight on a button keeps the redirection from sending the trolley on to ten. The TMO says impermissible. Wrong.
- *Move 2*: Kevin is on a movable platform; were he absent, a crate would be there. The TMO says permissible. Wrong.
- Øverland's repair (the counterfactual in which no one and nothing is in that place, *MO* pp. 489–90) narrows the theory and still fails on two more cases:
  - *Flamethrower 4*: Andy leans on the button that activates the flamethrower.
  - *Standard Trolley 3*: Joey stands on the button that activates the switch.
- Liao & Barry: the DDE gets all of these right "robustly," while the TMO is "ad hoc" (L213).

**Section IV** (L215–261). Against Walen's appeal to personal force they offer *Car Rescue 2* (a switch sends a self-driving car over the person): still impermissible, with no personal force involved. Against Walen's remark that "a claim not to be killed for a bad reason" is a different wrong: accepting it concedes that mental states matter.

**Section V: RCP(R)** (L263–333). Three objections:
1. **It cannot mark the difference without the MP.** "Why does the sidetrack man not have an interest 'in controlling the use of his property (his body)' *and* 'in protecting his life'? ... just why is it problematic to use the massive man's body? Here it becomes difficult to marshal a response that does not sound very much like the MP" (L291).
2. ***Trolley Switch Tool 2*** (L317–319). The tool was bought for daily trips to the doctor. Walen forbids using it. Liao & Barry: "intuitively, the agent's moral reasons against using the sidetrack man's tool in this case seem no more stringent than in Trolley Switch Tool." They also charge that Walen's appeal to what the owner *intended* the tool for is a mental-state criterion (L311).
3. **RCP vs MP distinctness** (L321–333). "Restricting claims lower the barriers" is equivalent to "non-restricting claims raise them." *Armor* does not separate the RCP from the MP.

**→ SV** (major).
1. **The replacement test passes every one of the counterfactual-specification cases** that sink the TMO and Walen's original RCP:
   - *Flamethrower 3*: permissible. The 50's safety is no respect in which flaming beats not flaming (they are safe either way), so Andy supplies nothing.
   - *Rifle 2*: forbidden. Frank's body supplies the stopping; if that came from elsewhere (the log), his death would be avoidable.
   - *Standard Trolley 2*: permissible. Same shape as *Tracks*.
   - *Move 2*: forbidden. A crate could supply the stopping, so his death is the cost.
   - *Flamethrower 4*: permissible. He supplies activation, but his death is not avoidable by activating it another way (the flame still passes behind Tom).
   - *Standard Trolley 3*: permissible, by the same reasoning.

   Why it works: the SV replaces the *contribution*, not the *person*, and it judges supply against the agent's alternatives, not against the person's absence. **This is the strongest single demonstration for §2.** The SV reaches victim-centred verdicts "robustly," in Liao & Barry's own sense, with no stipulated counterfactual.
2. ***Trolley Switch Tool 2*: the SV agrees with Liao & Barry against Walen.** His death is not the cost of the tool's service, so the MP is not engaged. No appeal to what the owner intended the tool for is needed, so the SV escapes their mental-state charge against the RCP(R).
3. **The "sounds like the MP" charge.** The SV answers their question "just why is it problematic to use the massive man's body?" without presupposing the MP. His death is the cost of what his body supplies, and beyond what he owes, whether his body is to supply that at that cost is for him to decide (P3). The sidetrack man supplies nothing, so no such decision of his is made for him. This is the SV's advertised explanatory advantage over the RCP(R).
4. **Car Rescue / Flamethrower 2 (the person in the path).** The SV, like the TMO and the RCP, does not classify these as spending. The essay must scope them out: the MP is not the only constraint. Liao & Barry themselves attribute the verdicts to Quinn's DDA-with-intention and to Kamm's causal structure, not to the MP. **Scope line for §1**: the essay claims to say what the Means Principle forbids, not everything that constrains harming. Constraints on harm caused by one's means upstream of the good (Kamm), or on newly created threats (Foot's rescue cases), are left open.
5. **Enemy Trolley.** The SV is objective, so the enemy acts permissibly (with Quong's *Murders Averted* and Walen). The prerogative point in Plan §6(a) does not apply, since the enemy acts on an agent-neutral justification. Mark it as a known disagreement with agent-centred views. The weight of Liao & Barry's objection is modest by their own account ("does not perhaps constitute a knock-down argument," L159).

## 22 — Walen 2022, "In Defense of Patient-Centered Theories of Deontology: A Response to Liao and Barry," *Law and Philosophy* 41: 627–638

**Status:** read in full (L1–171). He summarizes and updates *The Mechanics of Claims* (MOC, 2019; REQUESTS #16, not on disk).

**Updates.**
- **Terminology.** Non-restricting claims are now "property claims" (MOC p. 87; L19).
- **Rationale (L47).** "property claims are the patient-analog of an agent's claim not to have to make sacrifices for the sake of others to whom they owe no special duty of aid. As an agent, a person may be expected to make small sacrifices for the welfare of others, but respect for her autonomy requires that she not be treated as a mere tool for the production of the greater welfare. The same is true for her as a patient. She may be put in the toolbox of others ... if the costs to her are relatively small and the benefits to others great. But ... she may not be put in the toolbox of another if the cost to her would be grave."
- ***Rescue Ahead*** (L59–61). He now agrees with Liao & Barry that it is *impermissible*. He withdraws the squeamishness error theory (fn 12) and rests the verdict on "a property-like claim to the space he occupies, which the agent needs to use." Contrast: Switch needs only "the turnout," not the space where the sidetrack man stands.
- **Tightness / split-victim test** (L79, MOC p. 93). If the owner's death is "tightly connected to the use," the property claim takes on the strength of the interest in avoiding death. If the connection is loose ("one could imagine the use having a small effect on the owner and the ultimate harm [of death] befalling someone else"), only the small harm counts toward the property claim, and the death registers as a restricting claim.
  - *Drug Owner*: tight, so forbidden.
  - *Trolley Switch Tool*: loose, so permitted.
  - New *Trolley Switch Tool 2*: the sidetrack man needs the tool at that very moment to open an oxygen tank. Walen: forbidden, "whether we separate the case into two sidetrack men or leave it one" (L97).
  - He concedes that Liao & Barry were right about his 2016 version, in which the tool was needed later: permitted (L99).
- **Intentions** (L107–123). The malicious killer's act is "still permissible *qua* killing"; what is impermissible is "acting on a disrespectful reason," which is "a different and lesser wrong." By being willing to kill out of malice she "forfeits her claim to be free not to save the five."
- **Explanatory significance** (L125–143). Restricting claims push costs onto others, so fairness limits their strength, especially the dominance of negative restricting claims over positive ones. Property claims set the baseline, via the "agent–agent division" and the "agent–patient frontier" (quoting MOC p. 87). Fn 17: using a person at the cost of his life may be permissible at high stakes, but only "as a matter of threshold deontology, outside ... 'the space of rights'."

**→ SV** (major originality recalibration).
1. **The rationale is anticipated in spirit.** Walen's L47 already derives the strength of the claim not to be used from its parallel with the agent's claim not to have to make large sacrifices, bounded by small costs. That is P3–P4 of the SV in the language of property and autonomy. The SV's rationale can no longer be sold as new *in kind*. What remains distinctive:
   - (i) The SV locates the object of the protected decision in *supplying at a cost*, not in property or the toolkit. That makes the criterion non-moralized for bodies and time: no prior theory of who "owns" what is needed to classify *Room*, *Heart Attack*, or the loops.
   - (ii) The SV's criterion applies to omissions directly (next point).
2. **The tightness test ≈ the SV's "cost of supplying" for holdings.** Walen's split-victim test and the SV's replacement test agree on *Drug Owner*, *Trolley Switch Tool*, and the two-person version of *Trolley Switch Tool 2*. They diverge on the one-person *Trolley Switch Tool 2*, where he would be hit by the diverted trolley anyway:
   - The SV's comparative notion of loss says his death is not the cost of the tool's service, so the MP is not engaged.
   - Walen says forbidden.

   An edge case; footnote at most, or leave it out.
3. **Omissions and Loop (the key divergence).** Walen's toolkit counts inaction as always within the agent's toolkit. Positive claims are "restricting," so letting someone suffer is never a "property" violation unless it involves using his property or space. The SV instead counts letting someone suffer as spending whenever the loss is the cost of what she supplies (*Heart Attack*, the sliding man). Consequences:
   - (a) The SV condemns the sliding-man and *Heart Attack* cases on a single criterion. It is unclear how the RCP handles *Heart Attack*; check MOC (#16) if it arrives. Do not claim the RCP fails there.
   - (b) The SV vindicates Thomson's symmetric Loop, where Walen 2016 bites the bullet.
4. ***Rescue Ahead* / *Car Rescue*.** Walen now forbids them via a claim to space. The SV does not count them as spending. The essay should scope them out of the MP (Plan §1), noting that Walen, Liao & Barry, Quinn and Kamm all forbid them for different reasons.
5. **Structure.** Walen places high-stakes use outside "the space of rights" (threshold deontology). The SV's bound is set by duty, and duties can grow with the stakes. Minor contrast.

## 28 — Choo 2025, "The Loop Trolley Case Strikes Again! A Threat to the Doctrine of Double Effect and the Mere Means Principle," *PPR* 111(1): 195–215 (open access, CC BY)

**Status:** read in full (L1–384).

**Target and version.**
- Mere Means Principle: "It is impermissible to (harmfully) use someone as a mere means to bring about a greater good" (quoting Bruers & Braeckman 2014: 256; L56). Also the DDE.
- **He uses the ASYMMETRIC Loop** (fn 12, L102): "Thomson's version is symmetrical, where the track forms a perfect circle, such that the first track loops back to the large man ... The version I present is an asymmetric version." He chooses it because later philosophers (Kamm 2007 p. 122 fn 4; Otsuka 2008 p. 95 fn 7) and all the empirical studies (Greene et al. 2009; Hauser et al. 2007; Liao et al. 2012; Waldmann & Dieterich 2007) concern the asymmetric version.
- The Loop case is at Thomson 1985 pp. 1402–1403 (L88); the "extra bit of track" point is at p. 1403 (L96).

**Group 1: reject the Permissibility Verdict** (§3). Choo's three replies:
- **(3.1) Order effects.** Transfer is asymmetric: judgments after Bridge are suspect, others are not (Wiegmann et al.). The studies with no case shown first all favour permissibility:
  - Liao et al. Group C, mean 4.19/6;
  - Waldmann & Dieterich, 4.74/6 (Standard 4.85; Bridge 3.73);
  - Greene et al. 2009, 5.89/9;
  - Greene 2013 (pp. 220–221): **81% permissible** for Loop vs 87% for Standard (L153).
  - Philosophers meet both Standard and Bridge first, so they are unaffected (L155).
- **(3.2) Hauser's 56%.** There are framing problems: the man is compared to a heavy object, the trolley is only slowed, and there were no diagrams. And "even if ... 56% ... that would still be a problem" (L161–179).
- **(3.3) "The large man is a mere means"** (Otsuka; Bruers). This is dialectically ineffective: philosophers who treat Loop as a counterexample already recognize the use and still judge diverting permissible (L193–195).

**Group 2: accommodate the verdict within the DDE or the Mere Means Principle** (§4). All three attempts fail:
- **Kleingeld 2020** (a Kantian practical-reasoning construal): if Manuela can reason that way in Loop, she can in Bridge (L225–231).
- **Kaufman 2016** (two causal paths): the goal is to save the five *simpliciter* (L247–253).
- **Kamm's DTE** ("because" vs "in order to"; defeater and defeater-defeater).
  - The analogy with *Party* fails: *Party* has two final goals, its defeater does not block the goal, and its defeater-defeater is not a means.
  - In Loop the defeater-defeater (the man's body stopping the trolley) *is* a means to the single goal. *Party Variant* (L305–323; the unwilling neighbours forced by local norms to clean up) has Loop's structure, and there the use is plainly intended.
  - He credits Otsuka's *Illuminated Trolley* and Liao 2009 for the one-goal point.
  - Fn 28: Otsuka's *Six Behind One* (six people behind the large man on the second track) is the diagram *Party* really corresponds to. It is Tracks-like.

**→ SV.**
1. **The symmetric point does not answer Choo.** He sets Thomson's symmetric version aside explicitly. The SV's "not diverting also spends the five" analysis stands for Thomson's *original* case, which is a real but modest result. The asymmetric Loop is the case the debate and the data concern.
2. **The SV forbids the asymmetric Loop, and its structural analysis matches Choo's.** The man's body supplies the whole of the benefit relative to not diverting. Kamm's defeater-defeater analysis mistakes a supplier of the benefit for a mere preventer of collateral harm. (The SV's "supply relative to alternatives" clause *is* the principled version of Choo's point against Kamm, while *Tracks* and *Six Behind One* stay permissible.) So the SV bites the bullet, with Otsuka, Walen 2016, and Ramakrishnan (weakly).
3. **What the SV can add that Choo's group-1 critics lack: a rationale-based argument, not an appeal to use or to data.** If the Loop verdict is right, the permission not to give (P3) is empty in exactly the case where the victim's body supplies the entire benefit. Choo's objection to (3.3) is that philosophers see the use and still judge permissible. The SV's reply is that seeing the *use* is not seeing the *spending*.

   Possible debunking: the permissibility intuition may track the redirection of an existing threat among people already on the tracks (Thomson's "distributive" thought; verify in file 23). That feature is shared with Standard and matters to the doing/allowing asymmetry, not to the MP. Test: Choo's own *Party Variant* has Loop's structure without the redirection of a threat, and there the use is plain. Check this against Thomson 1985 before relying on it.
4. **Honest stance for the essay.** Loop cannot be made "a strength" for the asymmetric version. Revise Plan §5: the result is (i) Thomson's own case vindicated; (ii) the asymmetric bullet bitten with a rationale-based argument and a diagnosis; (iii) Kamm's rescue declined on Choo's grounds, stated through the SV's supply criterion. Choo's data (81%) must be reported fairly.

## 27 — Otsuka 2008, "Double Effect, Triple Effect and the Trolley Problem: Squaring the Circle in Looping Cases," *Utilitas* 20(1): 92–110

**Status:** read in full (L1–173). No page markers. Otsuka's own internal cross-references give Kamm pages.

**Key content.**
- **Fn 7 (L41): the symmetric/asymmetric point.** On Thomson's configuration, "the one would inevitably be hit by a trolley if the five were not present." Costa argued that "the permissibility of turning the trolley onto the one in Thomson's Loop Case is partially explained by the fact that the one is so-protected by the five." Otsuka therefore *stipulates* the asymmetric version.
  - Citation conflict: Otsuka gives Costa as *SJP* **24** (1987), pp. 437–49; Walen 2014 fn 34 gives *SJP* **25** (1987), p. 463. Verify before citing (REQUESTS #21).
- **Against Kamm's DTE** (§I).
  - The Extra Push vs Two Loop analysis: no defensible difference in intention between them.
  - Looping cases (L91): "you do not eliminate or lessen the threat that the trolley poses to the five by sending it down a looping track ... Its diversion down a looping track is valuable merely as a means to make it come to such a halt."
  - Contrast ***Six Behind One*** (L93–99), where six people stand behind the one on a non-looping side track. Diversion "completely eliminates the threat ... irrespective of the fact that the trolley will hit the one ... one does not do something in order to mitigate the bad consequences of the doing of that very thing." Permissible. The DTE separates *Six Behind One* from Bridge but not the loops from Bridge (L105).
- ***Loop-Bridge*** (L107–125): the circling trolley triggers the pole that topples the man from the bridge. It is indistinguishable from Bridge.
- ***Lazy Susan*** (Kamm): moving a person into a threat vs moving a threat into a person makes no moral difference (L119).
- **Fn 20 (L127): "redistribution" of a threat cannot explain the cases**, since Bridge also redistributes the threat to the man on the bridge.
- ***Illuminated Trolley*** (L131–135): illuminating the trolley does not lessen the threat, so it is valuable only as a means.
- ***Ramp*** (Fischer; L145–151): launching the trolley upward into a man on a footbridge. "Is there a moral distinction to be drawn between horizontal and vertical diversions?"
- **§II: the assimilation pull runs in both directions** (L141–159). It is harder to separate the loops from Bridge than from the Trolley Case.
- Loops are not modelled on real-world cases, and the intuition may be an artefact of Thomson's presentation (L161). In a classroom poll (Bridge, then Loop-Bridge) a majority said impermissible. Hauser's first-response data split 50–50.
- **Conclusion (L165).** Reject the Loop intuition "as a moral illusion." The grounds are theoretical (Quinn) plus "a wealth of concrete cases in which we recoil from the harmful using."

**→ SV.**
1. **The symmetric Loop point is Costa's (1987), and Otsuka states it** (fn 7). The SV's contribution there is only to *explain* Costa's observation: not diverting would spend the five, whose bodies supply the one's safety. It is a small, principled addition, not a headline.
2. **The SV's "supply relative to alternatives" is the objective counterpart of Otsuka's intention-based Six-Behind-One/Loop contrast** (and of Ramakrishnan's "prevents a would-be defeater"). Credit both. What the SV adds is the objective formulation, which fits Ramakrishnan's and Quong's rejection of intention-based versions.
3. **Loop strategy for the essay.**
   - Side with Otsuka on the asymmetric loop, using his two-directional assimilation argument (Loop-Bridge, Ramp, Lazy Susan).
   - Add the SV's rationale: in a loop the victim's body supplies the *entire* benefit, and supplying at that cost is his decision. This argues from principle, not merely from "he is used."
   - Choo's replies to Otsuka (Choo §3.1–3.3) target the order-effect and "mere means" arguments. They **do not engage the Loop-Bridge/Ramp assimilation argument directly**. Use that gap.
   - **Do not** use a redirection or redistribution debunking (Otsuka fn 20 already refutes it).

## 25 — Alexander 2016, "The Means Principle," in Ferzan & Morse (eds.), *Legal, Moral, and Metaphysical Truths* (OUP)

**Status:** read in full (L1–197). This is the chapter version; the fuller paper is SSRN 2378608 (L11). No page markers.

**The view.**
- **Statement (L9, L31).** The MP "forbids actors from achieving otherwise good consequences by using, without their consent, others' bodies, labor, talents, or other resources that are rightfully theirs." It is "a jurisdictional limitation on the reach of strong (legitimately enforceable) moral obligations."
- Objective and causal: facts fix permissibility; beliefs fix culpability; intentions affect neither.
- **The resource framing (L79).** "The core idea is that we are not resources for others' benefit without our consent ... Enforceable morality has no jurisdiction over us as resources for its ends."
- **Prerogative as flipside (L81–89; fn 16).** "The logical corollary of the MP is a prerogative not to be compelled to further C," and "the logical corollary of a prerogative is a deontological constraint (such as the MP) on others." The bystander cannot be compelled to switch, since compulsion would use him.
- **The objective accounting (L167): the disabler.** "an act is objectively permissible if its benefits, excluding those causally derived from usings or breaches of promises, exceed all of its costs, including those caused by usings."
- **Use needs no intent (L145).** In the drawbridge-and-flowers case, "Using as a means does not require an intent to use. It only requires that the use is necessary to justify the act."
- Resources are covered only "derivatively," as products of labor or of one's rightful share (fn 6; L51–53). The Short/Tall apples case (L55) shows how the MP limits redistribution.
- **Limits (L99–105):**
  - consent and promise are internal to the MP;
  - an actor who has created peril has a duty to avert it;
  - the culpable may be used, up to their desert;
  - threshold deontology is left open.
- **Omissions (L151–161).** "May Al refuse to rescue Fat Man ... because if he is not rescued, the five workers will be saved? If one has no duty to rescue Fat Man, then refusing to rescue him for the stated reason is not wrong." The act/omission distinction is "immaterial to the MP," since both fall within the prerogative. Ducking a rock and walking away from Balanced Rock are permitted (L161).

**Loops and *Six Behind One* (fn 8, L45–57).**
- **Asymmetric loops** violate the MP "unless there is a C-benefit to turning it other than the use," such as more time for the five, or a heavy object further down the siding (cf. Persson's *Double Saving*).
- **Symmetric loops.** Not switching might seem to use the five, but "it is chance (the position of the switch) that will 'use' the bodies of the five." His image: lightning kills five shipwrecked crew, whose corpses the survivor then eats, vs throwing a meatier crew member into the lightning's path. So switching is permissible only for some other benefit.
- ***Six Behind One* (six people behind the one): IMPERMISSIBLE** on Alexander's view. "there is no C-benefit in such a case for switching the trolley other than to use the one as a means of saving the six" (L57).
- *Lazy Susan*: permissible, since there is a benefit (more time) other than the use.

**→ SV.**
1. **The rationale is anticipated, and must be credited up front.** Alexander has:
   - the resource framing ("we are not resources for others' benefit");
   - the jurisdictional idea;
   - **the explicit prerogative ↔ MP corollary**;
   - the disabler (benefits derived from usings are excluded).

   The SV's claim to novelty in its rationale reduces to one point. The correct *object* of the prerogative and of the jurisdiction is supplying at a cost, not use, and this fixes the scope.
2. **The SV diverges from Alexander on three families of cases, all by that scope point:**
   - (a) **Omissions.** Alexander permits refusing to rescue so that the man's body saves five, if there is no duty to rescue. The SV forbids, since letting him die is spending him (the sliding man, *Heart Attack*). The SV sides with Parry, Tadros, and Ramakrishnan here. The essay should state this disagreement with Alexander.
   - (b) ***Six Behind One* / Tracks.** Alexander forbids, because the net benefit depends on the one's body. The SV permits: relative to the alternative of not diverting, the six are safe anyway, so their safety is no benefit of diverting. This agrees with Otsuka (L93–99 of file 27), Ramakrishnan's *Tracks*, and Walen's sidetrack man who protects others. **Alexander's accounting counts preventing the act's own harm as a benefit derived from use. The SV's comparative accounting corrects this.** This is a clean, citable divergence showing why "supply relative to alternatives" matters.
   - (c) **Property.** Using Barry's wrench (a product of his labor) makes the benefit "derived from" use, so on Alexander's view the wrench cases seem forbidden, as on Quong's. The SV permits them. Check the SSRN long version before asserting this; Alexander does not discuss these cases in the chapter.
3. **Symmetric Loop.** Alexander rejects the "not switching uses the five" analysis (chance, not the agent, uses them). The SV's reply draws on (a): whether an omission spends someone does not depend on who put the victims in place. Letting someone die *for the sake of* what his body supplies spends him, whether chance or an agent placed him there. (*Heart Attack* is chance too: nobody caused her attack.) So Alexander's reply to the symmetric case commits him to permitting *Heart Attack*-type omissions, which is where the SV disagrees.

## 24 — Øverland 2014, "Moral Obstacles: An Alternative to the Doctrine of Double Effect," *Ethics* 124(3): 481–506

**Status:** read the core (L1–120) and searched the rest; the reading notes cover the argument.

**The view.** A victim-centred "theory of moral obstacles" (TMO). A moral obstacle is an innocent whose presence means that either he or the defender (or both, in some measure) will be harmed, if the defender uses means she could otherwise have used harmlessly (L95).
- Moral obstacles "give rise to cost," so "fairness dictates" that they bear some of it (L59, L117–119).
- The contrast: *Flamethrower* (people behind Tom: obstacles) vs *Rifle* (Frank: a bystander whose presence "only provides Mary with an option," L107).
- **Duties of obstacles** (*Mo's Escape*, L157–167): an obstacle must bear more cost to get out of the way than a bystander must bear to help. His refusal needs a proportionate reason.
- **Benefit-generating presence** reduces the cost he must bear (L237–241).
- **Dependence on the defender's means** (L243–247).
- ***Armor*** (L291–297): using an obstacle as a shield at some cost is permissible, where the DDE and Quinn's view forbid it.
- He contrasts the TMO with Quinn's direct and indirect agency (L309–311).
- Fn 34 (L333): in earlier work he grounded permissible force in "an agent's initial duty to bear cost."

**→ SV.** No overlap with the SV's criterion: the TMO is about presence, cost, and fairness, not supply. The SV avoids the counterfactual-specification problems Liao & Barry raise against the TMO (see the entry for file 29). One small point of contact: Øverland's obstacles owe more than bystanders do. The SV's "owes" clause can allow that the duties of those who give rise to cost may be greater, but the essay should not engage *Armor*. Footnote-level only.

## 23 — Thomson 1985, "The Trolley Problem," *Yale Law Journal* 94: 1395–1415

**Status:** read the key sections (IV–VIII: L119–290) and the loop passage. No page markers. Choo gives the loop at pp. 1402–1403 and the "extra bit of track" at p. 1403; Otsuka gives the "no plausible account" sentence at p. 1403. Confirm against a paginated copy (#20).

**Key content.**
- **The original loop** (L133–149) is SYMMETRIC: the five "are thin, but thick enough so that although all five will be killed if the trolley goes straight, the bodies of the five will stop it, and it will therefore not reach the one." Verdict: "we cannot really suppose that the presence or absence of that extra bit of track makes a major moral difference ... it really does seem right to think (despite the discomfort) that the agent may proceed." And: "there is no plausible account of ... 'treating a person as a means only,' or 'using one to save five,' under which the surgeon would be doing this whereas the agent in this variant ... would not be."
- **Fn 8 (L159): Thomson rejects the symmetric "not acting uses the five" idea.** "Should we say, then, that the agent uses one to save five if he acts, *and* uses five to save one if he does not act? No ... If the agent does not act, he uses nobody. (I doubt that it can even be said that if he does not act, he lets them *be used*. For what is the active for which this is passive? Who or what would be using them if he does not act?)"
- **Her positive account** (L161–290):
  - (i) The "distributive exemption": the agent "makes be a threat to fewer what is already a threat to more" (L243).
  - (ii) The means must not themselves infringe stringent rights. In *Fat Man*, toppling him is itself an infringement; this uses a "tighter notion of 'means'" that includes everything one must succeed in doing (L253–267).
  - (iii) The degree of the right matters. The nailfile and land-crossing variants are the original *Trolley Tool*: "I do not find it obvious that he may ... others tell me they think it clear the bystander may proceed ... property rights of no great importance" (L271–275).
  - The Mayor/convalescent case: a promise-based right (L277–281).

**→ SV.**
1. **Thomson's fn 8 is the source of the objection to the SV's symmetric-loop point**, and Alexander ("chance") and Walen 2016 (the ladder) repeat it. Answer: not acting can let someone be spent (*Heart Attack*; the sliding man, where "who uses Billy?" has the answer "Sarah, by letting him slide for that purpose"). Still, the symmetric point is contestable and minor. **Demote it to a footnote.**
2. Thomson's nailfile case is the ancestor of Parry's wrench case. Credit Thomson 1985 (p. 1411 per Parry fn 34).
3. Her distributive exemption is an alternative *explanation* of the diversion case, not a version of the MP. Her 2008 turnabout (file 13) abandons the bystander's permission.

- **Loop** (ch. 4 pp. 94–95, L61–65; the page marker for p. 95 opens L65, and pp. 92–94 have no markers): the looping trolley is "a *new problem*" created by the diversion, equivalent to a *Wagon* case in which a new device threatens the five.

- **Miracle** (p. 155): also impermissible.
- **Substitution vs subordination** (ch. 5 G, pp. 165–166):
  - *Substitution*: one person occupies "the very same position that another person would have occupied relative to the threat" (the Trolley case).

**→ SV.** The SV sides with Otsuka and Choo against Kamm's "new problem" and "structural equivalent" analysis. Stated objectively: judged against the alternative of not diverting, the five's survival is a benefit of diverting only because of the one's body. That makes it a supply, not the defeat of a new problem. Kamm's subordination idea (usefulness to another's good) is close to the SV's "resource" gloss. At most one footnote.

## 19 — Mack 2005, "Prerogatives, Restrictions, and Rights," *Social Philosophy and Policy* 22(1): 357–393

**Status:** read in full (L1–289). No page markers.

**Argument.**
- The anticonsequentialist "progression" runs from a prerogative (declining self-sacrifice) to restrictions (L23, quoting Nozick 32–33). Scheffler halts at the prerogative, via his "independence thesis" (L57).
- Mack: a bare prerogative fails its own rationale. The personal point of view can be subordinated along "two distinct dimensions": being required to devote oneself to the optimal outcome, and being devoted to it *by others* (L117, L143).
- Examples:
  - Rebekah's organs and her vacation fund;
  - the despot's "microinjection" (L159).
- "The restriction on others that seems to be the natural companion of a given prerogative is a restriction against suppressing Rebekah's exercise of that prerogative" (L167).
- Minimizing suppressions is itself an impersonalist canon, so it cannot serve (L175–183).
- The more robust the prerogative, the more robust the restrictions. The individualist prerogative (agent-relative values only) yields maximal restrictions (L187–255).
- **Scope (§VI, L261–277).** The prerogative "operates upon or ranges over a certain discrete set of resources: to begin with, the various resources for purposive action that constitute her person" (energy, attention, bodily parts). The restrictions "prohibit Rebekah from devoting Joshua ... to the promotion of her personal ends." Correlative right: self-ownership (L269, L283).

**→ SV.** Mack is the canonical non-hollowness argument (P3–P4). Credit him at P3.
1. **His restriction is broad**: self-ownership, the Nozickian side-constraints. It forbids "suppression of [one's] chosen employment of [one's] energy, attention, bodily parts." Read naturally, that covers side-effect killing too, and he is not trying to derive the MP.
2. **The SV narrows the argument's output to the MP.** The SV argues that the relevant prerogative (the limit of beneficence) concerns what one must *give* others at a cost to oneself. A restriction against making someone give is therefore all that non-hollowness requires. Side-effect harms make no one give anything, and Switch shows that they may be imposed.
3. **Scope of the claim.** This narrowing, plus the criterion that implements it, is the SV's addition to Mack's argument. The essay should present it as a correction of the Mack/Alexander line, not as an original discovery of the prerogative–restriction link.

## 16 — Steinhoff 2019, "Wild Goose Chase: Still No Rationales for the Doctrine of Double Effect and Related Principles," *Criminal Law and Philosophy* 13(1): 1–25

**Status:** read §§1–4 and §6 in part (L1–154, L209–240); skimmed §5 (Sarch). No page markers.

**Arguments.**
- Against "guided by evil" (Nagel): refuted by others (§2).
- Against Quinn (§3): the difference in "attitude" and the "cooperation" presumption (not shooting down the bomber counts as cooperation in both cases).
- **Against Tadros (§4).**
  - "Right to set one's own ends": on ordinary readings it applies to side-effect victims too; on a technical reading it merely restates the MP (L85–93).
  - Using a body is not "compelling a person to serve an end" (L95–105).
  - **"Using vs discarding"**: *Car 1* (shove an unconscious person off a bridge so her body reserves my parking space) vs *Car 2* (shove her off as a side effect of parking where she lies). "intuitively it is not" more wrong. Millionaire variant: must parking save more lives in *Car 1*? "there is no discernible difference" (L107–109).
  - **Incoherence** (L127–143): if the sidetrack man may not avert the diverted trolley (as Tadros holds), he is required to serve the rescuer's ends by omission, so "not required to serve others' ends" cannot separate the cases.
  - "a person is entitled to determine not only which ends to pursue but also in pursuit of which ends her body is to suffer adverse side effects" (L119).
  - Methodology: Trolley vs Bridge is not a controlled pair (touching, force, falling). Better pairs:
    - ***Sensor Trolley*** (diverting by pointing a sensor at the sidetrack man's body; intuitively permissible);
    - ***Robot Bridge 1/2*** (a robot throws the fat man, either as a side effect of the button that stops the trolley or as the means; both impermissible at the same threshold) (L145–147).
- Against Øverland and Walen (§6): counterfactual counterexamples, in the style of Liao & Barry.

**→ SV** (the standing challenge the rationale must meet).
1. **Steinhoff's key question is the SV's central target**: why is *using* worse than *discarding*? The SV's answer, for the essay's §3:
   - The permission not to give protects a person's say over what she supplies. If the question "must she supply this?" were settled by weighing everyone's interests impartially, she would have no such say; her giving would be fixed by others' needs.
   - The question "may others do what incidentally harms her?" *can* be settled impartially without touching that say, since nothing of hers is given.
   - So the prerogative requires that spending, and only spending, be exempt from impartial weighing. Switch confirms the pattern: she need not divert the trolley onto herself, yet others may.

   Steinhoff's "entitled to determine ... in pursuit of which ends her body is to suffer adverse side effects" is exactly the extension this argument denies. Reply: no one has such an entitlement in general, or Switch would be forbidden. His *Sensor Trolley* is handled by the SV (permissible: his death is not the cost of the sensor's use).
2. **Car 1/2 and Robot Bridge 1** involve newly created harms that fall directly on a person in the path. As with *Car Rescue*, the SV says these are not spending. Their wrongness, if they are wrong, lies elsewhere: doing and allowing, or new versus redirected threats. So they do not test the MP cleanly. Steinhoff's "same threshold" intuition about *Car 1/2* is compatible with the SV if *Car 2* is wrong for those other reasons.
3. **The incoherence charge does not reach the SV.** The SV's rationale concerns *supplying*, not "serving ends." A duty not to resist a permissible diversion is at most a duty to *bear*, not to *supply*, so the SV's supply/bear distinction answers the charge directly.

## 15 — Hecht 2023, "Activating the Right to Be Rescued," *Journal of Moral Philosophy* 20

**Status:** read §§1, 3, 5 and 6 in full; §§2 and 4 by their cases (L1–360). No page markers. Issue and pages still to confirm.

**Thesis.** A person's right to be rescued is activated only if she has an evidence-relative justification for the action that caused her peril: the *Justification Condition* (L29, L75, L123). This is against Quong's "maximally inclusive" *Coherence Condition* (L71, L81–101).
- **Argument.** Activation lets a person impose duties on others unilaterally. Arbitrary control over another's normative situation conflicts with moral equality (L13, L123, L169).
- **Cases:** *Manipulation* (Ivan would gain a right by wronging Mel, L113–119); *Careless*; *Mountain-climber*; *Child*; *Smoker*.
- **Charity.** A residual, non-directional duty of charity covers those without the right (L291).

**§5.** Quong grounds the necessity condition on the aggressor's right to be rescued (RESCUE, L309, citing Quong p. 131). Aggressors lack a justification, so on Hecht's view the right is not activated and cannot ground necessity (L321). She offers CHARITY, a non-directional duty, as an alternative ground (L343).

**Other.** Fn 2 (L21, L31): Fabre 2006, *Whose Body Is It Anyway?*, invokes the right to be rescued to explain why agents must provide "material resources, personal services, and even parts of their own body."

**→ SV.**
1. **The SV takes the duty of rescue as an input.** Its bound ("beyond what she owes") inherits whatever the correct theory of rescue says, whether Quong's, Hecht's or another. So the SV is neutral on activation conditions. The essay should say this in one clause, not argue it.
2. **The SV's omission verdicts are conditional on a reason to aid.** The disabler removes the good that someone's plight would supply from the case *against* helping her; it creates no duty to help. Where there is no reason to aid, there is nothing to disable, and refusing needs no justification. (An example on Hecht's view: someone careless, with no rescue right.) So the SV condemns *Heart Attack* and the sliding man only because each victim has an undefeated claim to aid once the supplied good is set aside. §4 must state this conditional explicitly.
3. Fabre's "parts of their own body" line is the beneficence side of the SV's P3. Cite it only if the essay discusses duties to give body parts. Not on disk.

## Correction to the entry for file 25 (Alexander), made after a re-read of L151–161

Point 2(a) of the Alexander entry overstates the divergence on omissions.
- **The structure is the same.** Alexander holds that a duty to rescue "is not canceled by the setback to C" when the setback comes only from the victim’s not being used (L151). That is the SV's structure: a supplied good cannot defeat a duty to aid.
- **The difference is over which duties to aid exist.** Alexander's examples of a duty to rescue are special ones (L151), and he denies any duty to use one's body to shield others: "one has no duty to use his body to shield others even at little or no cost," unless threshold deontology applies (L161). He allows only special duties (one's child; a peril one caused).

So on omissions the SV's structure is anticipated by Alexander. What is distinctive is only the background of rescue duties it assumes, plus the explicit criterion. The rethink memo uses this corrected reading.

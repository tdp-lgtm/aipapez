# Spending Persons

**Abstract.** [To be written last, about 150 words.]

## 1. Introduction

Compare two cases, both set in a burning house.

*Smoke.* Five people are trapped in an attic that is filling with smoke. The only way out is a trapdoor that falls shut unless something holds it open. An unconscious neighbour lies directly beneath it. His body is the only thing within reach that the trapdoor can be propped against, and propping it against him does him no harm. But once the trapdoor opens, the smoke will pour down onto him, and there is no time to move him. A firefighter props the trapdoor against him. The five climb down, and he dies of the smoke.

*Wedge.* Everything is the same, except that a roof vent draws the smoke away, so none will reach him, and the trapdoor is too heavy to prop. It stays open only if something is wedged beneath it, and the only thing there is his body, which the trapdoor's weight will crush. The firefighter wedges him beneath it. The five climb down, and he dies.

It seems permissible for the firefighter to act as she does in *Smoke*, and wrong for her to act as she does in *Wedge*. Yet the difference is hard to locate. In both cases his body holds the trapdoor open, he dies, and five people are saved. In both, the firefighter kills him rather than letting him die. So the difference lies neither in whether she uses him, nor in whether she harms him, nor in whether she kills him. The verdict in *Smoke* does not depend on any theory. Without the prop, *Smoke* would be a case of a familiar kind, in which a threat to five people is let out onto one, as when a runaway trolley is diverted from five people onto one; most people judge such diversions permissible (Otsuka 2008, [p.]). The prop changes nothing for him. Had a beam been within reach, the firefighter would have propped the trapdoor on the beam, and he would have died of the smoke all the same. Adding a use of someone that makes no difference to what happens to him cannot turn a permissible act into a wrong one.

Not everyone accepts this verdict. On Jonathan Quong's version of the means principle, it is "morally wrong to harm Y in the pursuit of an objective if doing so involves using Y's body or other things over which Y has a rightful prior claim, unless Y is duty bound to suffer this harm, or has consented to this harm" (Quong 2020, [p.]). Quong considers a case with the structure of *Smoke*, in which a woman on a side track is killed by a diverted trolley whose switch works only while her body presses a button, and he counts it a violation (Quong 2020, [p.] n. 27). I will argue that he need not.

The idea at the heart of the principle is Warren Quinn's. His version of the doctrine of double effect rests on "the strong moral presumption that those who can be usefully involved in the promotion of a goal only at the cost of something protected by their independent moral rights ... ought, prima facie, to serve the goal only voluntarily", unless they "have or would have strong moral obligations" to serve (Quinn 1989, [p.]). Later work has split into two tracks. One seeks a criterion. Quong asks whether the agent's act "could succeed in the absence of Y and Y's rightful property" (Quong 2020, [p.]); Ketan Ramakrishnan asks whether a person will be useful to others only if her rights are infringed, a relation he takes as primitive (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). The other track seeks a rationale: persons may not be made to serve ends beyond what they owe (Mack 2005; Tadros 2011; Alexander 2016). Each track lacks what the other has. The criteria come without a good reason, since the reason Quong gives attaches to use, which *Smoke* shows to be the wrong target. The rationale comes without a criterion, and Quong argues that it either condemns too much or explains nothing (Quong 2020, [p.]).

I argue that the means principle forbids spending persons, not using them. To spend a person is to impose on her, or let her suffer, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear. A loss is the cost of supplying a good when the good could be had without the loss if what she contributes came from some other source. In *Wedge*, a beam wedged under the trapdoor would spare the man, so his death is the cost of what his body supplies. In *Smoke*, a beam would not spare him, since the smoke would reach him however the trapdoor were held. The view breaks with Quong's, which condemns harmful use as such, and goes beyond Ramakrishnan's, which it matches closely for harms to persons. The idea is Quinn's, and the rationale belongs to him and his successors (Mack 2005; Tadros 2011; Alexander 2016; Walen 2022). What is new is a test that makes the idea precise, an argument that it is the right test, and what follows from it.

The principle is one constraint among several. It does not forbid driving over a man who lies across the only road to people in need of rescue, since the driver needs his absence, not anything he supplies. I take duties of rescue as given, offer no theory of property, and set aside harmless uses, since the principle concerns losses. Section 2 states the view and its test; section 3 argues that it is the right version of the principle; section 4 extends it to omissions and holdings; section 5 answers objections, chiefly one drawn from looping trolley cases.

## 2. Using and Spending

The view I defend says that the means principle forbids spending persons. It rests on a difference between two ways a thing can figure in other people's plans. A log burned for heat is a resource: those who burn it decide whether its heat is worth its being burned. A tree felled because it stands where a road will go, and left where it falls, is a casualty: nothing it supplies is weighed, because it supplies nothing. What makes the log a resource is that whether its service is worth what that service costs it is for others to decide. That is also exactly what persons are said not to be. Quinn's ideal is that each person be treated "as existing only for purposes that he can share" (Quinn 1989, [p.]), and Quong gives each person "a sphere of independence where her decisions are sovereign" (Quong 2020, [p.]).[^1]

Stated in full, the view is this:

> **The Spending View.** The good that a person's contribution would supply to others cannot justify imposing on her, or letting her suffer, the loss that supplying it would cost her, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear.

A person's contribution is whatever of hers serves others: her body, its parts and position, her time and labour, her holdings. A loss is a setback she would not suffer under the alternative. To spend her is to impose such a loss on her, or let her suffer it, for the sake of the good her contribution supplies, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear. "For the sake of" concerns justification, not motive: the good is what would be offered to justify the loss. The bound has two parts. A loss she is duty bound to bear for the good may be imposed, and so may a loss she agrees to bear.[^2]

The view needs a test for when a loss is the cost of supplying a good. I propose a replacement test: her loss is the cost of her supplying a good if, were what she contributes provided from some other source, the good could be had without that loss. Take the footbridge case, in which the only way to stop a trolley bound for five people is to push a large man from a bridge into its path. Were the stopping supplied by something else, such as a heavy weight, the five could be saved and he could live. So his death is the cost of what his body supplies, and pushing him spends him. As section 1 anticipated, the test also separates *Wedge* from *Smoke*. What the test varies is what the person contributes, never the person. She stays where she is; only the source of her contribution changes.

The familiar tests vary the person instead. Quinn asked whether an agent would still need his victims' cooperation if they had shelters or medicines of their own (Quinn 1989, [p.]). His successors ask what the agent could achieve in the victim's absence. Quong's test is of this kind, and Ramakrishnan traces a principle of this form to Quinn himself (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.] n. 21). Absence tests fail. In one of Ramakrishnan's cases, a woman lies on the only pill that could cure five people of a deadly disease. She also has a harmless condition that would let a doctor devise a second cure by testing her. She suffers a heart attack, and the doctor leaves it untreated, because treating it would make her useless for the tests (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). The five could be saved in her absence, with the pill, so an absence test finds nothing amiss. Yet leaving her to die so that the tests can run seems plainly wrong. The replacement test condemns it. Were the information her body yields supplied by another source, the doctor could save the five and treat her, so her death is the cost of what she supplies. Is the replacement test just another counterfactual test? It is. But it varies only the source of her contribution, never her presence or her position, so it never has to say what would fill the place she leaves empty.[^3]

The view has a second feature: what a person supplies is judged against what the agent could otherwise do. A justification is a reason for one option rather than another, so her contribution supplies a good only if the good is a respect in which the act is better for others than an alternative. Suppose six people stand behind the man on the side track, so that his body will shield them from the diverted trolley. They are safe if the agent does not divert, so their safety is no respect in which diverting is better than not diverting. What makes diverting better is the rescue of the five, and nothing of his supplies that. Diverting is permissible. When the side track loops back to the five, by contrast, the whole improvement comes from his body (section 5). Michael Otsuka draws this contrast in terms of the agent's intentions, and Ramakrishnan discusses a version with twenty people behind (Otsuka 2008, [p.]; Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]).[^4] The comparison also corrects Larry Alexander, who counts only benefits "excluding those causally derived from usings" and so forbids diverting in the six-behind case (Alexander 2016, [p.] and n. 8 (verify)). His accounting measures the diversion against the man's absence, in which the trolley would run on into the six. The agent cannot choose that. Measured against what she can choose, the six gain nothing from his body.

Together the two features sort the canonical pairs. The footbridge case is spending; the diversion case is not, since nothing of the one supplies the five's rescue. Ramakrishnan's own pair has the same shape as the opening pair, now for letting die. In one case, a man's legs hold open the door of a room where five people are dying of a virus, and a doctor gives his scarce medicine to the five rather than to the man (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). Were the door held by something else, the man would still go without the medicine, since there is not enough for him and the five. His death is not the cost of what his legs supply, and the doctor acts permissibly. In the other case, a patient is left untreated so that tests on her can run, as in the case of the pill, and were the information supplied elsewhere she would be treated (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). That is spending.

Two cases from S. Matthew Liao and Christian Barry, pressed against views that test by absence, show that the replacement test needs no stipulation about empty places (Liao and Barry 2020, [p.]). In the first, a defender can stop a lethal attack only by shooting a man off a roof so that he falls on the attacker; had he not been there, builders would have put a log in his place, which she could have shot instead. An absence test says that his presence costs her an option, and so weakens his claim; yet shooting him is as wrong as ever. In the second, a defender kills an attacker with a flamethrower, and the blast kills a bystander behind him; had the bystander not stood in front of a door, fifty people would have come through it into the flames. An absence test says that his presence costs her nothing, and so leaves his claim at full strength; yet the act seems as permissible as it would be without the door. The replacement test sorts both. The man on the roof supplies the stopping, and were it supplied by something else he would be spared. The bystander supplies nothing relative to the defender's alternative, since the fifty are safe if she does not fire.[^5]

Using a person is therefore neither necessary nor sufficient for spending her. *Smoke*, the legs holding the door, and a case of a borrowed wrench in section 4 involve use without spending. The untreated patient involves spending without harmful use: the tests on her are harmless, and what kills her is being left untreated so that they can run.

A reader may suspect that the view is Ramakrishnan's principle renamed. On his principle, a justification for infringing someone's claim that holds because she "will be useful to others ... only if C is infringed" is "substantially weaker" than it otherwise would be (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). For harms to persons the two views agree closely, but they differ in four ways. First, the view grounds its comparison with the agent's alternatives, and his treatment of cases where a person's position prevents "a would-be defeater" of a justification falls out as a special case of it (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). Second, the view is explained by a permission almost everyone accepts (section 3), where he takes his principle as primitive. Third, it covers holdings, which Quong presses against him (Quong 2020, [p.]; section 4). Fourth, it disables the justification within a bound set by duty, where his principle discounts it.

## 3. Why Spending Matters

The replacement test sorts the cases. But why should the means principle track it? Ramakrishnan doubts that such a principle has a deeper explanation, and Uwe Steinhoff doubts that any rationale for it succeeds (Steinhoff 2019). If a principle's best version is the one that does its job, no more and no less, the question is what the means principle's job is. My answer takes the form of an argument.

> **The argument.**
>
> (P1) The means principle exists to keep morality from treating persons as resources for others at their own expense.
>
> (P2) To treat something as a resource for others at its expense is to treat the question whether a good it can supply them is worth what supplying it costs it as a question for others to settle.
>
> (P3) Beyond what a person owes, that question is hers to settle, and she settles it by agreeing or refusing; within what she owes, her duty has already settled it.
>
> (P4) Others settle it for her exactly when they impose on her, or let her suffer, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good.
>
> (C) So the principle should hold that the good that a person's contribution would supply to others cannot justify imposing on her, or letting her suffer, the loss that supplying it would cost her, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear. That is, it should forbid spending her.

Premise 1 is common ground: Alexander states the principle's "core idea" as that "we are not resources for others' benefit without our consent" (Alexander 2016, [p.]). Premise 2 is the analysis given with the log and the tree in section 2. Premise 3 is inherited, and I defend it below. Premise 4 is where the view does its work. Section 2 supplied the test, and the rest of this section argues that only spending settles the question for her.

Premise 3 rests on a permission that nearly every non-consequentialist accepts: no one need give a kidney, or her life, to others, even when they would gain more than she loses. What premise 3 adds is that this permission not to give is more than a permission to refuse. Imagine a rule that let a hospital take a kidney from anyone whose kidney would save a life, while leaving everyone free to refuse to donate. No one would say that the rule respects the permission not to donate. Every refusal stands, and the kidney is taken anyway.[^6] The rule offends for a reason. The permission's point, in Walen's words, is that a person is "a free agent leading her own independent life", whom morality "cannot treat ... as though she may be pressed into duty to serve others at great expense to herself" (Walen 2016, [p.]). Whether her body serves others at a cost to her is part of that life. A permission that others could override by taking would leave that question at their disposal, and would settle only who does the taking. Within what she owes, her duty has settled the question already; that is Quinn's exception for strong moral obligations. None of this is new. Mack argues that a personal permission needs a matching restriction on others, Alexander calls the permission the principle's "logical corollary", and Tadros and Walen ground the principle in the independence the permission protects (Mack 2005, [p.]; Alexander 2016, [p.]; Tadros 2011, [p.]; Walen 2022, [p.]).[^7]

Quong argues that no rationale of this kind can work (Quong 2020, [p.]). Read as the claim that "if Y is not duty bound to bear some cost, C, for the sake of goal, G, then it is wrong to impose C on Y for the sake of G", the rationale condemns the diversion case, since the one on the side track is not duty bound to die for the five. Read as Tadros's claim that the wrong is to "compel [a victim] to act for the sake of some end that she is permitted to reject for herself", it faces a dilemma. If compelling means imposing unowed costs, this is the first reading again. If it means using her body or property without her consent or duty, the explanation "amounts to saying: harmful using is distinctively wrong because it involves harmful using".

Both readings assume that the permission not to give limits what a person must bear. It does not. It limits what she must supply and what she must do. The diversion case imposes a cost on the one, but no cost of supply, so the first reading is not the rationale I have given. The second reading does not apply either, because the replacement test speaks of sources, goods and costs, never of use, so the explanation is not circular. The same point meets Liao and Barry's charge that rationales of this kind end up sounding "very much like the MP" (Liao and Barry 2020, [p.]). Quong's own rationale, finally, has the right structure: rights over a fair share of the world exist "to block" the fact that something would be useful "from serving as a reason" (Quong 2020, [p.]). But its object is use. What the permission should block is the good a person's contribution supplies, as a reason to impose on her the cost of supplying it, and *Smoke* shows that the difference matters.

Premise 4 says that others settle the question for her exactly when they spend her. Why only then? The permission protects two things: what she must do, and what of hers must serve. Others cannot override the first without making her act; coercion does that, and is forbidden for that reason. They override the second exactly by spending her, as the hospital's rule does. A side-effect harm does neither. Its loss is not the cost of anything of hers serving: either nothing of hers serves, as in the diversion case, or the loss would fall on her all the same were what serves supplied from elsewhere, as the smoke falls in *Smoke*. So it leaves the permission's object untouched, and falls under the ordinary constraint against harming, which weighs harms against goods. Why not all harms? Mack's formulation shows why not. The prerogative lets each person "devote *herself*" to her own ends, and the matching restriction forbids others to devote her "energy, attention, bodily parts, and so on" to theirs (Mack 2005, [p.]). A side-effect harm devotes nothing of anyone. So others may divert a trolley onto her that she need not divert onto herself. The complaints differ accordingly. The person spent is taken from, and the value of what was taken cannot answer that complaint. The person harmed as a side effect is harmed, and that complaint is weighed.

The distinction answers Judith Jarvis Thomson's argument against turning the trolley. Her bystander could turn the trolley onto himself, onto one workman, or not at all. If he turns it onto the workman because he would rather not die, he makes "the one workman pay the cost of his good deed because he doesn't feel like paying it himself", which is worse than stealing money to give to Oxfam because one would rather not give one's own (Thomson 2008, 364). Since an ordinary bystander would not turn the trolley onto himself, she concludes, he may not turn it onto the one (Thomson 2008, 365–366). The analogy fails. The thief makes another's money serve his good deed. The bystander's refusal to turn the trolley onto himself concerns an act of his own, and turning it onto the workman makes nothing of the workman's serve. The argument slides from serving to doing. Tadros and Garrett Cullity reply in related ways (Tadros 2011, [p.]; Cullity 2018, [p.] n. 45).

Steinhoff presses a related question: is using a person's body worse than discarding it, when both bypass her agency (Steinhoff 2019, [p.])? On the view it is, when the use is at the cost of what she supplies, because that makes something of hers serve an end she may refuse. Steinhoff's own alternative, that a person is entitled to decide "in pursuit of which ends her body is to suffer adverse side effects" (Steinhoff 2019, [p.]), would make the diversion case as hard to justify as the footbridge case, which few will accept.

A last worry is Ramakrishnan's. A principle of this kind makes permissibility turn on a person's "location in causal space", which strikes him as "a terribly arbitrary feature of his situation—not much less arbitrary than his location in physical space" (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). It does so in a fine-grained way. His own principle lets a person's position prevent "a would-be defeater" of a justification, as in the case of twenty behind, and does not forbid harming someone who is a necessary means to others' survival when medicine is short (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). The view explains the grain. What matters is whether she is made to give, and the test for being made to give has exactly this shape. The comparison with the agent's alternatives removes the case of twenty behind, since the twenty gain nothing from her relative to not diverting. Replacement removes the cases of shortage, since there her loss would fall on her whatever supplied the rest. And giving is a structural notion: to give is to be the source of what another receives. A moral notion with a causal condition is not arbitrary on that account. Whether you have stolen from me depends on where what you have came from, and no one thinks theft an arbitrary wrong.

## 4. Omissions and Holdings

The view covers omissions as well as acts. Jonathan Parry, adapting a case of Tadros's, describes a man who slips on ice and slides toward a trolley that will otherwise paralyse five people (Parry 2023, [p.]; Tadros 2011, [p. 122]). His body would slow the trolley, and he would be paralysed. A passer-by could easily stop him, and refrains so that his body will stop the trolley. This seems wrong, though letting a man be hit is permissible when two trolleys threaten him and five others and she can stop only one. Quong can separate the cases only by giving the sliding man a prior right to be rescued. Parry argues that the five's claims extinguish any such right, since rights to be rescued, unlike rights against harm, give way to greater numbers (Parry 2023, [p.]), and he concludes that the principle must help determine rights rather than merely follow them (Parry 2023, [p.]).

The Spending View explains the case directly. Letting the man suffer the loss so that his body serves settles, for him, whether his body serves at that cost, just as imposing the loss would. Stopping him was easy, and she declines so that his body will do the stopping. This is the hospital's rule by omission: no one asks him, and his body serves the five at his cost all the same. So premise 4 covers omissions. An account on which the wrong is compelling someone to serve cannot say this, since failing to aid someone compels no one, as Ramakrishnan argues against Tadros (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). It follows that the good his body would supply cannot count against rescuing him, so the ordinary duty of easy rescue stands. In the two-trolley case nothing of the man's is supplied, and the numbers decide. Alexander's view has this structure: rescuing such a man is not wrong though it saves fewer, since it sets back the overall good "only by saving someone from being an involuntary means" of promoting it (Alexander 2016, [p.]).[^8]

The view extends to holdings without strain. Quong presses a pair of cases against Ramakrishnan: a swimmer who reaches the only piece of driftwood first, and one who steals another's life jacket (Quong 2020, [p.]). The first seems permissible and the second not, and a principle about persons alone cannot say why. The Spending View can. Taking the life jacket spends its owner, since another jacket would spare her. Winning the race to the driftwood spends no one, since nothing of the loser's supplies anything.

The view also avoids the costs Parry finds in Quong's extension of the principle to property. On Quong's view, turning a trolley toward a man with his own wrench uses him, so that when fifteen people are at risk, the only permissible rescue may be to turn the trolley onto three others instead (Parry 2023, [p.]). On the Spending View, the owner dies of the trolley, not as the cost of the wrench's service: had another tool turned the switch, he would have died all the same. Parry also objects that a property-inclusive principle lets acquisition impose new constraints on everyone else (Parry 2023, [p.]). On the view, holdings constrain others only where the owner's loss would be the cost of their service, and the duties that bound the principle grow with holdings.[^9]

The bound does real work here. Someone who uses a stranger's vase as a hammer to free a stuck switch, and breaks it, imposes on the owner a loss that is the cost of what the vase supplies, since another hammer would have spared it. But the loss falls within what the owner owes under a duty of easy rescue, so the act does not spend her. It is permitted, and she is owed redress.

## 5. Objections

The most serious objection comes from a looping trolley. In *Loop*, a trolley is headed for five people. An agent can divert it onto a side track that loops back toward them, and on the loop stands one man whose body will stop the trolley, killing him; if the trolley went straight, he would be safe (Choo 2025, [p.]; Liao et al. 2012, [p.]). Most people judge that diverting is permissible, and Frederick Choo argues that the judgment is robust. It survives controls for order effects. In studies that show no other case first, most participants accept it; in one, 81% do (Choo 2025, [p.]). And it is no illusion of inattention, since the philosophers who hold it see that the man is used (Choo 2025, [p.]). I grant all of this.

The view nonetheless forbids diverting. Relative to not diverting, the five survive only because his body stops the trolley, and a stopper placed before him would spare him. That is the footbridge case's structure. Frances Kamm tries to separate the cases: in diverting, she says, the agent removes the first threat and creates "a *new problem*", the trolley looping back, which his body merely prevents (Kamm 2007, 94–95). But the agent has a single goal, to save the five, and diverting serves it only through his body. Otsuka and Choo reject Kamm's description on this ground (Otsuka 2008, [p.]; Choo 2025, [p.]). The comparison with the agent's alternatives keeps the six-behind case permissible, since there the diversion saves the five by itself.[^10]

So the view overrides a robust intuition, and I should say why it should prevail. Otsuka links *Loop* to a variant of the footbridge case in which a lever moves a pole that topples a man into the trolley's path (Otsuka 2008, [p.]). That it is wrong to topple him is, in his words, "a moral fixed point" for non-consequentialists (Otsuka 2008, [p.]). He goes by two routes, each through one intermediate case. In the first, the diverted trolley, as it circles back, itself trips the pole. This case differs from *Loop* only in moving the man into the trolley rather than the trolley into the man, a difference Kamm herself argues is insignificant, and it is "morally indistinguishable" from the toppling case, since in both "you push a lever that causes someone to fall from the bridge" (Otsuka 2008, [p.]). In the second, opening a drawbridge sends the trolley up a ramp into a man on a footbridge overhead. This differs from the toppling case in the same insignificant way, and it appears "completely morally indistinguishable" from *Loop* (Otsuka 2008, [p.]). Otsuka doubts that any principle separates these cases (Otsuka 2008, [p.] n. 25). Choo answers Otsuka's general claim: those who judge *Loop* permissible see the use, and they trust that judgment over the principles that condemn it (Choo 2025, [p.]). Neither point reaches the chain. It needs no one to miss the use, and it sets the verdict on *Loop* against a verdict on a case, not against a principle. So one set of firm judgments must yield, and the principle that explains the wider set, and has an independent rationale, forbids diverting. This is the one place where the view overrides a robust intuition, and I concede the cost. Others reach the same verdict, including Otsuka, Walen, Liao and McMahan, and, on reflection, Ramakrishnan (Walen 2016, [p.]; Liao et al. 2012, [p.] n. 3; Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.] n. 36).[^11]

A second objection is that the view permits too much, since it seems to allow driving over a man who lies across the only road to people in need of rescue. The view is silent on that case, not permissive. He supplies nothing; what the driver needs is his absence. Other constraints forbid running him over. Quinn condemns it under his account of doing and allowing, since the driver intends an action of the car that will kill the man (as Liao and Barry 2020, [p.], discuss), and Walen now defends a claim to the space one occupies (Walen 2022, [p.]). The means principle is one constraint among several.[^12]

A third objection concerns motive. Parry describes a rescuer who is willing to lose an arm to stop a sliding man, sees five people further down the track, and refrains so that the man's body will stop the trolley (Parry 2023, [p.]). Refusing a rescue that would cost an arm is normally permitted, yet this refusal seems wrong.

The view can explain the case if one assumption holds. Suppose the permission not to give is a standing to favour oneself that is used in choosing, as premise 3's talk of the question being hers to settle suggests. Then, where conduct would otherwise spend someone, the permission justifies it only if the agent acts on it. The rescuer's refusal is made for what the man's body will supply, not to spare her arm, so her permission does not justify it. The five's rescue cannot justify it either, since the good his body supplies is disabled. Agent-neutral justifications justify whatever the motive, so motive matters only here. For the same reason the view disagrees with Liao and Barry about an enemy who turns a trolley from five people onto one only because he hates the one: on the view, he acts permissibly, if viciously (Liao and Barry 2020, [p.]).[^13]

Finally, the view may seem absolutist. It is not. Duties grow with the stakes, and the principle yields where duty yields: if a person can owe her life to save a million, taking it does not spend her. Whether she can is a question about the content of duty that the view inherits and does not settle. Ramakrishnan's principle, by contrast, meets large stakes by discounting the justification rather than bounding the disabler (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]).

## 6. Conclusion

I have argued that the means principle forbids spending persons, not using them. A replacement test, with supply judged against the agent's alternatives, sorts the opening pair, the canonical pairs, and the cases that defeat tests by absence. It gives Quinn's idea a precise form, and it repairs the rationale his successors offered, which fails when aimed at use and succeeds when aimed at spending. It extends to omissions and holdings, and it forbids diverting in *Loop*, at a cost I have stated.

The view is not the whole of the ethics of harm. It is silent about people who are merely in the way, and it takes duties of rescue as given. Within its scope, it says what the means principle protects. What separates *Smoke* from *Wedge* is not what the firefighter does with the man's body, but whether his death is the price of what it gives.

# Spending Persons

**Abstract.** [To be written last, about 150 words.]

## 1. Introduction

Compare two cases, both set in a burning house.

*Smoke.* Five people are trapped in an attic that is filling with smoke. The only way out is a trapdoor that falls shut unless something holds it open. An unconscious neighbour lies directly beneath it. His body is the only thing within reach that the trapdoor can be propped against, and propping it against him does him no harm. But once the trapdoor opens, the smoke will pour down onto him, and there is no time to move him. A firefighter props the trapdoor against him. The five climb down, and he dies of the smoke.

*Wedge.* Everything is the same, except that a roof vent draws the smoke away, so none will reach him, and the trapdoor is too heavy to prop. It stays open only if something is wedged beneath it, and the only thing there is his body, which the trapdoor's weight will crush. The firefighter wedges him beneath it. The five climb down, and he dies.

It seems permissible to prop the trapdoor in *Smoke* and wrong to wedge it in *Wedge*. Yet the difference is hard to locate. In both cases his body holds the trapdoor open, he dies, and five people are saved; in both, the firefighter kills him rather than letting him die. So the difference lies neither in using him, nor in harming him, nor in killing him. The verdict in *Smoke* also has support that owes nothing to theory. Without the prop, *Smoke* would be a familiar diversion, like turning a runaway trolley from five people onto one, which most people judge permissible (Otsuka 2008, [p.]). And the prop changes nothing for him: had a beam been within reach, the firefighter would have used it, and he would have died of the smoke all the same. A use that makes no difference to what happens to him cannot be what turns a permissible killing into a wrongful one.

Not everyone accepts this verdict. Jonathan Quong's version of the means principle forbids harming someone when doing so "involves using Y's body or other things over which Y has a rightful prior claim", absent duty or consent, and he counts as a violation a case with the structure of *Smoke*: a woman on a side track is killed by a diverted trolley whose switch works only while her body presses a button (Quong 2020, [p.]; [p.] n. 27). I will argue that he need not.

The idea at the heart of the principle is Warren Quinn's. His version of the doctrine of double effect rests on "the strong moral presumption that those who can be usefully involved in the promotion of a goal only at the cost of something protected by their independent moral rights ... ought, prima facie, to serve the goal only voluntarily", unless they "have or would have strong moral obligations" to serve (Quinn 1989, [p.]). Later work has split into two tracks. One seeks a criterion: Quong asks whether the agent's act "could succeed in the absence of Y and Y's rightful property" (Quong 2020, [p.]), and Ketan Ramakrishnan whether a person will be useful to others only if her rights are infringed (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). The other seeks a rationale: persons may not be made to serve ends beyond what they owe (Mack 2005; Tadros 2011; Alexander 2016; Walen 2022). Each track lacks what the other has. Ramakrishnan takes his criterion to be primitive, and Quong's reason for his attaches to use, which *Smoke* shows to be the wrong target; the rationale lacks a criterion, and Quong argues that it therefore condemns too much or explains nothing (Quong 2020, [p.]).

I argue that the means principle forbids spending persons, not using them. To spend a person is to impose on her, or let her suffer, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear. A loss is the cost of supplying a good when the good could be had without the loss if what she contributes came from some other source. In *Wedge*, a beam wedged under the trapdoor would spare the man, so his death is the cost of what his body supplies; in *Smoke*, as I noted, a beam would not. The view breaks with Quong's, which condemns harmful use as such, and goes beyond Ramakrishnan's, which it matches for harms to persons. The idea is Quinn's and the rationale his successors'; what is new is a test that makes the idea precise, an argument that it is the right test, and what follows from it.

The principle is one constraint among several, and it is silent about people who are merely in the way. I take duties of rescue as given, offer no theory of property, and set aside harmless uses, since the principle concerns losses. Section 2 states the view and its test, section 3 argues that it is the right version of the principle, section 4 extends it to omissions and holdings, and section 5 answers objections, chiefly one from looping trolley cases.

## 2. Using and Spending

The view I defend says that the means principle forbids spending persons. A log burned for heat is a resource: those who burn it decide whether its heat is worth its being burned. A tree felled because it stands where a road will go, and left where it falls, is a casualty: nothing it supplies is weighed, because it supplies nothing. The log is a resource because others decide whether its service is worth what that service costs it. That is exactly what persons are said not to be. Quinn's ideal is that each person be treated "as existing only for purposes that he can share" (Quinn 1989, [p.]), and Quong gives each person "a sphere of independence where her decisions are sovereign" (Quong 2020, [p.]).[^1]

Stated in full, the view is this:

> **The Spending View.** The good that a person's contribution would supply to others cannot justify imposing on her, or letting her suffer, the loss that supplying it would cost her, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear.

A person's contribution is whatever of hers serves others: her body, its parts and position, her time and labour, her holdings. A loss is a setback she would not suffer under the alternative. To spend her is to impose such a loss on her, or let her suffer it, for the sake of the good her contribution supplies, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear. "For the sake of" concerns justification, not motive: the good is what would be offered to justify the loss. A loss she is duty bound to bear for the good may be imposed, and so may a loss she agrees to bear.[^2]

The view needs a test for when a loss is the cost of supplying a good. I propose a replacement test: her loss is the cost of her supplying a good if, were what she contributes provided from some other source, the good could be had without that loss. Take the footbridge case, in which the only way to stop a trolley bound for five people is to push a large man from a bridge into its path. Were the stopping supplied by something else, such as a heavy weight, the five could be saved and he could live. So his death is the cost of what his body supplies, and pushing him spends him. The test varies what the person contributes, never the person: she stays where she is, and only the source of her contribution changes.[^3]

The familiar tests vary the person instead. Quinn asked whether an agent would still need his victims' cooperation if they had shelters or medicines of their own (Quinn 1989, [p.]); his successors, Quong among them, ask what the agent could achieve in the victim's absence (see Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.] n. 21). Absence tests fail. In one of Ramakrishnan's cases, a woman lies on the only pill that could cure five people of a deadly disease, and harmless tests on her could yield a second cure. She has a heart attack, and the doctor leaves it untreated so that the tests can run (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). The five could be saved in her absence, with the pill, so an absence test finds nothing amiss; yet leaving her to die seems plainly wrong. The replacement test condemns it, since were the information her body yields supplied by another source, the doctor could save the five and treat her. Is it just another counterfactual test? It is. But it varies only the source of her contribution, never her presence or her position, so it never has to say what would fill the place she leaves empty.[^4]

The view has a second feature: what a person supplies is judged against what the agent could otherwise do. A justification is a reason for one option rather than another, so her contribution supplies a good only if the good is a respect in which the act is better for others than an alternative. Suppose six people stand behind the man on the side track, so that his body will shield them from the diverted trolley. They are safe if the agent does not divert, so their safety is no respect in which diverting is better. What makes diverting better is the rescue of the five, and nothing of his supplies that: diverting is permissible. When the side track loops back to the five, by contrast, the whole improvement comes from his body (section 5). Michael Otsuka draws the contrast by intention, and Ramakrishnan discusses a version with twenty behind (Otsuka 2008, [p.]; Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]).[^5] The comparison also corrects Larry Alexander, who counts only benefits "excluding those causally derived from usings" and so forbids this diversion, measuring it against the man's absence, which the agent cannot choose (Alexander 2016, [p.] and n. 8 (verify)).

Together the two features sort the canonical pairs. The footbridge case is spending; the diversion case is not, since nothing of the one supplies the five's rescue. Ramakrishnan's own pair has the shape of the opening pair, now for letting die. A man's legs hold open the door of a room where five people are dying of a virus, and a doctor gives his scarce medicine to the five rather than to the man (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). Were the door held by something else, the man would still go without, since there is not enough medicine for him and the five, so the doctor acts permissibly. The patient left untreated so that tests can run, by contrast, is spent. Using a person is thus neither necessary nor sufficient for spending her: the man at the door, like the man in *Smoke*, is used without being spent, and the patient is spent though the tests on her are harmless.

Two cases from S. Matthew Liao and Christian Barry show that the replacement test needs no stipulation about empty places (Liao and Barry 2020, [p.]). In one, a defender can stop an attack only by shooting a man off a roof onto the attacker; had he not been there, builders would have put a log there for her to shoot instead. An absence test says that his presence costs her an option and so weakens his claim, yet shooting him is as wrong as ever. In the other, a defender's flamethrower kills an attacker and a bystander behind him; had the bystander not stood in front of a door, fifty people would have come through it into the flames. An absence test says that his presence costs her nothing, so his claim is at full strength and the act forbidden, yet it seems permissible. The replacement test sorts both: the man on the roof supplies the stopping and would be spared were it supplied otherwise, and the bystander supplies nothing relative to the defender's alternative, since the fifty are safe if she does not fire.[^6]

A reader may suspect that the view is Ramakrishnan's principle renamed. On his principle, a justification for infringing someone's claim that holds because she "will be useful to others ... only if C is infringed" is "substantially weaker" than it otherwise would be (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). For harms to persons the two agree closely, but they differ in four ways. First, the view grounds its comparison with the agent's alternatives, and his treatment of cases where a person's position prevents "a would-be defeater" of a justification falls out as a special case (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). Second, it is explained by a permission almost everyone accepts (section 3), where he takes his principle as primitive. Third, it covers holdings, which Quong presses against him (section 4). Fourth, it disables the justification within a bound set by duty, where his principle discounts it.

## 3. Why Spending Matters

The replacement test sorts the cases. But why should the means principle track it? Ramakrishnan doubts that such a principle has a deeper explanation, and Uwe Steinhoff doubts that any rationale for it succeeds (Steinhoff 2019). If a principle's best version is the one that does its job, no more and no less, the question is what the means principle's job is. My answer is the following argument.

> **The argument.**
>
> (P1) The means principle exists to keep morality from treating persons as resources for others at their own expense.
>
> (P2) To treat something as a resource for others at its expense is to treat the question whether a good it can supply them is worth what supplying it costs it as a question for others to settle.
>
> (P3) Beyond what a person owes, that question is hers to settle, and she settles it by agreeing or refusing; within what she owes, her duty has already settled it.
>
> (P4) Others settle it for her exactly when they impose on her, or let her suffer, the loss that supplying a good would cost her, for the sake of that good.
>
> (C) So the principle should hold that the good that a person's contribution would supply to others cannot justify imposing on her, or letting her suffer, the loss that supplying it would cost her, beyond what she owes or agrees to bear. That is, it should forbid spending her.

Premise 1 is common ground: Alexander states the principle's "core idea" as that "we are not resources for others' benefit without our consent" (Alexander 2016, [p.]). Premise 2 is the analysis given with the log and the tree. Premise 3 is inherited, and I defend it below. Premise 4 is where the view does its work: section 2 supplied the test, and the rest of this section argues that only spending settles the question for her.

Premise 3 rests on a permission that nearly every non-consequentialist accepts: no one need give a kidney, or her life, to others, even when they would gain more than she loses. Premise 3 adds that this permission not to give is more than a permission to refuse. Imagine a rule that let a hospital take a kidney from anyone whose kidney would save a life, while leaving everyone free to refuse to donate. No one would say that the rule respects the permission: every refusal stands, and the kidney is taken anyway.[^7] The permission's point explains why. As Walen puts it, a person is "a free agent leading her own independent life", whom morality "cannot treat ... as though she may be pressed into duty to serve others at great expense to herself" (Walen 2016, [p.]). Whether her body serves others at a cost to her is part of that life, and a permission that others could override by taking would leave the question at their disposal, settling only who does the taking. Within what she owes, her duty has already settled it; that is Quinn's exception for strong moral obligations. Mack and Alexander pair the permission with the principle in the same way (Mack 2005, [p.]; Alexander 2016, [p.]).[^8]

Quong argues that no rationale of this kind can work (Quong 2020, [p.]). Read as the claim that "if Y is not duty bound to bear some cost, C, for the sake of goal, G, then it is wrong to impose C on Y for the sake of G", the rationale condemns the diversion case, since the one on the side track is not duty bound to die for the five. Read as Tadros's claim that the wrong is to "compel [a victim] to act for the sake of some end that she is permitted to reject for herself", it faces a dilemma. If compelling means imposing unowed costs, this is the first reading again. If it means using her body or property without her consent or duty, the explanation "amounts to saying: harmful using is distinctively wrong because it involves harmful using".

Both readings assume that the permission not to give limits what a person must bear. It does not. It limits what she must supply and what she must do. The diversion case imposes a cost on the one, but no cost of supply, so the first reading is not the rationale I have given. Nor is my explanation circular, since the replacement test speaks of sources, goods and costs, never of use; this also meets Liao and Barry's charge that such rationales end up sounding "very much like the MP" (Liao and Barry 2020, [p.]). Quong's own rationale has the right structure, since rights over a fair share of the world exist "to block" usefulness "from serving as a reason" (Quong 2020, [p.]). But its object is too wide. What should be blocked, as *Smoke* shows, is the good a person's contribution supplies, as a reason to impose on her the cost of supplying it.

Premise 4 says that others settle the question for her exactly when they spend her. Why only then? The permission protects two things: what she must do, and what of hers must serve. Others override the first only by making her act, which is why coercion is forbidden; they override the second exactly by spending her, as the hospital's rule does. A side-effect harm does neither. Its loss is not the cost of anything of hers serving: either nothing of hers serves, as in the diversion case, or the loss would fall on her all the same were what serves supplied from elsewhere, as the smoke falls in *Smoke*. So it falls under the ordinary constraint against harming, which weighs harms against goods. Why not all harms? Because, in Mack's terms, the permission lets each person "devote *herself*" to her own ends, and the matching restriction forbids others to devote her "energy, attention, bodily parts, and so on" to theirs (Mack 2005, [p.]); a side-effect harm devotes nothing of anyone. The complaints differ accordingly. The person spent is taken from, and the value of what was taken cannot answer that complaint; the person harmed as a side effect is harmed, and that complaint is weighed. This also answers Steinhoff's question whether using a person's body is worse than discarding it (Steinhoff 2019, [p.]): it is, when the use is at the cost of what she supplies. His own alternative, a right to decide "in pursuit of which ends her body is to suffer adverse side effects" (Steinhoff 2019, [p.]), would make the diversion case as hard to justify as the footbridge case.

The distinction answers Judith Jarvis Thomson's argument against turning the trolley. Her bystander could turn the trolley onto himself, onto one workman, or not at all. If he turns it onto the workman because he would rather not die, he makes "the one workman pay the cost of his good deed because he doesn't feel like paying it himself", which is worse than stealing money for Oxfam (Thomson 2008, 364). Since an ordinary bystander would not turn the trolley onto himself, she concludes, he may not turn it onto the one (Thomson 2008, 365–366). The analogy fails. The thief makes another's money serve his good deed; turning the trolley makes nothing of the workman's serve. The argument slides from serving to doing: others may turn a trolley onto her that she need not turn onto herself (compare Tadros 2011, [p.]; Cullity 2018, [p.] n. 45).

A last worry is Ramakrishnan's. A principle of this kind makes permissibility turn on a person's "location in causal space", which strikes him as "a terribly arbitrary feature of his situation" (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). It does so in a fine-grained way: his principle lets a person's position prevent "a would-be defeater" of a justification, as in the case of twenty behind, and does not forbid harming someone who is a necessary means when medicine is short (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]). The view explains the grain. What matters is whether she is made to give, and the test for being made to give has this shape: the comparison with alternatives removes the case of twenty behind, and replacement removes the cases of shortage. Giving is a structural notion: to give is to be the source of what another receives. A moral notion with a causal condition is not arbitrary on that account. Whether you have stolen from me depends on where what you have came from, and theft is no arbitrary wrong.

## 4. Omissions and Holdings

The view covers omissions as well as acts. Jonathan Parry, adapting a case of Tadros's, describes a man who slips on ice and slides toward a trolley that will otherwise paralyse five people (Parry 2023, [p.]; Tadros 2011, [p. 122]). His body would slow the trolley enough to save the five, and he would be paralysed. A passer-by could easily stop him, and refrains so that his body will stop the trolley. That seems wrong, though it is permissible to let a man be hit when two trolleys threaten him and five others and she can stop only one. Quong can separate the cases only by giving the sliding man a prior right to be rescued; Parry argues that the five's claims would extinguish such a right, and concludes that the principle must help determine rights rather than merely follow them (Parry 2023, [p.]).

The Spending View explains the case directly. Letting the man suffer the loss so that his body serves settles, for him, whether it serves at that cost, just as imposing the loss would. It is the hospital's rule by omission: no one asks him, and his body serves the five at his cost all the same. So premise 4 covers omissions, which an account on which the wrong is compelling someone to serve cannot do, since failing to aid compels no one (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.], against Tadros). It follows that the good his body would supply cannot count against rescuing him, and the ordinary duty of easy rescue stands. In the two-trolley case nothing of the man's is supplied, and the numbers decide. Alexander's view has this structure: rescuing such a man is not wrong though it saves fewer, since it sets back the overall good "only by saving someone from being an involuntary means" of promoting it (Alexander 2016, [p.]).[^9]

The view extends to holdings without strain. Quong presses a pair of cases against Ramakrishnan: a swimmer who reaches the only piece of driftwood first, and one who steals another's life jacket (Quong 2020, [p.]). The first seems permissible and the second not, and a principle about persons alone cannot say why. The Spending View can. Taking the life jacket spends its owner, since another jacket would spare her. Winning the race to the driftwood spends no one, since nothing of the loser's supplies anything.

The view also avoids the costs Parry finds in Quong's extension of the principle to property. On Quong's view, turning a trolley toward a man with his own wrench uses him, so that when fifteen people are at risk, the only permissible rescue may be to turn the trolley onto three others instead (Parry 2023, [p.]). On the Spending View, the owner dies of the trolley, not as the cost of the wrench's service: had another tool turned the switch, he would have died all the same. Nor does acquisition impose sweeping new constraints on everyone else, as Parry fears (Parry 2023, [p.]): holdings constrain others only where the owner's loss would be the cost of their service, and the duties that bound the principle grow with holdings.[^10]

The bound does real work here. Someone who uses a stranger's vase as a hammer to free a stuck switch, and breaks it, imposes on the owner a loss that is the cost of what the vase supplies, since another hammer would have spared it. But the loss falls within what the owner owes under a duty of easy rescue, so the act does not spend her. It is permitted, and she is owed redress.

## 5. Objections

The most serious objection comes from a looping trolley. In *Loop*, a trolley is headed for five people. An agent can divert it onto a side track that loops back toward them, and on the loop stands one man whose body will stop the trolley, killing him; if the trolley went straight, he would be safe (Choo 2025, [p.]; Liao et al. 2012, [p.]). Most people judge that diverting is permissible, and Frederick Choo argues that the judgment is robust: it survives controls for order effects, most participants accept it when no other case is shown first (81% in one study), and the philosophers who hold it see that the man is used, so it is no illusion of inattention (Choo 2025, [p.]). I grant all of this.

The view nonetheless forbids diverting. Relative to not diverting, the five survive only because his body stops the trolley, and a stopper placed before him would spare him. That is the footbridge case's structure. Frances Kamm tries to separate the cases: in diverting, she says, the agent removes the first threat and creates "a *new problem*", the trolley looping back, which his body merely prevents (Kamm 2007, 94–95). But the agent has a single goal, to save the five, and diverting serves it only through his body. Otsuka and Choo reject Kamm's description on this ground (Otsuka 2008, [p.]; Choo 2025, [p.]). The comparison with the agent's alternatives keeps the six-behind case permissible, since there the diversion saves the five by itself.[^11]

The view thus overrides a robust intuition, and I should say why it should prevail. Otsuka links *Loop* to a variant of the footbridge case in which a lever moves a pole that topples a man into the trolley's path, a case whose wrongness is, in his words, "a moral fixed point" for non-consequentialists (Otsuka 2008, [p.]). He goes by two routes, each through one intermediate case. In the first, the diverted trolley, as it circles back, itself trips the pole. This differs from *Loop* only in moving the man into the trolley rather than the trolley into the man, a difference Kamm herself argues is insignificant, and it is "morally indistinguishable" from the toppling case, since in both "you push a lever that causes someone to fall from the bridge" (Otsuka 2008, [p.]). In the second, opening a drawbridge sends the trolley up a ramp into a man on a footbridge overhead; it differs from the toppling case in the same way, and appears "completely morally indistinguishable" from *Loop* (Otsuka 2008, [p.]). Otsuka doubts that any principle separates these cases (Otsuka 2008, [p.] n. 25).

Choo answers Otsuka's general claim: those who judge *Loop* permissible see the use, and trust that judgment over the principles that condemn it (Choo 2025, [p.]). Neither point reaches the chain, which needs no one to miss the use and sets the verdict on *Loop* against a verdict on a case, not a principle. So one set of firm judgments must yield, and the principle that explains the wider set, and has an independent rationale, forbids diverting. I concede the cost; this is the one case where the view overrides a robust intuition. Others reach the same verdict: Otsuka, Alexander, Walen, Liao, McMahan and, on reflection, Ramakrishnan (Alexander 2016, n. 8; Walen 2016, [p.]; Liao et al. 2012, [p.] n. 3; Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.] n. 36).[^12]

A second objection is that the view permits too much, since it seems to allow driving over a man who lies across the only road to people in need of rescue. The view is silent on that case, not permissive. He supplies nothing; what the driver needs is his absence. Other constraints forbid running him over. Quinn condemns it under his account of doing and allowing, since the driver intends an action of the car that will kill the man (as Liao and Barry 2020, [p.], discuss), and Walen now defends a claim to the space one occupies (Walen 2022, [p.]).

A third objection concerns motive. Parry describes a rescuer who is willing to lose an arm to stop a sliding man, sees five people further down the track, and refrains so that the man's body will stop the trolley (Parry 2023, [p.]). Refusing a rescue that would cost an arm is normally permitted, yet this refusal seems wrong.

The view can explain the case if one assumption holds. Suppose the permission not to give is a standing to favour oneself that is used in choosing, as premise 3's talk of the question being hers to settle suggests. Then, where conduct would otherwise spend someone, the permission justifies it only if the agent acts on it. The rescuer refuses for what the man's body will supply, not to spare her arm, so her permission does not justify the refusal; nor can the five's rescue, since the good his body supplies is disabled. Agent-neutral justifications justify whatever the motive, so motive matters only here. The view therefore disagrees with Liao and Barry about an enemy who turns a trolley from five people onto one only because he hates the one: he acts permissibly, if viciously (Liao and Barry 2020, [p.]).[^13]

Finally, the view may seem absolutist. It is not. Duties grow with the stakes, and the principle yields where duty yields: if a person can owe her life to save a million, taking it does not spend her. Whether she can is a question about the content of duty that the view inherits and does not settle. Ramakrishnan's principle, by contrast, meets large stakes by discounting the justification rather than bounding the disabler (Ramakrishnan 2016, [p.]).

## 6. Conclusion

I have argued that the means principle forbids spending persons, not using them. A replacement test, with supply judged against the agent's alternatives, sorts the opening pair, the canonical pairs, and the cases that defeat tests by absence. It gives Quinn's idea a precise form, and it repairs the rationale his successors offered, which fails when aimed at use and succeeds when aimed at spending. It extends to omissions and holdings, and it forbids diverting in *Loop*, at a cost I have stated.

The view is not the whole of the ethics of harm. It is silent about people who are merely in the way, and it takes duties of rescue as given. Within its scope, it says what the means principle protects. What separates *Smoke* from *Wedge* is not what the firefighter does with the man's body, but whether his death is the price of what it gives.

A spiteful donor who declines to give spends no one; the needy supply nothing, and their suffering is the object of her spite, not the cost of anything they give. Walen (2022, [p.]) holds instead that a malicious diverter "forfeits her claim to be free not to save the five". On intentions generally, see Tadros (2020).

## Sketch A — The Spending View (rank 1; recommended)

**Thesis (P/Q/R).** I argue that the Means Principle should prohibit *spending* persons — counting the good a person supplies to others as a reason for imposing on her a loss that is the cost of her supplying it — rather than *using* them, against Quong's version (keyed to use of body or property plus a presence test) and in place of Ramakrishnan's view that his closely related principle has no deeper explanation, on the grounds that spending is exactly what it is to treat something as a resource, which is what the Means Principle exists to rule out.

**The principle (draft display).** A good that a person supplies to others cannot count as a reason for imposing on her, by act or omission, a loss that is the cost of her supplying it — unless she owes that supply at that cost, or consents.

**Central argument (premise form).**
1. The Means Principle's job, across the uses it is put to (the paired trolley and rescue cases, the limit on lesser-evil justification, non-combatant immunity, the ethics of punishment and of strikes), is to keep morality from treating persons as resources for others.
2. To treat something as a resource for others is to count the good it supplies to them as a reason for imposing on it the losses that are the cost of its supplying that good. (A log burned for heat is a resource; a tree felled to make way for a road is a casualty of the road.)
3. Requiring a person to give what she owes does not treat her as a resource: the reason is one she herself has, and the demand addresses her as an agent.
4. So the Means Principle should prohibit counting the good a person supplies as a reason for imposing on her the loss that is the cost of supplying it, beyond what she owes or consents to.

**The test.** Her loss is the cost of her supplying the good if, were that contribution supplied by something else, her loss would be avoidable. "Supplying" means making a positive difference to the good that would justify the act, not merely preventing the act's own collateral damage.

**Key steps.** (i) The puzzle: *Room* (a man's legs keep a door open; the medicine goes to five others) versus *Heart Attack* (a woman is left untreated so that harmless tests on her can cure five), and a borrowed wrench versus a stolen life jacket — why should such fine structure matter? (ii) Derive the principle from the Means Principle's function (above). (iii) Show the test sorts the standard pairs and the new cases: Bridge and Switch, Scarcity and Guinea Pig, Room and Heart Attack, Tracks. (iv) Omissions without prior rights: Parry's *Icy Trolley* (a sliding man's body would stop a trolley bound for five) is spending, his *Icy Trolleys* (two separate trolleys) is not — so the principle helps fix rescue rights instead of presupposing them. (v) Holdings: the principle covers property only where the owner's loss is the cost of the holding's service to others (the life jacket, the drug, the decoy house) and not where property is merely used on the way (the wrench), which removes Quong's perverse *Three-Track* result and answers Parry's worry that property-inclusion lets acquisitions reshape others' protections.

**Payoff.** A version of the principle with a rationale, a precise test, and one structure (disabled beyond duty — neither a discount nor an absolute), which gets the disputed cases right and explains why their fine structure matters: not because of anyone's location in causal space, but because of whether a person's loss is the price of what she is made to give.

**Main objection and reply.** *Objection:* the test is Ramakrishnan's "in virtue of usefulness" relation relabeled, so the view still ascribes great weight to causal structure. *Reply:* what the test tracks is whether a loss is the cost of what the victim gives — the difference between being taken from and losing out in a competition — which has independent moral standing in ordinary thought; the rationale also explains features Ramakrishnan's principle lacks (disabling rather than discounting; the duty limit; the property scope).

**Second objection.** *Loop.* The view condemns Loop, against a common intuition that Choo (2025) argues still defeats the Means Principle. Reply: Loop and a trapdoor version of Bridge have the same spending structure, so no version of the principle can permit Loop without permitting that trapdoor case; the pull of Loop is plausibly explained by the redirection of an existing threat, a doing/allowing feature that sits outside the Means Principle.

**The crux (to be solved first).** Premise 2: that "spending" names a morally deep category (resource-treatment; protection that shrinks as usefulness grows; a person's goods being hers to give) and not a relabeled counterfactual — and that the test is not gerrymandered (the replacement must be read existentially: some other supply would make her loss avoidable).

**Limits flagged in advance.** On the objective test alone, Parry's *Costly Icy Trolley* and Christensen's *Streets* come out permissible; Sketch B's point about prerogatives can enter as one short section if space allows, otherwise it is conceded as a limitation.

**Must-read literature before Plan.** Øverland 2014 (*Ethics*); Walen 2016 and 2022 (*Law and Philosophy*); Liao & Barry 2020 (*Law and Philosophy*); Alexander 2016; Choo 2025 (*PPR*); Otsuka 2008 (*Utilitas*); Kamm, *Intricate Ethics* (triple effect; permissible harm); Frowe, *Defensive Killing* (the means principle chapter).

**Ranking rationale.** Highest S and best fit to the brief (a full new version derived from the principle's function). O is moderate-high: the rationale, the property scope, and the use/spend distinction look new; the core test is close to Ramakrishnan's for harms to persons, which the essay must say up front.

## Sketch B — Keeping and Taking: When Intentions Matter to the Means Principle (rank 2)

**Thesis (P/Q/R).** I argue that intentions bear on the Means Principle only through agent-relative prerogatives — a permission to *keep* one's own resources for one's own sake does not cover a refusal of aid undertaken in order to *take* the value of another's plight — against both Quong's claim that intentions are irrelevant to the principle and Tadros's and Parry's inference that the principle itself is intention-based, on the grounds that prerogatives are permissions to weight one's own interests, so their scope is fixed by the reasons acted on, while agent-neutral justifications are not.

**Central argument.**
1. Agent-relative prerogatives are permissions to give one's own interests extra weight in deciding whether to aid.
2. A permission to give extra weight to certain considerations is exercised only by acting on those considerations; a refusal made for other reasons is not an exercise of it.
3. When the only other consideration that could justify the refusal is the value of the victim's plight to others, the (objective) Means Principle disables it.
4. So a refusal made in order to exploit someone's plight is impermissible, while the same refusal made for self-regarding reasons is permissible.

**Key steps.** Parry's *Costly Icy Trolley* (a rescuer who would lose an arm declines, first for her arm, then in order that the sliding man's body stop the trolley); Christensen's *Streets* (a passer-by declines to help homeless people so that their visible suffering will deter her rival from buying a house); exploitative strikes; contrast with Quong's *Murders Averted* (a hateful killer of a liable aggressor still acts permissibly, because the justification there is agent-neutral). Legal model: the civil-law doctrine of abuse of rights.

**Payoff.** A precise answer to a live dispute: intentions matter to the principle exactly where the agent relies on a prerogative, and nowhere else; the principle stays objective for acts.

**Main objection and reply.** *Objection:* permissibility cannot turn on which of two available reasons the agent acts on — she could just switch reasons. *Reply:* the prerogative is a permission to act on certain reasons; "switching" is exercising it, and the resulting requirement is disjunctive (rescue, or decline for your own sake).

**The crux.** Premise 2: that prerogatives are reason-relative in a way agent-neutral justifications are not.

**Ranking rationale.** High O and high feasibility; narrower S, and it is a component of a version rather than a full new version of the principle (it presupposes an objective core like Sketch A's).

## Sketch C — The Jurisdiction View: A Service Conception of the Means Principle (rank 3)

**Thesis (P/Q/R).** I argue that harmfully using a person is pre-empting her judgment about what her body, agency, or holdings are to serve, and that such pre-emption is legitimate only as a service to her own reasons (her duties) or with her consent, against Quong's distributive-justice rationale and Tadros's compulsion rationale, on the grounds that reasons for outcomes cannot confer authority — so the numbers never license use by themselves — which also explains the duty limits, consent, the wrongness of harmless uses, and the parallel with the state's authority to tax and conscript.

**Central argument.**
1. To use a person harmfully is to decide, in her place, what her body or holdings are for.
2. Such pre-emption is legitimate only if it helps her conform to reasons that apply to her, or she consents (after Raz's normal justification and dependence theses).
3. Others' need gives her reason to contribute only up to the limit of her duties of aid.
4. Harming as a side effect pre-empts nothing; it is an exercise of the agent's own liberty, governed by the balance of claims.
5. So the numbers can justify side-effect harm but can never, by themselves, justify use.

**Main objection.** Pushing a man is not an exercise of authority, which is a power to issue directives; the view re-describes a claim violation. **Crux:** premises 1 and 4 — the authority reading of use.

**Ranking rationale.** Creative cross-literature derivation, significant if it works; but Alexander (2016) already frames the principle as "jurisdictional," Tadros (2011) speaks of "taking command" of a body within a duty, and the content converges with Tadros and Quong, so O is moderate.

## Sketch D — Levers: Harms That Work Through Other Minds (rank 4; applied option)

**Thesis (P/Q/R).** I argue that harms whose benefit flows through another agent's will (terror bombing, hostage-taking, deterrent punishment, coercive strikes, sanctions) fall under a stricter version of the Means Principle — permissible only when the victim is responsible for the conduct they aim to change, or consents — against treating them as ordinary means-harms (Quong, Ramakrishnan) or settling them by case-by-case thresholds (Christensen), on the grounds that such harms make the victim's suffering a message addressed to someone else.

**Central argument.**
1. Some harms benefit others only by changing a third party's choices.
2. In such cases the victim's suffering is conscripted as a message to another agent, so its justification needs a link between the victim and the addressee's conduct.
3. Liability for a threat one poses does not supply that link (Tadros's *Mines*: a combatant who may be killed to stop her own threat may not be burned to terrorize her comrade).
4. So lever-harms require the victim's responsibility for the conduct they target.

**Main objection.** Lever-harms are just means-harms; the extra condition is ad hoc. **Crux:** premise 2 — that the communicative structure adds a distinct wrong.

**Ranking rationale.** Open on the pre-screen and practically significant (strikes, sanctions, war), but it is a sub-principle rather than a new version of the principle, so the weakest fit to the brief.

Displays (final wording, subject to 4C rounds):

> **The Conferral Account.** A responsible agent is liable to defensive harm when she acts upon
> another person without an entitlement that his own acts, the balance of claims, or a legitimate
> authority has conferred, or when she imposes risk on him without due care, unless he has
> knowingly induced her to act as she does. She is liable in no other way.

> **The argument.** (1) A responsible agent is liable to defensive harm when she is about to
> violate another's claim and he has standing to enforce it. (2) When she acts upon him, she
> violates his claim unless an entitlement conferred by his own acts, by the balance of claims, or
> by a legitimate authority permits what she does; her evidence confers no such entitlement.
> (3) When she merely imposes risk on him, she violates his claim only if she fails to take due
> care. (4) Any conduct that threatens him either acts upon him or merely imposes risk on him.
> (5) So she is liable exactly when she acts upon him without such an entitlement, or imposes risk
> on him without due care, and he has not lost his standing by knowingly inducing her conduct.

## §1 Introduction (600)

- **1.1** [130] *Mistaken Attacker*, three sentences: warned that an escaped killer attacks on
  sight, a resident opens his door at night to the killer's identical twin, whose car has broken
  down, and lunges at him with a knife (McMahan 2005a, 387; Quong 2012, 53). Verdict, lightly
  hedged: the twin may fight back, and an officer who knows the truth may stop the resident (Quong
  2012, 60–61). So the resident is liable, though he acted as any reasonable person would.
- **1.2** [130] *Conscientious Driver*: a careful driver's well-kept car veers, through a freak
  failure, toward a pedestrian, who can save herself only by destroying the car and killing him
  (McMahan 2005a, 393). Many hold he is not liable (Quong 2012, 56–57; Ferzan 2012, 682; Burri
  2025 §4); McMahan disagrees. *Bluff*: a prankster points an unloaded gun at his manager and
  shouts "Time to die!", and she breaks his leg. He may not fight back, others may not defend him,
  and he is owed nothing (Clark 2025, 431–32).
- **1.3** [120] The puzzle: in the first case liability follows the facts (the twin's innocence
  beats a reasonable belief); in the second it follows the evidence (due care decides, though the
  pedestrian is as innocent as the twin); in the third a man who threatens no one bears the cost
  of how he appeared. Accounts that fix one standpoint keep two of these verdicts and lose one.
- **1.4** [130] Thesis: the Conferral Account, compressed. Liability follows the entitlement the
  conduct needs and who can confer it. Acting upon a person needs an entitlement that only that
  person's own acts, the balance of claims, or a legitimate authority can confer, never the
  agent's evidence; imposing risk needs only the licence due care confers. Neighbours, same
  paragraph: Quong's Moral Status Account shares the first two verdicts but tests the wrong thing;
  Burri shares the enforcement framing but clears the resident.
- **1.5** [90] Scope: whether one is liable, not how much; not blame; not a claim that the resident
  acted wrongly given his evidence. Roadmap, one sentence per section.

## §2 The standpoint problem (800)

- **2.1** [120] Thomson: whoever will otherwise violate your right lacks a right against your
  defence, and agency is not needed to violate a right (Thomson 1991, 301–302), so the falling man
  is liable (287). Cost, in Ferzan's words: a right fundamental enough to kill for, lost "by mere
  misfortune" (Ferzan 2012, 675).
- **2.2** [140] McMahan's responsibility account: liability is "moral responsibility, through
  action that lacks objective justification, for a threat of unjust harm" (McMahan 2005a, 394). It
  gets the resident right but makes the careful driver liable (393–94), a case McMahan himself
  says draws "only weak or doubtful intuitions" (403). *Bluff* forces a second clause, responsibility
  "for appearing to pose a threat" (McMahan 2011, 555–56).
- **2.3** [150] Accounts fixed by the agent's evidence. For Ferzan, culpable aggression is
  necessary (Ferzan 2012, 670), so reasonable defenders "do all that morality can fairly ask of
  them" (682 n. 49). Burri makes liability turn on acting against the subjective requirements of
  another's rights (2025 §4). Both clear the driver, but also the resident; Burri must say he "acts
  disrespectfully" by opening his door (§4). Neither can explain Police Intervention, or the
  peacekeepers who may stop soldiers duped into shelling a village (Quong 2012, 60–62). Burri calls
  this counterintuitive and accepts it (2022, 552; 2025 §6.1).
- **2.4** [140] Quong's mixed test, quoted: liability when "the evidence-relative permissibility of
  choosing X depends either on the assumption that those who are harmed … are liable to the harm,
  or else on false moral beliefs" (Quong 2012, 67–68). Its rationale is that such agents treat
  others "as if" they lack rights (64–67). It gets the resident and the driver right.
- **2.5** [130] Clark's objections: too narrow (a grenade thrown into a warehouse on false
  intelligence about a lesser evil; a hiker's grenade that hits unseen neighbours) and arbitrary (two
  trolley cases differing only in an unknown back-up justification) ("As If", §III). And every
  account that makes bluffers liable faces problems with necessity, proportionality, incentives
  and aggregation (Clark 2025, 435–41).
- **2.6** [120] Diagnosis. All these accounts share the enforcement premise: whoever is about to
  violate another's claim lacks a claim against proportionate force to stop her (Burri 2022,
  546–47; Thomson 1991; Quong 2020, verify — #1). They differ over what makes conduct a violation,
  and each answers with a standpoint: the world's, the agent's, or a test on the agent's reasoning.
  I answer with what the conduct needs, and who can supply it.

## §3 The Conferral Account (1,150)

- **3.1** [70] Display 1, then a one-sentence gloss: liability borrows its standard from the
  entitlement the conduct needs.
- **3.2** [140] Definition: an agent *acts upon* a person when she aims at that person's harm,
  knowingly harms her, or singles her out to bear a risk. She merely *imposes risk* when her conduct
  exposes whoever happens to be affected. Placements: the resident and the warehouse
  grenade-thrower act upon their victims; the careful driver and the hiker impose risk.
- **3.3** [90] Borderline cases: near-certain harm to a particular person counts as singling out;
  a statistically certain death in a vaccination programme, no one in particular, does not. The
  line can blur near certainty, but its point is clear: does the plan pick out a particular person
  to bear the burden?
- **3.4** [140] First argument, from consent. No one can consent for another, and a reasonable
  belief that someone consented is not her consent: her permission comes from her, not from what
  others believe about her (Ferzan 2012, 690 n. 70). Entitlements over a person are conferred the
  same way.
- **3.5** [130] Forfeiture, overriding and authority. Forfeiture is the change in a person's
  position that follows her own conduct; that is why it answers Renzo's demand for a principle
  explaining how "by acting in certain ways we alter our normative status" (Renzo 2017, verify —
  #12). The balance of claims overrides a claim without removing it: infringement, not violation
  (McMahan 2005a, 388; Quong 2012, 51 n. 11). Legitimate authority is the one conferrer that may act
  on evidence (§5). The agent's evidence is none of these.
- **3.6** [150] Second argument, from independence. If the twin's claim against being acted upon
  asked only for due care, his protection would turn on his brother's crimes, the warning and the
  storm: facts about how he appears, none of them his doing. A claim that protects a person only as
  she appears protects her appearance. Burri's hybrid view already makes the protection itself
  objective (2025 §2); since acting upon a person is what that protection is against, the constraint
  on those who act upon her cannot be looser than the protection.
- **3.7** [140] Why the licence to impose risk is conferred by due care. A licence to impose risk
  must be usable by someone who cannot know how things will turn out; a licence that lapsed
  whenever the risk materialised would be no licence at all. Its content is due care: an activity
  imposes risk when it may be pursued only under duties of care (Burri 2020, verify — #8). The
  driver's trip is justified by its own prospects under due care (Frowe 2022, verify — #4).
- **3.8** [110] The pedestrian was never singled out. Her claim against the driver is a claim to his
  due care, which he gave, so he violates nothing. Whether she may defend herself is a separate
  question (§4).
- **3.9** [120] The responsibility condition, derived rather than stipulated. The capacity to alter
  one's claims by one's acts is one capacity, whether the act waives or forfeits; young children
  and the deluded can do neither. So the falling man, who does nothing (Thomson 1991, 287), and the
  drugged aggressor, who lacks the capacity (284), are not liable.

## §4 The argument and the cases (1,100)

- **4.1** [150] Display 2, then defence locations: premise 1 (§2), premises 2–4 (§3), the standing
  clause (§5). The argument is valid; premise 2 carries the weight.
- **4.2** [140] Liable: *Mistaken Attacker*. The twin's resistance enforces an intact claim; an
  officer may help him (Quong 2012, 60–61); peacekeepers may stop the duped soldiers (61–62). The
  resident is blameless and acted permissibly by his evidence, yet owes the twin repair.
- **4.3** [110] The coerced attacker, who shoots a stranger to save her child from torture, is
  liable too: duress excuses but confers no entitlement (Quong 2012, 50; Burri 2025 §4). How much
  she is liable to is another matter (§6).
- **4.4** [140] Not liable: *Conscientious Driver*; the ambulance driver (Quong 2012, 57); the cell
  phone user who unknowingly detonates a bomb (McMahan 2005a, 397–98); the falling man. Modularity:
  whether the pedestrian may kill the driver depends on whether there is an agent-relative
  prerogative (Quong 2009, 517) or a priority of the status quo (Tadros 2012, 272). On either view
  the driver is not liable, and a neutral third party may not take sides.
- **4.5** [130] Justification defeats liability, explained. McMahan asserts it (2005a, 399), and
  Quong finds the assertion ad hoc on McMahan's account (2012, 70). Here it follows: the bomber's
  entitlement, conferred by the balance of claims, exists, so no claim is violated.
- **4.6** [150] Where the account parts from Quong (1). The warehouse grenade-thrower knowingly
  harms the hostages on a lesser-evil entitlement that does not exist: liable, as Clark holds; the
  Moral Status Account says not. In both trolley cases the agent acts upon the victim on an
  entitlement that does not exist: same verdict in both; the Moral Status Account splits them.
- **4.7** [120] Where it agrees with Quong against Clark: the hiker imposes risk with due care and
  is not liable, like the driver. Clark's own note links the two cases ("As If", §III n. 24); I take
  the other branch.
- **4.8** [130] The rationale differs as well. What matters is not treating someone "as if"
  lacking status but acting on an entitlement only others can give. That explains why mistakes
  about justification and about numbers count as much as mistakes about rights.

## §5 Appearances and authority (850)

- **5.1** [140] Clark's challenge: a prankster and a would-be murderer with a jammed gun bear the
  upshots of liability without posing any threat (Clark 2025, 431–32). Treating them as liable
  meets four problems: necessity, proportionality, perverse incentives, aggregation (435–41).
- **5.2** [150] Estoppel, defined at first use: one who knowingly leads another to rely on a state
  of affairs cannot deny it against her reliance (compare the objective theory of assent,
  Restatement §19, via Ferzan 2012, 691). Knowingly inducing reliance is one's own act, so it can
  change one's position. It removes the standing to enforce the claim, not the claim. This is
  Clark's "partial forfeiture" (2025, 442), from a single ground.
- **5.3** [130] Contrasts. Not estopped: the twin, and the victim of false testimony (Clark 2025,
  432), since no act of theirs induced anything; an actor rehearsing a gangster play with a replica,
  unaware of anyone watching (Frowe 2010 §II). Estopped: an actual attacker who meets reasonable but
  unnecessary force (Clark 2025, 455).
- **5.4** [130] Why the four problems do not arise. The claim survives, so necessity and
  proportionality still bind; a bystander who knows the gun is empty should say so; and choosing
  under uncertainty counts infringed claims (Clark 2025, 453–54).
- **5.5** [150] Legitimate authority. Officers acting under fair procedure act on evidence with real
  authority over those within their jurisdiction. The wrongly arrested may not resist, and are owed
  redress through the procedure. This explains the institutional exception Quong notes but leaves
  open (2012, 64 n. 30).
- **5.6** [120] Soldiers lack such authority over foreigners, since there is no common authority
  between states. So the duped soldiers remain liable even if their state is legitimate. The line
  follows who is subject to the authority, not how reasonable the officials are.

## §6 Objections (1,150)

- **6.1** [150] (A) The fair-allocation objection, at its strongest: reasonable mistakes are
  permissible "as the byproduct of a justified social practice for fairly managing the risk of
  suffering unjust harm" (Jorgensen 2021, 143 n. 3, as quoted in Clark 2025, 451; verify — #3).
  Ripstein asks whether it is reasonable to make the apparent aggressor bear the risk of the
  defender's mistake (verify — #5). On this view the twin's claim is only to reasonable treatment.
- **6.2** [160] Reply: compare two principles. Under (F), the target keeps his claim; the defender
  keeps his permission and is blameless, but may not prevail over the innocent's resistance, and
  owes repair. Under (A), the target's claim is only to reasonable treatment. (A) lets a person be
  killed, unable to resist, for another's appearance, a burden he could not avoid. (F) costs the
  defender only the claim to prevail over an innocent he has wrongly attacked, which he could avoid
  by not taking another's fate into his hands. (A) is reasonably rejected; (F) is not.
- **6.3** [110] Response: defenders must act on judgment in the dark; under (F) they act without
  assurance that they will be in the right.
- **6.4** [120] Reply to the response: (F) leaves the permission to act, and the full right against
  real attackers, untouched. The assurance it withholds was never theirs to have: a guarantee that
  an innocent stranger must submit.
- **6.5** [150] (B) Control and fairness: liability should rest on an agential failure (Burri 2022,
  550–51; 2025 §5; Ferzan 2012, 682). Reply: the resident's liability is not a sanction but the other
  side of the twin's intact claim, and he controlled the one thing that mattered, whether to take
  another's fate into his hands. Compare the person who takes an umbrella believing it his: he must
  still give it back (Ferzan 2012, 690).
- **6.6** [90] Concession: culpability still bears on how much harm one is liable to, and on which
  of several liable agents should bear it (Tadros 2021, 3506, 3510).
- **6.7** [130] (C) The driver objection: he chose to set tons of steel in motion (McMahan 2005a,
  393–94); or the line between him and the resident cannot be held (Lang 2022, verify — #2).
- **6.8** [130] Reply: he acts on a valid licence and singles no one out, and nothing in his
  conduct needed an entitlement over the pedestrian. McMahan rates the case weak (403). Lang's
  arguments target Quong's version of the line (verify — #2).

## §7 Conclusion (300)

- **7.1** [170] Restate: liability follows the entitlement one's conduct needs. The three claims:
  entitlements over a person come only from her own acts, the balance of claims, or legitimate
  authority; a licence to impose risk comes from due care; knowingly inducing reliance alters
  standing, not the claim.
- **7.2** [130] Scope, and one flagged open question: how culpability fixes the degree of
  liability, which the account leaves where Tadros put it.

## §1 Introduction

- 1.1 *Mistaken Attacker* (McMahan 2005a, 387; Quong 2012, 53): a resident, warned that an escaped
  killer attacks on sight, opens his door at night to the killer's identical twin, whose car has
  broken down, and lunges with a knife. Verdict: the twin may fight back, and an officer who knows
  the truth may stop the resident (Quong 2012, 60–61). So the resident is liable, though he acted
  reasonably.
- 1.2 *Conscientious Driver* (McMahan 2005a, 393; Quong 2012, 56): a careful driver's well-kept car
  veers, through a freak failure, toward a pedestrian. Verdict, widely but not universally held:
  the driver is not liable (Quong 2012, 56–57; Ferzan 2012, 682; Burri 2025 §4). Contrary view:
  McMahan.
- 1.3 *Bluff* (Clark 2025, 431, after Ferzan 2017): a prankster points an unloaded gun at his
  manager and shouts "Time to die!"; she breaks his leg. Verdict: he may not fight back, others may
  not defend him, he is owed nothing (Clark 2025, 431–32).
- 1.4 The puzzle stated: liability follows the facts in *Mistaken Attacker*, the evidence in
  *Conscientious Driver*, and a harmless man's own appearance in *Bluff*. No single standpoint fits
  all three.
- 1.5 Thesis, compressed and named (the Conferral Account). Same paragraph: Quong's Moral Status
  Account shares the first two verdicts but tests the wrong thing; Burri's view shares the
  enforcement framing but clears the resident.
- 1.6 Scope fences: not blame; the resident's evidence-relative permission stands; whether, not how
  much. Roadmap.

## §2 The standpoint problem

- 2.1 Accounts fixed by the facts:
  - (a) Thomson: one who will otherwise violate your right lacks a right against your defence, and
    "agency is no more required for violating a right than fault is" (Thomson 1991, 301–302).
    Hence the falling man is liable (287). Cost: a fundamental right lost by misfortune (Ferzan
    2012, 675).
  - (b) McMahan: "moral responsibility, through action that lacks objective justification, for a
    threat of unjust harm" (McMahan 2005a, 394). *Conscientious Driver* comes out liable (393–94).
    McMahan himself calls it a case "about which most of us have only weak or doubtful intuitions"
    (403).
  - (c) *Bluff* needs a second clause, responsibility "for appearing to pose a threat" (McMahan
    2011, 555–56). The account becomes disjunctive.
- 2.2 Accounts fixed by the agent's evidence:
  - (a) Ferzan: culpable aggression (purpose, knowledge or recklessness, with no justification or
    excuse) is necessary (Ferzan 2012, 670). The resident is not liable: reasonable defenders "do
    all that morality can fairly ask of them" (682 n. 49).
  - (b) Burri: liability requires acting against the subjective requirements of another's rights
    (Burri 2025 §4). She must say the resident "acts disrespectfully" by opening his door (§4), a
    strained verdict, and she accepts that Duped Soldiers are not liable (§6.1).
  - (c) The cost: Police Intervention and Duped Soldiers (Quong 2012, 60–62). An officer who knows
    the truth may stop the resident; peacekeepers may stop soldiers duped into shelling a village.
    Burri concedes this is counterintuitive (Burri 2022, 552).
- 2.3 The mixed test:
  - (a) Quote Quong: liability when "the evidence-relative permissibility of choosing X depends
    either on the assumption that those who are harmed … are liable to the harm, or else on false
    moral beliefs" (Quong 2012, 67–68); rationale, "treat others as if they lack … rights" (64–67).
    It gets the resident and the driver right.
  - (b) Clark's objections ("As If", §III): too narrow (a grenade thrown into a warehouse on false
    intelligence about a lesser evil; a hiker's grenade that hits unseen neighbours); arbitrary (two
    trolley cases differing only in an unknown backup justification).
  - (c) Apparent attackers treated as liable by every version of "orthodoxy" run into four
    problems: necessity, proportionality, perverse incentives, aggregation (Clark 2025, 435–41).
- 2.4 Diagnosis and the enforcement premise:
  - (a) Shared premise: "threatening a rights violation is both necessary and sufficient for
    liability" (Burri 2022, 547); Thomson and Quong 2020 frame liability the same way (Burri 2022,
    546–47; verify Quong's own wording — #1).
  - (b) So the question is what makes conduct a violation. Each family answers with a standpoint:
    the world's (Thomson, McMahan), the agent's (Ferzan, Burri), or a test on the agent's
    reasoning (Quong).
  - (c) Pivot: the answer should depend instead on what the conduct needs and who can supply it.

## §3 The Conferral Account — CRUX

- 3.1 Display 1 (the view, as in Plan v0.2 §1).
- 3.2 Acting upon versus imposing risk:
  - (a) Definition: an agent *acts upon* a person when she aims at that person's harm, knowingly
    harms her, or singles her out to bear a risk. She merely *imposes risk* when her conduct exposes
    whoever happens to be affected.
  - (b) Placements: the resident acts upon the twin. The grenade-thrower in the warehouse acts upon
    the hostages (knowing harm). The careful driver and the hiker impose risk.
  - (c) Borderline cases (concession iv): near-certain harm to a particular person counts as
    singling out. Statistical certainty over a population does not (a vaccination programme that
    will kill someone, no one in particular).
- 3.3 Who can confer an entitlement over a person (first argument):
  - (a) Consent: no one can consent for another, and a reasonable belief that she consented is not
    her consent (Ferzan 2012, 690 n. 70, on belief and permission).
  - (b) Forfeiture: forfeiture is a change in a person's position that follows her own conduct.
    Renzo's demand for "some other normative principle … to explain why by acting in certain ways we
    alter our normative status" (Renzo 2017; verify wording — #12) is answered: only one's own acts
    alter one's claims.
  - (c) The balance of claims overrides a claim without altering it: infringement, not violation
    (McMahan 2005a, 388; Quong 2012, 51 n. 11).
  - (d) Legitimate authority within its jurisdiction is the one conferrer that may act on evidence
    (developed in §5). So the agent's evidence is not among the conferrers.
- 3.4 The independence argument (second argument):
  - (a) Suppose the twin's claim against being acted upon had due care as its content. Then his
    protection would turn on his brother's crimes, the warning, and the storm: facts about how he
    appears, none of them his doing.
  - (b) A claim that protects a person only as she appears protects her appearance, not her.
    Burri's own hybrid concedes the patient-side protection is objective (Burri 2025 §2). Since
    acting upon a person is the very thing that protection is against, the protection and the
    constraint on those who act upon her cannot come apart.
  - (c) Micro-objection: "the balance of claims also binds her without her act." Reply: it
    outweighs her claim; it does not remove it, so she keeps her claim to repair.
- 3.5 Why a licence to impose risk is conferred by due care:
  - (a) A licence to impose risk must be usable by someone who does not know how things will turn
    out. A licence that lapsed whenever the risk materialised would be no licence.
  - (b) Its content is due care: an activity counts as risk-imposing when it may be permissibly
    pursued only under duties of care (Burri 2020; verify — #8). The driver's single trip is
    justified by its own prospects under due care (Frowe 2022's token point; verify — #4).
  - (c) The pedestrian was never singled out: her claim against the driver is a claim to his due
    care, which he gave.
- 3.6 The responsibility condition, derived:
  - (a) The capacity to alter one's claims by one's acts is the same capacity whether the act
    waives (consent) or forfeits. Children and the deluded can do neither.
  - (b) So the falling man, who does nothing (Thomson 1991, 287), and the drugged aggressor, who
    lacks the capacity (Thomson 1991, 284), are not liable, without a separate stipulation.

## §4 The argument and the cases

- 4.1 Display 2: the five-step argument (Plan v0.2 §6), with defence locations.
- 4.2 Liable: *Mistaken Attacker*; Police Intervention; Duped Soldiers; the coerced attacker (duress
  confers no entitlement; Quong 2012, 50; Burri 2025 §4). The twin's resistance enforces an intact
  claim; the resident is blameless and owes repair.
- 4.3 Not liable: *Conscientious Driver*; the ambulance driver (Quong 2012, 57); the cell phone case
  (McMahan 2005a, 397–98); the falling man.
  - Modularity: whether the pedestrian may kill the driver turns on whether there is an
    agent-relative prerogative (Quong 2009, 517) or instead a priority of the status quo (Tadros
    2012, 272). Either way the driver is not liable, and a neutral third party has no ground to take
    sides.
- 4.4 Justification defeats liability, explained. McMahan asserts it (2005a, 399); Quong calls the
  assertion ad hoc on McMahan's view (2012, 70). On the Conferral Account the bomber's entitlement
  (the balance of claims) exists, so no claim is violated.
- 4.5 Pre-emptive defence against "Quong restated":
  - (a) Warehouse: knowing harm to the hostages on a lesser-evil entitlement that does not exist,
    so liable (Clark's verdict). The Moral Status Account says not liable.
  - (b) The trolley pair: in both, the agent acts upon the victim on a non-existent entitlement, so
    the same verdict in both. The Moral Status Account splits them.
  - (c) The hiker: imposes risk with due care, so not liable, like the driver. The Moral Status
    Account agrees here; Clark disagrees, and his own note ties the hiker to the driver ("As If"
    §III n. 24).
  - (d) Rationale: not "treating as if lacking moral status" but "acting on an entitlement only
    others can give". This explains why mistakes about justification and about numbers count, as
    well as mistakes about rights.

## §5 Appearances and authority

- 5.1 Clark's challenge and the four problems for orthodoxy (Clark 2025, 431–41).
- 5.2 Estoppel as self-conferral:
  - (a) Definition in one sentence: one who knowingly leads another to rely on a state of affairs
    cannot deny it against her reliance. Precedent: the objective theory of assent (Restatement
    §19, via Ferzan 2012, 691).
  - (b) Knowingly inducing reliance is one's own act, so, by the conferral claim, it can change
    one's position. It removes the standing to enforce the claim, not the claim. This is exactly
    Clark's "partial forfeiture" (2025, 442), now with a single ground.
- 5.3 Contrasts:
  - (a) Not estopped: the twin (no act of his); the victim of false testimony (Clark 2025, 432);
    an actor rehearsing a gangster play with a replica gun, unaware of anyone watching (Frowe 2010
    §II).
  - (b) Estopped: an actual attacker who meets reasonable but unnecessary force (Clark 2025,
    455).
- 5.4 Why the four problems do not arise: the claim survives, so necessity and proportionality
  still bind, an informed bystander should speak up, and aggregation counts infringed claims (Clark
  2025, 453–54).
- 5.5 Legitimate authority, scoped:
  - (a) Officers under fair procedure act on evidence with real authority over those within their
    jurisdiction; the wrongly arrested may not resist and are owed redress through the procedure.
    This explains the institutional exception Quong flags but leaves open (2012, 64 n. 30).
  - (b) Soldiers have no such authority over foreigners (there is no common authority between
    states), so Duped Soldiers remain liable even when their state is legitimate.

## §6 Objections

- 6.1 (A) Fair allocation (dedicated; reply-layered):
  - Objection, in its best form: reasonable mistakes are permissible "as the byproduct of a
    justified social practice for fairly managing the risk of suffering unjust harm" (Jorgensen
    2021, 143 n. 3, as quoted in Clark 2025, 451; verify in source — #3). Ripstein asks whether it
    is reasonable to make the apparent aggressor bear the risk of the defender's mistake (verify —
    #5). So the twin's claim is only a claim to reasonable treatment.
  - Reply: compare two principles. Under (F), the target keeps his claim; the defender keeps his
    permission, is blameless, but may not prevail over the innocent's resistance and owes repair.
    Under (A), the target's claim is only to reasonable treatment. (A) lets a person be killed,
    unable to resist, for another's appearance, a burden he could not avoid. (F) costs the defender
    only the claim to prevail over an innocent he has wrongly attacked, which he could avoid by not
    taking another's fate into his hands. So (A) is reasonably rejectable, and (F) is not.
  - Response: defenders need to act on judgment, and under (F) they fight without assurance.
  - Reply to the response: (F) leaves their permission and their defence against actual attackers
    untouched, and the assurance they lose was never theirs to have: a guarantee that an innocent
    stranger must submit.
- 6.2 (B) Control and fairness (Burri 2022, 550–51; Burri 2025 §5; Ferzan 2012, 682): liability
  should rest on an agential failure.
  - Reply: the resident's liability is not a sanction for failure but the other side of the twin's
    intact claim. The resident controlled the one thing that mattered, whether to take another's
    fate into his hands. Compare the reasonable umbrella-taker, who must yield the umbrella (Ferzan
    2012, 690).
  - Concession: culpability still matters for degree and for selecting among liable parties (Tadros
    2021, 3506, 3510).
- 6.3 (C) The driver (McMahan 2005a, 393–94; Frowe 2022; Lang 2022):
  - Objection: he chose to set tons of steel in motion; or the line between him and the resident
    cannot be held (Lang 2022; verify — #2).
  - Reply: he acts on a valid licence and singles no one out. McMahan himself rates the case weak
    (403). Lang's arguments target Quong's version of the line (verify — #2).

## §7 Conclusion

- 7.1 Restate: liability follows the entitlement one's conduct needs. Three claims: conferral; two
  kinds of entitlement; estoppel by one's own act.
- 7.2 Scope, plus the flagged open question: how culpability sets the degree of liability.

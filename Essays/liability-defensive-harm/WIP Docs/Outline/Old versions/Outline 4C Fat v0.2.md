Displays (wording for the draft):

> **The Conferral Account.** A responsible agent is liable to defensive harm when, and only when,
> (a) she acts upon another person without an entitlement conferred by his own acts, by the balance
> of claims, or by a legitimate authority, or (b) she imposes risk on him without due care. The one
> exception: she is not liable to him if he has knowingly or carelessly behaved like an attacker and
> she acts in reasonable reliance on that appearance.

> **The argument.** (1) A responsible agent is liable to defensive harm exactly when she is about to
> violate another person's claim and he has standing to enforce it. (2) When she acts upon him, she
> violates his claim exactly when no entitlement conferred by his own acts, by the balance of claims,
> or by a legitimate authority permits what she does; her evidence confers no such entitlement.
> (3) When she merely imposes risk on him, she violates his claim exactly when she fails to take due
> care. (4) Any conduct that threatens him either acts upon him or merely imposes risk on him.
> (5) So she is liable exactly when she acts upon him without such an entitlement, or imposes risk
> on him without due care, and he has standing to enforce his claim.
> *(Standing premise: to the argument-clinic pass, Coverage Map A5.)*

## §1 Introduction (570)

- **1.1** [120] *Mistaken Attacker*: warned that an escaped killer attacks on sight, a resident opens
  his door at night to the killer's identical twin, whose car has broken down, and lunges at him
  with a knife (McMahan 2005a, 387; Quong 2012, 53). The twin may fight back, and an officer who
  knows the truth may stop the resident (Quong 2012, 60–61). So the resident is liable, though he
  acted as any reasonable person would.
- **1.2** [130] *Conscientious Driver*: a careful driver's well-kept car veers, through a freak
  failure, toward a pedestrian (McMahan 2005a, 393). Many deny he is liable (Quong 2012, 56–57;
  Ferzan 2012, 682; Burri 2025 §4); McMahan disagrees. *Bluff*: a prankster points an unloaded gun
  at his manager and shouts "Time to die!"; she breaks his leg. He may not fight back, others may
  not defend him, and he is owed nothing (Clark 2025, 431–32).
- **1.3** [110] The puzzle: liability follows the facts in the first case (the twin's innocence
  beats a reasonable belief), the evidence in the second (due care decides, though the pedestrian is
  as innocent as the twin), and in the third a man who threatens no one bears the cost of how he
  appeared. Fix one standpoint and you keep two verdicts at most.
- **1.4** [130] Thesis: liability follows the entitlement the conduct needs and who can confer it.
  Acting upon a person needs an entitlement only her own acts, the balance of claims, or a
  legitimate authority can confer, never the agent's evidence; imposing risk needs only the licence
  due care confers; and one who knowingly or carelessly behaves like an attacker loses his standing,
  not his claim. Neighbours: Quong's Moral Status Account shares two verdicts but tests the wrong
  thing; Burri shares the enforcement framing but clears the resident.
- **1.5** [80] Scope: whether one is liable, not how much; liability, not blame; the resident's
  evidence-relative permission stands; liability incurred by threatening, not by failing to rescue
  (Tadros 2012). Roadmap in one sentence per section.

## §2 The standpoint problem (800)

- **2.1** [110] Thomson: whoever will otherwise violate your right lacks a right against your
  defence, and agency is not needed to violate one (Thomson 1991, 301–302); so the falling man is
  liable (287). The cost, in Ferzan's words: a right fundamental enough to kill for, lost "by mere
  misfortune" (Ferzan 2012, 675).
- **2.2** [130] McMahan: liability is "moral responsibility, through action that lacks objective
  justification, for a threat of unjust harm" (McMahan 2005a, 394). Right on the resident, but the
  careful driver comes out liable (393–94), a case McMahan says draws "only weak or doubtful
  intuitions" (403). *Bluff* needs a second clause, responsibility "for appearing to pose a threat"
  (McMahan 2011, 555–56).
- **2.3** [150] Standpoint of the agent's evidence: for Ferzan culpable aggression is necessary
  (2012, 670), since reasonable defenders "do all that morality can fairly ask of them" (682 n. 49);
  Burri ties liability to the subjective requirements of another's rights (2025 §4). Both clear the
  driver, but also the resident (Burri must say he "acts disrespectfully" by opening his door, §4).
  Neither explains Police Intervention or the peacekeepers who may stop soldiers duped into shelling
  a village (Quong 2012, 60–62); Burri accepts the cost (2022, 552; 2025 §6.1).
- **2.4** [130] Quong's mixed test, quoted: liability when "the evidence-relative permissibility of
  choosing X depends either on the assumption that those who are harmed … are liable to the harm, or
  else on false moral beliefs" (Quong 2012, 67, 72). The rationale: such agents treat others "as if"
  they lack rights (64–67). Right on the resident and the driver. Later statements keep the
  dependence test (Quong 2020, 38, 41; verify — #1).
- **2.5** [130] Clark ("As If", §III): too narrow (Warehouse, where an agent knowingly injures two
  hostages on false intelligence of a lesser evil; a hiker whose grenade hits neighbours he could
  not see); and arbitrary (two trolley cases alike in every fact, differing only in whether the
  agent's evidence also offered a lesser-evil back-up). And treating apparent attackers as liable
  meets four problems: necessity, proportionality, incentives, aggregation (Clark 2025, 435–41).
- **2.6** [150] Diagnosis. The rivals share the enforcement premise: "threatening a rights violation
  is both necessary and sufficient for liability" (Burri 2022, 547; cf. Thomson; Quong 2020, verify
  — #1). Its rejecters treat liability as distributive justice (McMahan 2008, 234, and Gordon-Solmon
  2018, 546, both quoted in Clark 2025, 442; verify — #11); Burri answers that liability is "a
  distinctly localised affair" (2022, 540, 545–46). I take the premise as common ground. The rivals
  differ over what violates a claim and answer with a standpoint; I answer with what the conduct
  needs and who can supply it.

## §3 The Conferral Account (1,150)

- **3.1** [70] Display 1, then the gloss: liability borrows its standard from the entitlement the
  conduct needs.
- **3.2** [130] Definitions: an agent *acts upon* a person when she aims at that person's harm,
  knowingly harms her, or singles her out to bear a risk. She *merely imposes risk* when her conduct
  threatens someone without acting upon him, exposing whoever happens to be affected. Placements:
  the resident and Warehouse's grenade-thrower act upon their victims; the careful driver and the
  hiker merely impose risk.
- **3.3** [80] Borderline cases (concession iv): near-certain harm to a particular person counts as
  singling out; a vaccination programme certain to kill someone, no one in particular, does not.
  The test: does the plan pick out a particular person to bear the burden?
- **3.4** [130] First argument, from consent. No one can consent for another, and a reasonable
  belief that she consented is not her consent: her permission comes from her, not from what others
  believe about her (Ferzan 2012, 690 n. 70). An entitlement to act upon a person is conferred the
  same way.
- **3.5** [120] The other conferrers. Forfeiture is the change in a person's position that follows
  her own conduct, which answers Renzo's demand for a principle explaining how "by acting in certain
  ways we alter our normative status" (Renzo 2017; verify — #12). The balance of claims overrides a
  claim without removing it: infringement, not violation (McMahan 2005a, 388; Quong 2012, 51 n. 11).
  Legitimate authority is the one conferrer that may act on evidence (§5). The agent's evidence is
  none of these.
- **3.6** [170] Second argument, from independence. If the twin's claim against being acted upon
  asked only for due care, his protection would turn on his brother's crimes, the warning and the
  storm: facts about how he appears, none of them his doing. A claim that protects a person only as
  she appears protects her appearance. Burri already makes the protection objective (2025 §2); the
  constraint on those who act upon her cannot be looser. Objection: the balance of claims also binds
  her without her act. Reply: it outweighs her claim without removing it, so she keeps her claim to
  repair.
- **3.7** [130] Why due care confers the licence to impose risk. The licence must be usable by
  someone who cannot know how things will turn out; one that lapsed whenever the risk materialised
  would be no licence. Its content is due care: an activity imposes risk when it may be pursued only
  under duties of care (Burri 2020; verify — #8), and a single trip is justified by its own prospects
  under due care (Frowe 2022; verify — #4).
- **3.8** [90] The pedestrian was never singled out. Her claim against the driver is a claim to his
  due care, which he gave, so he violates nothing. Whether she may defend herself is a separate
  question (§4).
- **3.9** [100] Precedent and credit. Private law draws the same line: a good-faith mistake is no
  excuse for using another's person or property (hunters who shot a dog they took for a wolf were
  liable for its value, *Ranson v. Kitner*, 31 Ill. App. 241 (1889); verify), while accidental harm
  answers to negligence. Ripstein treats trespass and battery as wrongs of use (Ripstein 2016;
  verify — #19). What is new here is the application to defensive liability, the argument for it,
  and §5.
- **3.10** [130] The responsibility condition, derived. Objection: consent is chosen, forfeiture is
  not, so they cannot share a source. Reply: both are ways one's own acts alter one's claims, and
  both need the same capacity; young children and the deluded can do neither. So the falling man,
  who does nothing (Thomson 1991, 287), and the drugged aggressor, who lacks the capacity (284), are
  not liable, without a separate stipulation.

## §4 The argument and the cases (1,030)

- **4.1** [160] Display 2, then defence locations: premise 1 (§2), premises 2–4 (§3), the standing
  premise (§5). The argument is valid; premise 2 carries the weight.
- **4.2** [130] Liable: *Mistaken Attacker*. The twin's resistance enforces an intact claim; an
  officer may help him (Quong 2012, 60–61); peacekeepers may stop the duped soldiers (61–62). The
  resident is blameless and acted permissibly by his evidence, yet owes the twin repair.
- **4.3** [90] The coerced attacker, who shoots a stranger to save her child from torture, is liable
  too: duress excuses but confers no entitlement (Quong 2012, 50; Burri 2025 §4). How much she is
  liable to is another matter (§6).
- **4.4** [140] Not liable: *Conscientious Driver*; the ambulance driver (Quong 2012, 57); the cell
  phone user who unknowingly detonates a bomb (McMahan 2005a, 397–98); the falling man. Whether the
  pedestrian may kill the driver depends on whether there is an agent-relative prerogative (Quong
  2009, 517) or a priority of the status quo (Tadros 2012, 272). On either view the driver is not
  liable, and a neutral third party may not take sides.
- **4.5** [120] Justification defeats liability, explained. McMahan asserts it (2005a, 399); Quong
  finds the assertion ad hoc (2012, 70). Here it follows: the justified bomber's entitlement,
  conferred by the balance of claims, exists, so no claim is violated. The same holds when an agent
  does not know of an entitlement: ignorance no more cancels one than evidence confers one.
- **4.6** [150] Where the account parts from Quong. Warehouse: the thrower knowingly harms the
  hostages on a lesser-evil entitlement that does not exist, so he is liable, as Clark holds; the
  Moral Status Account says not. The trolley pair: in both cases the agent acts upon the victim on
  an entitlement that does not exist, so she is liable in both; the Moral Status Account splits
  them.
- **4.7** [110] Where it agrees with Quong against Clark: the hiker imposes risk with due care and
  is not liable, like the driver. Clark's own note links the two cases ("As If", §III n. 24); I take
  the other branch.
- **4.8** [130] The rationale differs too. What matters is not treating someone "as if" lacking
  status but acting on an entitlement only others can give. That explains why mistakes about
  justification and about numbers count as much as mistakes about rights, and it makes no use of
  the "as if" idea, so Clark's worry that Quong's gloss on it is descriptively implausible ("As If",
  §III) does not reach it.

## §5 Appearances and authority (870)

- **5.1** [130] Clark's challenge: a prankster and a would-be murderer with a jammed gun bear the
  upshots of liability without posing any threat (Clark 2025, 431–32). Treating them as liable
  meets four problems: necessity, proportionality, perverse incentives, aggregation (435–41).
- **5.2** [150] Estoppel, defined at first use: one who leads another to rely on a state of affairs
  cannot deny it against her reliance. Leading her to rely is one's own act, so by the conferral
  claim it can change one's position: it removes the standing to enforce the claim, not the claim.
  That is Clark's "partial forfeiture" (2025, 442), from a single ground. Not ad hoc: contract law
  binds a party by the objective manifestation of assent (Restatement §19, via Ferzan 2012, 691).
- **5.3** [140] Knowingly or carelessly. Frowe's actor, rehearsing where he has no reason to expect
  onlookers, is not estopped; Frowe agrees he may defend himself (Frowe 2010 §II, §VI). Rehearse in
  a crowded park and he is estopped. Ferzan refuses liability for a negligently created appearance,
  since such a person "seems … to have done too little to have forfeited his rights" (2012, 691 n.
  72). Here he forfeits nothing; the lesser fault costs only standing.
- **5.4** [110] Contrasts and the unifying principle. Not estopped: the twin, since resemblance is
  not conduct and knocking is not attacking; the victim of false testimony (Clark 2025, 432).
  Estopped: an actual attacker who meets reasonable but unnecessary force (455). Between private
  persons, a claim changes only by its holder's own act, never by what another believes: the
  resident's attack changes his position, the twin's appearance changes nothing, the bluffer's
  performance changes his standing.
- **5.5** [110] Why the four problems do not arise. The claim survives, so necessity and
  proportionality still bind; a bystander who knows the gun is empty should say so; and choosing
  under uncertainty counts infringed claims (Clark 2025, 453–54).
- **5.6** [120] Legitimate authority. Quong sets aside the prison guard who must treat an innocent
  but lawfully convicted prisoner "as if" he lacks rights (2012, 64 n. 30). Here the guard acts on
  an entitlement that fair procedure confers on evidence, so he is not liable: the prisoner may not
  break out by force, and is owed redress through the procedure.
- **5.7** [110] Soldiers lack such authority over foreigners: there is no common authority between
  states. So the duped soldiers remain liable even if their state is legitimate. The line follows
  who is subject to the authority, not how reasonable the officials are.

## §6 Objections (1,030)

- **6.1** [150] (A) The fair-allocation objection, at its strongest: reasonable mistakes are
  permissible "as the byproduct of a justified social practice for fairly managing the risk of
  suffering unjust harm" (Jorgensen 2021, 143 n. 3, as quoted in Clark 2025, 451; verify — #3).
  Ripstein asks whether it is reasonable to make the apparent aggressor bear the risk of the
  defender's mistake (verify — #5). On this view the twin's claim is only to reasonable treatment.
- **6.2** [170] Reply: compare two principles. (F): the target keeps his claim; the defender keeps
  his permission and is blameless, but may not prevail over the innocent's resistance, and owes
  repair. (A): the target's claim is only to reasonable treatment. (A) lets a person be killed,
  unable to resist, for another's appearance, a burden he could not avoid. (F) costs the defender
  only the claim to prevail over an innocent he has wrongly attacked, which he could avoid by not
  taking another's fate into his hands. (A) is reasonably rejected; (F) is not.
- **6.3** [90] Response: defenders must act on judgment in the dark; under (F) they act without
  assurance that they will be in the right.
- **6.4** [120] Reply to the response: (F) leaves the permission to act, and the full right against
  real attackers, untouched. The assurance it withholds was never theirs to have: a guarantee that an
  innocent stranger must submit.
- **6.5** [150] (B) Control and fairness: liability should rest on an agential failure (Burri 2022,
  550–51; 2025 §5; Ferzan 2012, 682). Reply: the resident's liability is not a sanction but the
  other side of the twin's intact claim, and he controlled the one thing that mattered, whether to
  take another's fate into his hands. Compare the person who takes an umbrella believing it his: he
  must still give it back (Ferzan 2012, 690).
- **6.6** [100] Concession: culpability still bears on how much harm one is liable to, and on which
  of several liable agents should bear it (Tadros 2021, 3506, 3510).
- **6.7** [120] (C) The driver objection: he chose to set tons of steel in motion (McMahan 2005a,
  393–94); or the line between him and the resident cannot be held (Lang 2022; verify — #2).
- **6.8** [130] Reply: he acts on a valid licence and singles no one out, and nothing in his conduct
  needed an entitlement over the pedestrian. McMahan rates the case weak (403). Lang's arguments
  target Quong's version of the line (verify — #2).

## §7 Conclusion (250)

- **7.1** [150] Restate: liability follows the entitlement one's conduct needs. The three claims:
  entitlements over a person come only from her own acts, the balance of claims, or legitimate
  authority; a licence to impose risk comes from due care; behaving like an attacker, knowingly or
  carelessly, alters standing, not the claim. One principle beneath them: between private
  persons, a claim changes only by its holder's own acts, never by another's beliefs.
- **7.2** [100] Scope, and one open question: how culpability fixes the degree of liability, which
  the account leaves where Tadros put it.

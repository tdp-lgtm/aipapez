Displays (wording for the draft):

> **The Conferral Account.** A responsible agent is liable to defensive harm when, and only when,
> (a) she acts upon another person without an entitlement conferred by his own acts, by the balance
> of claims, or by a legitimate authority over him, or (b) she imposes risk on him without due care.
> The one exception: she is not liable to defensive harm on his behalf if he has knowingly or
> carelessly behaved like an attacker and she acts in reasonable reliance on that appearance.

> **The argument.** (1) A responsible agent is liable to defensive harm exactly when she is about to
> violate another person's claim and he has standing to enforce it. (2) Acting upon him violates his
> claim exactly when no entitlement conferred by his own acts, by the balance of claims, or by a
> legitimate authority over him permits it; the agent's evidence confers none. (3) Merely imposing
> risk on him violates his claim exactly when it lacks due care. (4) Any conduct that threatens him
> either acts upon him or merely imposes risk on him. (5) He lacks standing against her exactly when
> he has knowingly or carelessly behaved like an attacker and she acts in reasonable reliance on that
> appearance. (6) So she is liable exactly when she acts upon him without such an entitlement, or
> imposes risk on him without due care, unless (5)'s condition holds.

## §1 Introduction (570)

- **1.1** [120] *Mistaken Attacker*: warned that an escaped killer attacks on sight, a resident opens
  his door at night to the killer's identical twin, whose car has broken down, and lunges with a
  knife (McMahan 2005a, 387; Quong 2012, 53). The twin may fight back; an officer who knows the
  truth may stop the resident (Quong 2012, 60–61). So the resident is liable, though he acted
  reasonably.
- **1.2** [130] *Conscientious Driver*: a careful driver's well-kept car veers, through a freak
  failure, toward a pedestrian (McMahan 2005a, 393). Many deny he is liable (Quong 2012, 56–57;
  Ferzan 2012, 682; Burri 2025 §4); McMahan disagrees. *Bluff*: a prankster points an unloaded gun
  at his manager and shouts "Time to die!"; she breaks his leg. He may not fight back, others may
  not defend him, and he is owed nothing (Clark 2025, 431–32).
- **1.3** [90] The puzzle: liability follows the facts in the first case, the evidence in the second
  (though the pedestrian is as innocent as the twin), and in the third a man who threatens no one
  bears the cost of an appearance. Any single standpoint keeps two verdicts at most.
- **1.4** [150] Thesis: liability follows the entitlement the conduct needs, and who can confer it.
  Acting upon a person needs an entitlement that only her own acts, the balance of claims or a
  legitimate authority can confer, never the agent's evidence, because no private person's judgment
  binds another. Merely imposing risk needs only the licence due care confers. One who knowingly or
  carelessly behaves like an attacker keeps his claim but loses the standing to enforce it: he may
  not resist, others may not defend him, and he cannot demand repair. Neighbours: Quong's Moral
  Status Account (two shared verdicts, a different test); Burri (the enforcement framing, but the
  resident cleared).
- **1.5** [80] Scope: whether, not how much; liability, not blame; the resident's evidence-relative
  permission stands; liability for threatening, not for failing to rescue (Tadros 2012). Roadmap.

## §2 The standpoint problem (780)

- **2.1** [100] Thomson: whoever will otherwise violate your right lacks a right against your
  defence, and violating one needs no agency (Thomson 1991, 301–302), so the falling man is liable
  (287). The cost: a right fundamental enough to kill for, lost "by mere misfortune" (Ferzan 2012,
  675).
- **2.2** [110] McMahan: liability is "moral responsibility, through action that lacks objective
  justification, for a threat of unjust harm" (McMahan 2005a, 394). Right on the resident, but the
  careful driver comes out liable (393–94), a case McMahan says draws "only weak or doubtful
  intuitions" (403). *Bluff* needs a second clause: responsibility "for appearing to pose a threat"
  (McMahan 2011, 555–56).
- **2.3** [140] The agent's-evidence standpoint: for Ferzan culpable aggression is necessary (2012,
  670), and reasonable defenders "do all that morality can fairly ask of them" (682 n. 49); Burri
  ties liability to the subjective requirements of rights (2025 §4). Both clear the driver but also
  the resident (Burri: he "acts disrespectfully" by opening his door, §4). So neither explains Police
  Intervention, or the peacekeepers who may stop soldiers duped into shelling a village (Quong 2012,
  60–62). Burri accepts the cost (2022, 552; 2025 §6.1).
- **2.4** [140] Quong's mixed test: liability when "the evidence-relative permissibility of choosing
  X depends either on the assumption that those who are harmed … are liable to the harm, or else on
  false moral beliefs" (Quong 2012, 67, 72); such agents treat others "as if" they lack rights
  (64–67). Right on the resident and the driver. Later statements keep the dependence test (Quong
  2020, 38, 41; verify — #1).
- **2.5** [120] Clark ("As If", §III): too narrow (Warehouse, where an agent knowingly injures two
  hostages on false intelligence of a lesser evil; a hiker whose grenade hits neighbours he could not
  see) and arbitrary (two trolley cases alike in every fact, differing only in whether the agent's
  evidence also offered a lesser-evil back-up). It also treats apparent attackers as liable, with
  the costs listed in §5.
- **2.6** [170] Diagnosis. The rivals share the enforcement premise: "threatening a rights violation
  is both necessary and sufficient for liability" (Burri 2022, 547; cf. Thomson; Quong 2020, verify
  — #1). Its rejecters treat liability as distributive justice (McMahan 2008, 234; Gordon-Solmon
  2018, 546; both quoted in Clark 2025, 442; verify — #11); Burri replies that liability is "a
  distinctly localised affair" (2022, 540, 545–46). I take the premise as common ground. It is
  neutral on what counts as a violation, and there the rivals divide by standpoint. I answer instead
  by what the conduct needs and who can supply it.

## §3 The Conferral Account (1,270)

- **3.1** [110] Display 1. Gloss: claims are against what others do; violating one means doing what
  it forbids without the entitlement that conduct needs (violation in the fact-relative sense;
  permission, where granted, in the evidence-relative sense); conduct comes in two kinds, each
  needing its own entitlement. Three statuses result: liable (the claim yields to necessary and
  proportionate force); not liable (the claim is fully protected); estopped (the claim is kept, but
  he may not resist, others may not defend him, and he cannot demand repair).
- **3.2** [110] Definitions: an agent *acts upon* a person when she aims to harm, restrain or use
  that person, or knowingly harms her. She *merely imposes risk* when her conduct threatens someone
  without acting upon him. Placements: the resident and Warehouse's thrower act upon their victims;
  the careful driver and the hiker merely impose risk.
- **3.3** [120] Classification. The agent's aims and knowledge fix what kind of conduct she engages
  in, hence which entitlement it needs; they never fix whether it exists. So her standpoint does
  not return by the back door. Tort law draws the line in the same place: intent covers what one
  desires or believes "substantially certain to result" (Restatement (Second) of Torts §8A).
  Borderline, conceded: a vaccination programme certain to kill someone, no one in particular,
  knowingly harms no one; near certainty the line blurs.
- **3.4** [190] First argument, from authority. Evidence can give an agent permission to act, not
  power over another. For the twin to lose his claim against the knife, the resident's judgment that
  he is the killer would have to settle, as between them, what the twin may be subjected to. No
  private person's judgment binds another; each is independent of the other's choice (Kant's innate
  right, "independence from being constrained by another's choice": Kant 1797, 6:237, verify — #20;
  Ripstein 2009, verify — #21). So only what has authority over the twin's position can permit
  acting upon him: his own acts (he binds himself), the balance of claims (no one's say-so), and a
  legitimate authority (a judgment made with authority over him). Consent is the familiar case: no
  one can decide for her that she consented, and a reasonable belief in her consent is not her
  consent (Ferzan 2012, 690 n. 70).
- **3.5** [90] The conferrers, briefly. Forfeiture follows the forfeiter's own conduct, answering
  Renzo's demand for a principle explaining how "by acting in certain ways we alter our normative
  status" (Renzo 2017; verify — #12). The balance of claims overrides without removing: infringement,
  not violation (McMahan 2005a, 388; Quong 2012, 51 n. 11). Legitimate authority: §5.
- **3.6** [120] Second argument: the same point from the twin's side. If the resident's reasonable
  judgment bound him, his protection would reach only as far as his appearance, and would turn on
  his brother's crimes, the warning and the storm, none of them his doing. Burri already makes the
  protection itself objective (2025 §2); the constraint on those who act upon a person cannot be
  looser. Objection: the balance of claims binds him without his act, too. Reply: it outweighs his
  claim without removing it, so he keeps his claim to repair.
- **3.7** [150] The risk half. The careful driver passes no judgment on what the pedestrian may be
  subjected to. He judges only his own activity, as each of us must be free to do, and conducts it on terms of care
  that make everyone's freedom to act compatible with everyone's safety; those terms are shared, not
  his to set. So the pedestrian is subject to no one's judgment, and her claim is to his keeping the
  terms. The licence must also be usable by someone who cannot know how things will turn out: an
  activity imposes risk when it may be pursued only under duties of care (Burri 2020; verify — #8),
  and a single trip is justified by its own prospects under due care (Frowe 2022; verify — #4).
- **3.8** [160] The parity objection: both protections turn on facts not the victims' doing, so the
  driver should be liable too. Reply: the difference is not luck but subjection. If the resident's
  judgment bound the twin, the twin would be subject to another's judgment about him; the pedestrian
  is subject to no one's. This meets head-on the thought that morality can ask of an agent only
  reasonable conduct (Ferzan 2012, 682 n. 49): it asks only that of the resident, which is why he is
  permitted and blameless. Whether the twin must submit is a question about the twin's claim, and
  another's reasonableness is no reason for him to submit. The pedestrian had the driver's care, so
  nothing of hers was violated; whether she may defend herself is a further question (§4).
- **3.9** [110] Precedent and credit. Private law excuses no good-faith mistake in an intended
  interference (hunters who shot a dog they took for a wolf owed its value: *Ranson v. Kitner*, 31
  Ill. App. 241 (1889); verify), while accidental harm answers to negligence; Ripstein treats
  trespass and battery as wrongs of use (Ripstein 2016; verify — #19). The parallel shows that the
  line is familiar, not that private law's cheap remedies transfer: here the stakes are lethal on
  both sides (§6). New here: the application to defensive liability, the argument for it, and §5.
- **3.10** [110] The responsibility condition, derived. Objection: consent is chosen, forfeiture is
  not. Reply: both are ways one's own acts alter one's claims, and both need the same capacity,
  which young children and the deluded lack. So the falling man, who does nothing (Thomson 1991,
  287), and the drugged aggressor, who lacks the capacity (284), are not liable, with no separate
  stipulation.

## §4 The argument and the cases (910)

- **4.1** [150] Display 2. Defence locations: (1) §2, with its restriction to responsible agents from
  §3.10; (2) §3.4–3.6 and 3.8; (3) §3.7; (4) §3.2; (5) §5. The argument is valid; (2) carries the
  weight.
- **4.2** [140] Liable: *Mistaken Attacker*. The twin's resistance enforces an intact claim; an
  officer may help him (Quong 2012, 60–61); peacekeepers may stop the duped soldiers (61–62). So is
  the coerced attacker, who shoots a stranger to save her child from torture: duress excuses but
  confers no entitlement (Quong 2012, 50; Burri 2025 §4). The resident is blameless and acted
  permissibly by his evidence, yet owes the twin repair.
- **4.3** [150] Not liable: *Conscientious Driver*; the ambulance driver (Quong 2012, 57); the cell
  phone user who unknowingly detonates a bomb (McMahan 2005a, 397–98); the falling man. May the
  pedestrian kill the driver? An agent-relative prerogative (Quong 2009, 517), if there is one,
  weights her own claim in the balance of claims and so entitles her; on Tadros's priority of the
  status quo (2012, 272) nothing does, and her defence would make her liable. Either way the driver
  is not liable, and a neutral third party may not take sides.
- **4.4** [110] Justification defeats liability, explained. McMahan asserts it (2005a, 399); Quong
  finds the assertion ad hoc (2012, 70). Here it follows: the justified bomber's entitlement,
  conferred by the balance of claims, exists, so no claim is violated. Likewise when an agent does
  not know of an entitlement: ignorance no more cancels one than evidence confers one.
- **4.5** [140] Parting from Quong. Warehouse: the thrower knowingly harms the hostages on a
  lesser-evil entitlement that does not exist, so he is liable, as Clark holds; the Moral Status
  Account says not. The trolley pair: in both cases the agent knowingly harms the victim on an
  entitlement that does not exist, so she is liable in both; the Moral Status Account splits them.
- **4.6** [100] Agreeing with Quong against Clark: the hiker merely imposes risk and, on the case as
  told, takes due care, so he is not liable, like the driver. Clark's own note ties the two cases
  together ("As If", §III n. 24); I take the other branch.
- **4.7** [120] The rationale differs: what matters is not treating someone "as if" she lacks rights,
  but acting upon her on a judgment that has no authority over her. That explains why mistakes about
  justification and about numbers count like mistakes about rights: each is a private judgment
  about whether she may be subjected to the act. And the account needs no gloss on "as if", so
  Clark's worry that Quong's gloss is descriptively implausible ("As If", §III) does not reach it.

## §5 Appearances and authority (820)

- **5.1** [110] Clark's challenge: a prankster and a would-be murderer with a jammed gun bear the
  upshots of liability without posing any threat (Clark 2025, 431–32). Treating them as liable meets
  four problems: necessity, proportionality, perverse incentives, aggregation (435–41).
- **5.2** [160] Estoppel: one who leads another to rely on a state of affairs cannot deny it against
  her reliance. Leading her is his own act, so it can change his position (§3.4): it removes his
  standing to enforce the claim (to defend it, to have it defended, to demand repair), not the claim.
  That is Clark's "partial forfeiture" (2025, 442), now from a single ground. Not ad hoc: contract
  law binds a party by the objective manifestation of assent (Restatement (Second) of Contracts §19,
  via Ferzan 2012, 691).
- **5.3** [150] Why "knowingly or carelessly": behaving like an attacker risks misleading others, and
  risks are governed by due care (§3.7). The upshot is lost standing, not liability, because what
  the conduct risks is others' reasonable action against him. Frowe's actor, rehearsing where he has
  no reason to expect onlookers, is not estopped, and Frowe agrees he may defend himself (Frowe 2010
  §II, §VI); rehearsing in a crowded park, he is. Ferzan refuses liability for a negligent
  appearance, since such a person "seems … to have done too little to have forfeited his rights"
  (2012, 691 n. 72). Here he forfeits nothing.
- **5.4** [110] Contrasts. Not estopped: the twin (resemblance is not conduct, and knocking is not
  attacking); the victim of false testimony (Clark 2025, 432). Estopped: an actual attacker who meets
  reasonable but unnecessary force (455). Between private persons a claim changes only by its
  holder's own act, never by another's judgment: the resident's attack changes his position, the
  twin's appearance changes nothing, the bluffer's performance changes his standing.
- **5.5** [100] Why the four problems do not arise: the claim survives, so necessity and
  proportionality still bind; a bystander who knows the gun is empty should say so; and choosing
  under uncertainty counts infringed claims (Clark 2025, 453–54).
- **5.6** [110] Legitimate authority. Quong sets aside the prison guard who must treat an innocent
  but lawfully convicted prisoner "as if" he lacks rights (2012, 64 n. 30). The guard acts on an
  entitlement conferred by a procedure with authority over the prisoner, which may rightly act on
  evidence (§3.4), so he is not liable: the prisoner may not break out by force, and is owed redress
  through the procedure.
- **5.7** [80] Soldiers lack such authority over foreigners, since there is no common authority
  between states, so the duped soldiers remain liable even if their state is legitimate. The line
  follows who is subject to the authority, not how reasonable the officials are.

## §6 Objections (980)

- **6.1** [150] (A) Fair allocation, at its strongest: reasonable mistakes are permissible "as the
  byproduct of a justified social practice for fairly managing the risk of suffering unjust harm"
  (Jorgensen 2021, 143 n. 3, as quoted in Clark 2025, 451; verify — #3). Ripstein asks whether it is
  reasonable to make the apparent aggressor bear the risk of the defender's mistake (verify — #5).
  Then the twin's claim is only to reasonable treatment.
- **6.2** [180] Reply, first in the account's terms: a practice could confer an entitlement over the
  twin only as an authority that binds him to submit, and exempting reasonable defenders from blame
  and sanction binds no one to submit (the account keeps that exemption). Then the burdens. Call the
  objector's principle (A), and mine (F): the target keeps his claim, the defender keeps his
  permission and is blameless, but may not prevail over the innocent's resistance and owes repair.
  Under (A) the twin bears a lethal cost for an appearance he did nothing to create, and may not
  resist; under (F) the resident bears only the loss of any claim to prevail over an innocent he has
  attacked, which he could avoid by not acting upon another. The stakes are lethal either way; (F)
  places them on the one who took another's fate into his hands.
- **6.3** [80] Response: defenders must act on judgment in the dark; under (F) they act without
  assurance of being in the right.
- **6.4** [100] Reply: (F) leaves the permission to act, and the full right against real attackers,
  untouched. The assurance it withholds was never theirs: a guarantee that an innocent stranger
  must submit.
- **6.5** [150] (B) Control and fairness: liability should rest on an agential failure (Burri 2022,
  550–51; 2025 §5; Ferzan 2012, 682). Reply: the resident's liability is not a sanction but the
  other side of the twin's intact claim (§3.8), and he controlled the one thing that mattered,
  whether to take another's fate into his hands. Compare the person who takes an umbrella believing
  it his: he must still give it back (Ferzan 2012, 690).
- **6.6** [90] Concession: culpability bears on how much harm one is liable to, and on which of
  several liable agents should bear it (Tadros 2021, 3506, 3510).
- **6.7** [110] (C) The driver objection: he chose to set tons of steel in motion (McMahan 2005a,
  393–94); or no principled line separates him from the resident (Lang 2022; verify — #2).
- **6.8** [120] Reply: nothing in his conduct needed an entitlement over the pedestrian; he acts on
  a valid licence (§3.7), and the parity argument fails (§3.8). McMahan rates the case weak (403).
  Lang's arguments target Quong's version of the line (verify — #2).

## §7 Conclusion (230)

- **7.1** [140] Restate: liability follows the entitlement one's conduct needs. Three claims:
  entitlements over a person come only from her own acts, the balance of claims, or legitimate
  authority; a licence to impose risk comes from due care; behaving like an attacker, knowingly or
  carelessly, alters standing, not the claim. Beneath them: between private persons, no one's
  judgment binds another, and a claim changes only by its holder's own acts.
- **7.2** [90] Scope, and one open question: how culpability fixes the degree of liability, which the
  account leaves where Tadros put it.

## 1. The thesis, sharpest form

**The Conferral Account.** A responsible agent is liable to defensive harm when she acts upon
another person without an entitlement that his own acts, the balance of claims, or legitimate
public procedure has conferred, or when she imposes risk on him without due care, unless he has
knowingly induced her to act as she does. She is liable in no other way.

Compressed (for the abstract and introduction): *liability follows the facts when an agent acts
upon a person and follows the evidence when she merely imposes risk, because entitlements over a
person can be conferred only by that person's own acts, the balance of claims, or public
procedure, never by what the agent's evidence says.*

One-sentence test. I argue that liability borrows its standard from the entitlement the agent acts
on (fact-relative for acting upon a person, evidence-relative for imposing risk), against the view
that liability has a single standpoint (McMahan's responsibility, Burri's subjective disrespect) or
Quong's dependence test, on the grounds that only a person's own acts, the balance of claims, or
public procedure can confer an entitlement over her, while a licence to impose risk is by its
nature conferred by due care.

**Contribution type:** a new account, plus a diagnosis of why the field keeps splitting over the
fact–evidence choice.

**What I do not claim** (one sentence each, placed where each arises):
- The mistaken attacker is not blameworthy, and in the evidence-relative sense he acts permissibly.
- The pedestrian facing the careful driver may still have a permission to defend herself; she is
  not wronged by the driver, and the driver is not liable.
- The account settles *whether* one is liable, not *how much*: culpability may still bear on
  proportionality and on selecting among liable parties (Tadros 2021).
- Liability to punishment, and liability grounded in rescue duties, are out of scope.

## 2. The move

The analytic move has three steps.
1. Classify conduct by the kind of entitlement it needs. Acting upon a person (aiming at her harm,
   knowingly harming her, or singling her out to bear a risk) needs an entitlement over that
   person. Merely imposing risk on whoever happens to be affected needs only a licence to impose
   that risk.
2. Ask who can confer each kind. Only the person herself (by consenting or by her own wrong), the
   balance of claims, or legitimate public procedure can confer an entitlement over a person. A
   licence to impose risk is conferred by due care on the evidence, because a licence that lapsed
   whenever the risk materialised would be no licence.
3. Read liability off the result. An agent who acts beyond the entitlement she needed has no claim
   against the other's enforcement of his claim; an agent who stays within it keeps her claims. The
   fact–evidence split then *falls out* of the conferral facts rather than being stipulated.

**Why this is the sharpest version.** It states necessary and sufficient conditions from one
principle and one distinction. The weaker version ("sometimes liability is fact-relative") would be
true but uninformative. The stronger version (fixing degrees of liability too) cannot be defended
in 6,000 words and is not needed for the contribution.

## 6. The central argument (draft for Display 2)

1. A responsible agent is liable to defensive harm when she is about to violate another's claim,
   and he has standing to enforce it. (The enforcement premise, shared with Thomson 1991, Quong 2020
   and Burri 2022; the standing clause is defended in §5.)
2. When she acts upon him, she violates his claim unless an entitlement conferred by his own acts,
   by the balance of claims, or by legitimate public procedure permits what she does; her evidence
   confers no such entitlement. (§3; the conferral premise)
3. When she merely imposes risk on him, she violates his claim only if she fails to take due care.
   (§3; the licence premise)
4. Any conduct that threatens him either acts upon him or merely imposes risk on him. (§3, by the
   definition of acting upon)
5. So a responsible agent is liable to defensive harm exactly when she acts upon another without
   such an entitlement, or imposes risk on him without due care, and he has not lost his standing
   to enforce by knowingly inducing her conduct. (from 1–4)

Premise 2 carries the weight (the crux); §3 and §6A defend it. Premise 3 is defended in §3 and
against the driver objection in §6C. The standing clause in premise 1 does work only in the
appearance cases (§5), where it yields estoppel without loss of the claim.

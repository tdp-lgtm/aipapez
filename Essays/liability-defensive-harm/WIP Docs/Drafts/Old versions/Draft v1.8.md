## 1. Introduction

Consider a case Quong calls *Mistaken Attacker* (McMahan 2005a, 387; Quong 2012, 53). A resident of a remote house has been warned by the authorities that a mass murderer has escaped from prison and will almost certainly attack anyone he meets on sight. On a stormy night there is a knock at the door. The caller is the murderer's identical twin, whose car has broken down. He knows nothing of his brother's escape and wants to phone for help. The resident, justifiably taking him for the murderer, lunges at him with a knife. It seems clear that the twin may fight back, and that a police officer who knew the truth could stop the resident by force (Quong 2012, 60–61). If so, the resident is liable to defensive harm. He has no claim against the force needed to stop him, and he may not resist it. Yet he acted as any reasonable person would.

Compare *Conscientious Driver*, in which a man who keeps his car well maintained and drives carefully loses control of it in freak circumstances. The car veers toward a pedestrian, who can save herself only by blowing it up and killing him (McMahan 2005a, 393). Many judge that the driver is not liable, since he did nothing wrong by any standard he could have followed (Quong 2012, 56–57; Ferzan 2012, 682; Burri 2025, §4); McMahan disagrees. Finally, in *Bluff*, a prankster points an unloaded gun at his manager and shouts, "Time to die!" She reasonably believes she is about to be shot and breaks his leg. He may not fight back, others may not defend him, and she owes him nothing (Clark 2025, 431–32).

Together the three cases make a puzzle. In the first, liability follows the facts. The twin's innocence defeats the resident's reasonable belief. In the second, it follows the evidence. The driver's due care protects him, though the pedestrian is as innocent as the twin. In the third, a man who threatens no one bears the cost of how he appeared. An account that fixes liability by a single standpoint, the facts or the evidence, can capture at most two of these three verdicts.

I defend an account on which liability follows the entitlement that a person's conduct needs, and who can confer it. Call it the Conferral Account. An agent who acts upon a particular person, by attacking, restraining or using him, needs an entitlement. Only that person's own acts, the balance of competing claims, or a legitimate authority can confer it. The agent's evidence cannot confer it, however good, because no private person's judgment binds another. An agent who merely imposes risk, as the driver does, needs only the licence that due care confers. And a person who knowingly or carelessly behaves like an attacker keeps his claim but loses the standing to enforce it. He may not resist, others may not defend him, and he cannot demand repair. The nearest rivals are Quong's Moral Status Account, which shares my verdicts on the first two cases for a different reason, and Burri's account, which holds the resident liable only by finding an earlier fault in him.

I ask only whether a person is liable, not how much harm he is liable to. Liability is not blame, and I consider liability incurred by threatening others, not by failing to rescue them (Tadros 2012). Section 2 reviews the rival standpoints, Section 3 states and defends the account, Section 4 draws its verdicts, Section 5 treats appearances and authority, and Section 6 answers objections.

## 2. The Standpoint Problem

Begin with accounts that fix liability by the facts. For Thomson, a person who will otherwise violate your right lacks a right against your killing him to prevent it, and "agency is no more required for violating a right than fault is" (Thomson 1991, 301). So a man pushed off a cliff, who will crush you when he lands, may be deflected to his death (287). As Ferzan objects, a right important enough to kill for would then be lost "by mere misfortune" (Ferzan 2012, 675).

McMahan's responsibility account also looks to the facts: the criterion of liability is "moral responsibility, through action that lacks objective justification, for a threat of unjust harm" (McMahan 2005a, 394). The resident meets it, as he should. But so does the careful driver, who chose to drive. McMahan accepts that he is liable (393–94), though he concedes that the case is one "about which most of us have only weak or doubtful intuitions" (403). Handling *Bluff*, moreover, requires a second clause: responsibility "for appearing to pose a threat" (McMahan 2011, 555–56).

Other accounts fix liability by the agent's evidence. For Ferzan, culpable aggression is necessary for liability (Ferzan 2012, 670). A reasonable defender does "all that morality can fairly ask" of him and forfeits nothing (682 n. 49). Her view clears the careful driver, but also the resident, and so cannot explain why the police officer may stop him. Burri ties liability to disrespect for another's rights, that is, to acting against the duties those rights impose in light of what one can know (Burri 2025, §4). She holds the resident liable, but only by finding an earlier fault. Since these duties can stretch back over time, she argues, a resident warned of a killer on the loose should not have opened his door (§4). Where no such fault can be found, her view clears the agent. In her version of Quong's case of soldiers duped by their government into attacking just defenders, she accepts that the soldiers are not liable, though many find this counterintuitive (§6.1; see also Burri 2022, 552).

Quong's Moral Status Account mixes the two standpoints. Quong distinguishes what is permissible in the fact-relative sense, given all the facts, from what is permissible in the evidence-relative sense, given what the available evidence supports (Quong 2012, 48). On his account, a person who threatens innocent people is liable when the evidence-relative permissibility of her choice "depends either on the assumption that those who are harmed ... are liable to the harm, or else on false moral beliefs" (Quong 2012, 67, 72). Such agents treat others "as if" they lacked important rights, and so gamble with those rights (64–67). The resident's attack is permissible on his evidence only because he assumes the twin is liable, so by Quong's test he is liable. The driver assumes nothing of the kind, so by the same test he is not.

Clark (n.d., §III) presses two objections. The test is too narrow. In one of his cases an agent throws a grenade into a warehouse, knowing it will seriously injure two hostages, because reliable intelligence says this is the only way to stop a bomb that would kill thousands, but there is no bomb. In another, a hiker throws a grenade to scare off a bear, and it lands beside two neighbours he could not see. Clark finds both agents liable; the Moral Status Account finds neither liable. The test is also arbitrary. In a pair of Clark's trolley cases, an agent turns a runaway trolley onto a woman she reasonably but wrongly takes to be to blame for it. The cases are alike except that in one the agent's evidence also says the trolley would otherwise kill five people, which gives her a lesser-evil justification to fall back on; the test finds her liable only in the other. And like most accounts, Quong's treats merely apparent attackers as liable (Quong 2009, 519). This has serious costs (Section 5).

These accounts share a premise. Liability is a matter of enforcing claims, so that "threatening a rights violation is both necessary and sufficient for liability" (Burri 2022, 547). Burri finds this framing in Thomson and Quong as well (546–47; Quong 2020, verify — #1). Some reject it, treating liability as distributive justice, a matter of who should bear a harm that someone must bear (McMahan 2008, 234; Gordon-Solmon 2018, 546; both quoted in Clark 2025, 442; verify — #11). Burri replies that liability is "a distinctly localised affair" between the parties, whereas distributive justice depends on the wider social context (Burri 2022, 540, 545–46). I take the enforcement premise as common ground with my main rivals. It does not say what counts as a violation, and there the rivals divide, each fixing the standard by a standpoint. I fix it instead by what the conduct needs and who can confer it.

## 3. The Conferral Account

> **The Conferral Account.** A responsible agent is liable to defensive harm when, and only when, (a) she acts upon another person without an entitlement conferred by his own acts, by the balance of claims, or by a legitimate authority over him, or (b) she imposes risk on him without due care. The one exception: if the other person has knowingly or carelessly behaved like an attacker, and the agent acts in reasonable reliance on that appearance, no one may harm the agent in his defence.

A claim, on this view, is a claim against what another person does. Conduct violates the claim when it does what the claim forbids without the entitlement that conduct needs. "Violation" is meant in the fact-relative sense, given all the facts; "permission", where I grant it, is meant in the evidence-relative sense, given the agent's evidence. The account yields three statuses. A liable person's claim yields to necessary and proportionate defensive force. A person who is not liable has a fully protected claim. And a person who has behaved like an attacker keeps his claim but cannot enforce it (Section 5).

The account turns on one distinction. An agent *acts upon* a person when she aims to harm, restrain or use that person, or knowingly harms him. She *merely imposes risk* when her conduct threatens someone without acting upon him. The resident acts upon the twin, since he aims to stab him. The thrower in Clark's warehouse case acts upon the hostages, since he knows his grenade will injure them. The careful driver and the hiker merely impose risk, since neither aims at anyone or knows that anyone will be harmed. A police marksman who fires at a hostage-taker, knowing there is a small chance of hitting the hostage, acts upon the hostage-taker but merely imposes risk on the hostage.

The agent's aims and knowledge settle which kind of conduct she engages in, and so which entitlement it needs, but never whether the entitlement exists. Her standpoint classifies the conduct without deciding the liability. McMahan remarks in passing that the resident, unlike someone who threatens others unknowingly, "at least knows that he poses a threat, and thus is responsible for ensuring that it is not an unjust threat" (McMahan 2005a, 402). The Conferral Account turns that remark into a principle, and the argument from authority explains why it holds. Tort law draws the line in the same place. It treats a consequence as intended when the actor desires it or believes it "substantially certain to result" from his act (Restatement (Second) of Torts, §8A). I concede that there are borderline cases. A vaccination programme certain to kill someone, though no one in particular, knowingly harms no particular person. But as the known probability of harm to a particular person approaches certainty, the line blurs, and the account's verdicts blur with it. I accept this. The account's clear verdicts are for the clear cases.

Why should acting upon a person need an entitlement that the agent's evidence cannot supply? The first argument is from authority. Evidence can give an agent permission to act. The resident's evidence makes it permissible for him, in the evidence-relative sense, to defend himself. But permission to act is not power over another person. If the twin lost his claim against the knife, it would be because the resident's judgment that he is the murderer settled what the twin may be subjected to. But no private person's judgment has that authority over another. Each person is independent of every other's choice; Kant made this the one innate right, "independence from being constrained by another's choice" (Kant 1797, 6:237; verify — #20). So the resident's evidence, however strong, does not strip the twin of his claim. Only three things can settle the twin's position: his own acts, by which he binds himself; the balance of claims, which is no one's say-so; and a legitimate authority, whose judgment does have authority over him. Consent is the familiar case. No one can decide for another that she consented, and a reasonable belief that she consented is not her consent (Ferzan 2012, 690 n. 70).

The three sources work differently. Forfeiture follows the forfeiter's own conduct. Renzo asks accounts of forfeiture for a principle explaining how "by acting in certain ways we alter our normative status" (Renzo 2017; verify — #12). The answer here is that, among private persons, only the holder of a claim has authority over it. The balance of claims overrides a claim without removing it. The claim is infringed rather than violated, and it survives as a claim to repair (McMahan 2005a, 388; Quong 2012, 51 n. 11). Legitimate authority is the subject of Section 5.

The second argument, from appearance, makes the same point from the twin's side. If the resident's reasonable judgment bound the twin, the twin's protection would reach only as far as his appearance to others. Whether he kept that protection would then depend on his brother's crimes, the warning and the storm, none of them his doing. A claim that protected the twin only as he appeared would protect his appearance rather than him. Burri herself holds that the protection a right gives its holder is objective, fixed by the facts rather than by anyone's evidence (Burri 2025, §2). When conduct is aimed at the right-holder, what others may do to her cannot be looser than that protection. So the twin's protection cannot depend on how he appears to the resident. One might reply that the balance of claims also binds a person without any act of hers. But it binds her differently. It outweighs her claim without removing it, so that she may be permissibly harmed and yet is owed repair. What the second argument rules out is a private judgment that would remove the claim itself.

Merely imposing risk is different. The careful driver passes no judgment on what the pedestrian may be subjected to. He judges only his own activity, whether and how to drive, as each of us must be free to do. He conducts it on terms of care that make everyone's freedom to act compatible with everyone's safety. Those terms are shared; they are not his to set. So the pedestrian is subject to no one's judgment, and her claim against the driver is that he keep those terms. A licence to impose risk must also be usable by someone who cannot know how things will turn out. If it lapsed whenever the risk materialised, it would be no licence at all.

The obvious objection, which I will call the parity objection, is that the pedestrian's protection also turns on facts that are not her doing, namely the freak failure of the car. If the second argument works for the twin, it seems to work for her, and then the driver is liable too. The reply is that the cases differ in subjection, not in luck. If the resident's judgment bound the twin, the twin would be subject to another person's judgment about him. The pedestrian is subject to no one's. This also meets the thought that morality can ask of an agent only that he act reasonably (Ferzan 2012, 682 n. 49). It asks only that of the resident, which is why he is permitted and blameless. But whether the twin must submit is a question about the twin's claim, and another person's reasonableness gives him no reason to submit. The pedestrian had the driver's due care, which is all her claim against him asked, so nothing of hers was violated. Whether she may defend herself is a further question (Section 4).

The distinction is not new. Private law does not excuse a good-faith mistake about whose property one is intentionally using or destroying. Hunters who shot a dog they took for a wolf owed its owner its value (*Ranson v. Kitner*, 31 Ill. App. 241 (1889); verify), whereas accidental harm answers to the standard of negligence. Ripstein treats trespass and battery as wrongs of using what is another's (Ripstein 2016; verify — #19). The law does excuse reasonable mistakes about the need for self-defence (e.g. Restatement (Second) of Torts, §63), which is the fair-allocation view answered in Section 6. The parallel shows only that the line is familiar, not that private law's cheap remedies carry over when the stakes are lethal on both sides. What is new is the line's application to defensive liability, the argument for it, and its extension to appearances (Section 5).

The account also explains why liability requires a responsible agent. One might object that consent and forfeiture cannot share a source, since consent is chosen and forfeiture is not. But both are ways in which a person's own acts alter her claims, and both require the same capacity, which young children and the deluded lack. So the man pushed off the cliff, who does nothing at all (Thomson 1991, 287), is not liable, and neither is the driver drugged by a villain into trying to run you down (284). Neither verdict needs a separate stipulation.

## 4. The Argument and the Cases

> **The argument.** (1) A responsible agent is liable to defensive harm exactly when she is about to violate another person's claim and he has standing to enforce it. (2) Acting upon him violates his claim exactly when no entitlement conferred by his own acts, by the balance of claims, or by a legitimate authority over him permits it; the agent's evidence confers none. (3) Merely imposing risk on him violates his claim exactly when it lacks due care. (4) Any conduct that threatens him either acts upon him or merely imposes risk on him. (5) He lacks standing against her exactly when he has knowingly or carelessly behaved like an attacker and she acts in reasonable reliance on that appearance. (6) So she is liable exactly when she acts upon him without such an entitlement, or imposes risk on him without due care, unless the condition in (5) holds.

Premise (1) is the enforcement premise of Section 2, restricted to responsible agents for the reason given at the end of Section 3. Section 3 defended premises (2) to (4), premise (2) by the arguments from authority and from appearance, and Section 5 defends premise (5). The argument is valid, and premise (2) does most of the work.

In *Mistaken Attacker*, then, the resident acts upon the twin with no entitlement from any of the three sources, so he is liable. The twin's resistance enforces an intact claim, so a police officer who knows the truth may help him (Quong 2012, 60–61). Peacekeepers may likewise stop soldiers whom their government has duped into shelling a village (61–62). A mother who tries to shoot a stranger because terrorists will otherwise torture her child is liable too. Duress excuses her, but it confers no entitlement (Burri 2025, §4). The resident is blameless and acted permissibly on his evidence, yet he owes the twin repair.

The careful driver is not liable. Neither is the man whose phone a villain has secretly rigged to detonate a bomb when he presses "send" (McMahan 2005a, 397), nor the man pushed off the cliff. May the pedestrian nonetheless kill the careful driver to save herself? That is a separate question, on which the account is neutral. If there is an agent-relative prerogative to give one's own life extra weight (Quong 2009, 517), it lets her give her own life extra weight when the claims are balanced, so the balance entitles her. If there is only a priority of the status quo (Tadros 2012, 272), nothing entitles her, so her defence would make her liable. On either answer the driver's own position is unchanged: he is not liable, and a neutral third party has no ground to take a side against him.

The Conferral Account also explains why justification defeats liability. McMahan holds that an agent who acts with objective justification is not liable (McMahan 2005a, 399), but Quong finds this ad hoc on McMahan's view (Quong 2012, 70). On the Conferral Account, it follows directly. A bomber in a just war who destroys a military target, knowing that civilians nearby will die, acts upon them (McMahan 2005a, 388). But the balance of claims entitles him, so he violates no claim. The same holds when an agent does not know of an entitlement he has. His ignorance does not cancel the entitlement, just as his evidence could not have conferred one.

In two places the Conferral Account departs from Quong's. In the warehouse case, the agent knowingly harms the hostages on a lesser-evil entitlement that does not exist, so he is liable. Clark reaches the same verdict. The Moral Status Account says he is not liable, because he makes no mistake about anyone's rights. Nor is the account arbitrary in the way Clark's paired trolley cases show Quong's test to be. Whether an entitlement exists does not depend on what else the agent's evidence would have supported, so the agent is liable in both.

In one case the Conferral Account agrees with Quong against Clark. The hiker merely imposes risk, and on the case as described he takes due care, since he has good reason to think no one is near. So he is not liable, just like the careful driver. Clark himself notes that the two cases stand or fall together (n.d., §III n. 24). He takes the branch on which both are liable. I take the other.

The rationale differs as well. What makes the resident liable is not that he treats the twin "as if" the twin lacked rights, but that he acts upon the twin on a judgment that has no authority over him. This explains why mistakes about justification and about numbers matter as much as mistakes about rights: each is a private judgment about whether a person may be subjected to an act.

## 5. Appearances and Authority

*Bluff* raises what Clark calls the challenge of merely apparent attackers (Clark 2025, 430–33). The prankster, like a would-be murderer whose gun has jammed, bears the usual upshots of liability without posing any threat. The orthodox explanation, that such people are liable, meets four problems (435–41). First, harming them is never actually necessary. Second, it is unclear what harm to them is proportionate. Third, the view can give a bystander who knows the truth a reason to keep it to himself. Fourth, it yields paradoxes when a defender must choose among many apparent attackers under uncertainty.

The Conferral Account explains Clark's verdicts without making the prankster liable, through the idea of estoppel: one who has led another to rely on a state of affairs may not deny it against her reliance. Leading someone to rely on an appearance is one's own act, so by the argument from authority it can change one's position. What it changes is not the claim but the standing to enforce it: the standing to resist, to be defended by others, and to demand repair. That is the bundle Clark calls "partial forfeiture" (Clark 2025, 442). Clark derives it from either of two background theories of liability, one based on distributive fairness and one based on duty (Clark 2025, 442–50); here it follows from a single ground. Nor is the idea ad hoc. Contract law binds a party by the objective manifestation of assent, whatever he privately intended (Restatement (Second) of Contracts, §19, as discussed in Ferzan 2012, 691).

Estoppel covers careless as well as knowing conduct, because behaving like an attacker risks misleading others, and risks are governed by due care. What such conduct costs a person is standing, not liability, because what it risks is others' reasonable action against him. There is no threat to avert. Consider Frowe's actor, who rehearses a gangster play with a replica gun and has no reason to expect onlookers. He is not estopped, and Frowe agrees that he may defend himself against a passer-by who takes the rehearsal for a real attack (Frowe 2010, §II, §VI). Had he rehearsed in a crowded park, he would be estopped. Ferzan declines to make a negligently created appearance a ground of liability, since such a person "seems ... to have done too little to have forfeited his rights" (Ferzan 2012, 691 n. 72). On the Conferral Account, he forfeits nothing. His carelessness costs him only his standing.

The twin, by contrast, did nothing that behaved like an attack. His resemblance to his brother was not something he did, and knocking at a door is not an attack. So he is not estopped, and neither is a person whom a liar has falsely accused of being about to kill (Clark 2025, 432). An actual attacker who meets reasonable but unnecessary force is estopped (455). He cannot complain of force his own attack made it reasonable to use. The common principle is that, between private persons, a claim changes only by its holder's own act, never by another's judgment.

Estoppel avoids all four problems. The first does not arise, because the account never says the prankster is liable; it says only that he cannot complain of force his own performance made reasonable. The second is answered because his claim survives. Since the prankster keeps his claim, force against him needs a warrant, and the only warrant is the manager's reasonable reliance on the appearance he created. So she may use only the force that is necessary and proportionate as things reasonably appear; anything beyond that relies on nothing he did, and violates his surviving claim. The third is answered in the same way: a bystander who knows that the gun is empty should say so, since silence only makes an infringement of the prankster's surviving claim more likely. The fourth arises because orthodoxy lets each apparent attacker's liability turn on the defender's credence about him, so that she may treat as liable a whole crowd she knows to contain bluffers. On the Conferral Account the harmless keep their claims, so each infringed claim still counts against her choice (Clark 2025, 453–54).

Legitimate authority, the third source of entitlement, is the only one that may rightly act on evidence. Quong sets aside the case of a prison guard who must treat an innocent but lawfully convicted prisoner "as if" he lacked important rights (Quong 2012, 64 n. 30). On the Conferral Account, the guard acts on an entitlement conferred by a procedure with authority over the prisoner, and such a procedure may act on evidence. So the guard is not liable. The prisoner may not break out by force. His remedy lies within the procedure itself: appeal, pardon and compensation.

Soldiers have no such authority over foreigners, since states stand under no common authority. The soldiers duped into shelling a village therefore remain liable even if their own state is legitimate and its officials were reasonable.

## 6. Objections

The strongest objection, the fair-allocation view, holds that reasonable mistakes are part of a fair scheme for sharing the risk of unjust harm. Unlike the distributive view set aside in Section 2, it accepts that liability is a matter of enforcing claims; it disputes what the twin's claim is. On this view, reasonable defensive mistakes are permissible "as the byproduct of a justified social practice for fairly managing the risk of suffering unjust harm" (Jorgensen 2021, 143 n. 3, as quoted in Clark 2025, 451; verify — #3). Ripstein asks, in the same spirit, whether it is reasonable to make the apparent aggressor bear the risk that the defender is mistaken (Ripstein 1996; verify — #5). The thought is appealing: everyone benefits from a practice that lets people defend themselves on reasonable evidence, since anyone may need to, and the rare innocent who is mistakenly attacked bears a risk that the practice spreads across everyone, as driving spreads its risks. If so, the twin's claim was only ever a claim to reasonable treatment, which the resident gave him.

I have two replies. The first is in the Conferral Account's own terms. A social practice could confer an entitlement over the twin only if it were an authority that bound him to submit to reasonable defenders. A practice that exempts reasonable defenders from blame and punishment is not that kind of authority, and the Conferral Account already grants them that exemption. The second reply compares burdens. On my principle, the twin keeps his claim in full. The resident keeps his permission to act and his innocence, but he may not prevail over the twin's resistance, and he owes him repair. The objector's principle makes the twin bear a lethal cost, with no right to resist, for an appearance he did nothing to create. Mine makes the resident bear a lesser cost: the loss of any claim to prevail over an innocent person he has attacked. The stakes are lethal on either view, but only one of the two made a choice that put them in play. My principle places the cost on the person who chose to act upon another; the objector's places it on the person who did nothing.

The objector may press further: defenders must act on their judgment in the dark, often in seconds, and my principle seems to deny people who have done their best any assurance that acting well will keep them in the right.

But my principle leaves them everything that acting well can earn: their permission to act, their blamelessness, and their full right against real attackers, so that in the ordinary case, where the attacker is real, they are in the right. What the principle withholds is a guarantee that, once they have done their best, an innocent stranger must submit to them. No one's best efforts could earn that guarantee, since it would be a power over the stranger, and his claim is not theirs to dispose of.

A second objection appeals to control and fairness. Liability, it says, should rest on some failure by the agent, something he could have avoided by acting better (Burri 2022, 550–51; Burri 2025, §5; Ferzan 2012, 682). The resident failed in nothing. Ferzan adds that he could not even avoid gambling: if he had not defended himself, he would have gambled with his own life instead (Ferzan 2012, 682 n. 49). I accept both points, and they explain why the resident is permitted and blameless. But his liability is not a sanction for failure. It is the other side of the twin's intact claim, and nothing about the resident's choice, however unavoidable, gave him a say over that claim. Ferzan's own example shows the same structure. A person who takes an umbrella, believing it to be his, may be blameless, but he must still give it back (Ferzan 2012, 690).

I concede that culpability matters for other questions. It plausibly bears on how much harm a liable person may be made to bear. It also bears on which of several liable people should bear a harm when not all can be spared (Tadros 2021, 3506, 3510). The Conferral Account settles only whether a person is liable at all, and it is compatible with any reasonable answer to these further questions.

A third objection comes from the other direction. The first two held that the account makes someone liable who should not be; this one holds that the careful driver is liable after all. McMahan's reason is that the driver "voluntarily chose to set a couple of tons of steel rolling as a means of pursuing his ends", knowing that this carried a tiny risk to others. Since the cost of that choice cannot be divided, "it is fair that he should suffer" it rather than the pedestrian (McMahan 2005a, 394). Lang argues more generally that no principled line separates the careful driver from the resident, so that any account must treat them alike (Lang 2022; verify — #2).

The reply is that nothing in the driver's conduct needed an entitlement over the pedestrian. McMahan's argument assumes that the driver "will therefore *violate* the pedestrian's right not to be killed" (McMahan 2005a, 403). On the Conferral Account he does not. Her claim against his driving was a claim to his due care, and he gave it. Choosing to impose a risk that due care licenses is exactly what the licence covers. The parity objection fails for the reason given in Section 3.

McMahan has a second argument. The first premise is that the pedestrian may kill the driver in self-defence. The second is that one may not kill a person who is not liable in order to save oneself. So the driver must be liable (as reconstructed by Quong 2012, 57). The Conferral Account answers it either way. If there is an agent-relative prerogative, the second premise is false. If there is not, the first premise is false instead: the pedestrian may not kill the driver after all (Section 4). As for Lang, if the argument of Section 3 is sound, there is a principled line between the driver and the resident: the line between conduct that must be entitled and conduct that must only be careful (verify — #2).

## 7. Conclusion

I have argued that liability to defensive harm follows the entitlement a person's conduct needs. Entitlements to act upon a person come only from her own acts, the balance of claims, or a legitimate authority over her. A licence to impose risk comes from due care. And behaving like an attacker, knowingly or carelessly, alters a person's standing but not his claim. One principle underlies all three claims: between private persons, no one's judgment binds another, and a claim changes only by its holder's own acts.

The account leaves some questions open. It says only whether a person is liable, not how much harm he may be made to bear, and it leaves the role of culpability in fixing that degree where Tadros put it. Within its scope, though, it gives the three cases with which I began their familiar verdicts from a single ground. It also explains two things its rivals have had to stipulate: that justification defeats liability, and that liability requires a responsible agent.

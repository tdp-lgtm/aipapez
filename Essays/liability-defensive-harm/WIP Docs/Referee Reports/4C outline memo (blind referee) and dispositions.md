## The referee's findings (condensed faithfully; full text in the session transcript)

- **Summary.** Stated the thesis crisply, which the referee counted in the essay's favour on clarity.
  One misreading: it took the estopped bluffer to be "owed repair". The outline says he loses the
  standing to demand it. That misreading is itself evidence for major concern 2.
- **Major 1 [HIGH].** The fact-relative/evidence-relative asymmetry is asserted more than derived
  (3.4, 3.6, 3.8). The consent argument shows only that consent is fact-relative, which even
  evidence-relative theorists grant. The independence argument is a moral-luck complaint that seems
  to apply equally to the pedestrian. "Not directed at her" names the disanalogy without explaining
  it. Fix: a freestanding argument for why directedness flips the standard, meeting the Ferzan/Burri
  intuition ("morality can only ask reasonable conduct") head-on.
- **Major 2 [MED].** Display 1 hides the account's three statuses (liable; not liable; claim kept but
  enforcement lost). Fix: name the third status where the account is first stated.
- **Major 3 [MED].** Word budget is tight exactly where the crux needs room. Fix: trim a secondary
  case (the coerced attacker) or shorten the authority discussion in §5.
- **Major 4 [LOW-MED].** The reply to the fair-allocation objection uses contractualist vocabulary
  ("reasonably rejected") the account does not otherwise use. Fix: translate it into the account's
  own terms, or flag it as a minimal comparison of burdens.
- **Strongest missing objection.** Private law tolerates strict liability for intended interference
  because its remedies are cheap and reversible (pay for the dog, return the umbrella). Defensive
  harm involves maiming and death, so the precedent underwrites far less than the essay takes from
  it.
- **Minor.** (a) Could not find "As If" as a title and took it for a short title of Clark 2025. (b)
  "Restatement §19" should read "Restatement (Second) of Contracts §19", next to the Torts §8A cite.
  (c) "Concession (iv)" points to a list the outline never shows. (d) Verified as real and plausibly
  accurate: Frowe 2022, Burri 2020, 2022 (the "localised affair" wording), 2025, Renzo 2017,
  Gordon-Solmon 2018, Tadros 2021, and Clark 2025's page range for every pin-cite.
- **Overall.** About 20% finalist odds as planned, 45–50% after the revisions. "A shade below" a
  good specialist journal; the underdefended asymmetry would trigger an R&R. Top revisions: rebuild
  3.6–3.8; answer the stakes objection; make the three statuses explicit and give the crux room.

| M1 | Asymmetry asserted, not derived | **Accepted, and the central change of v0.4.** New first argument (3.4), from authority: evidence can give an agent a permission to act but not a power over another person. For the twin to lose his claim, the resident's judgment about him would have to bind him, and no private person's judgment binds another (Kant's innate right as "independence from being constrained by another's choice", 6:237; the thought is credited, not borrowed as an argument from authority). What can bind him is his own act, the balance of claims (no one's say-so) and legitimate authority (a judgment made with authority over him). That explains the list of conferrers, which v0.3 only stated. Risk (3.7): the careful driver makes no judgment about the pedestrian. He judges only his own activity, on terms of care that are shared, not his to set. Parity (3.8): the difference is not luck but subjection. This meets the Ferzan/Burri intuition head-on: morality asks of the resident only reasonable conduct, which is why he is permitted and blameless; whether the twin must submit is a question about the twin's claim, and another person's reasonableness is no reason for him to submit. The independence argument (3.6) is kept as the same point seen from the twin's side, shortened. |

## Sketch A — The Conferral Account (rank 1, recommended)

**Thesis (P/Q/R).** A person is liable to defensive harm when, as a responsible agent, she acts on
an entitlement to harm another that only the other (by consent or by her own wrong) or the actual
balance of claims could confer, and no such entitlement exists; or when she imposes risk on others
without due care. This is against the view that liability has one standpoint, whether fact-relative
(McMahan), evidence-relative (Burri), or Quong's dependence test. The grounds: entitlements to harm
another cannot be conferred by the harmer's own evidence, whereas a licence to impose risk is by its
nature conferred on evidence.

**Premises.** (1) To harm a person permissibly, one needs an entitlement: her consent, her
liability, or an overriding balance of claims. (2) Consent and liability are conferred only by the
person's own will or own wrong; the balance of claims is a fact about the claims. None is conferred
by what the harmer's evidence says. (3) So a reasonable but mistaken harmer acts without
entitlement: the victim's claim stands and she may enforce it, which is what liability to her
defensive force consists in. (4) A licence to impose risk, by contrast, must be satisfiable by an
agent who does not know how things will turn out, so it is conferred by due care on the evidence.
(5) So a careful risk-imposer whose risk misfires has exceeded no entitlement and is not liable.

**Payoff.** One principle yields the contested verdicts. The Resident and the Duped Soldiers are
liable, which saves Police Intervention. The Conscientious Driver, the Ambulance Driver and the Cell
Phone Operator are not. Nonresponsible threats are not liable, because only responsible agents act
on entitlements. The justified bomber is not liable, because his entitlement exists. It also
answers Clark's three objections to Quong: Warehouse comes out liable, the two Waiting on Trolley
cases get the same verdict, and Scare the Bear is treated like the driver. Bluffers and futile
attackers are not liable; they are *estopped* by the claim they manifest, which recovers Clark's
"partial forfeiture" without a second ground of liability. Legitimate public procedures can confer
evidence-based entitlements, which explains Quong's unexplained institutional exception.

**Main objection.** Why isn't the defender's entitlement just a licence to take reasonable
defensive risks, conferred on evidence the way the driver's is? This is the route taken by
Bolinger and by Ripstein's fair-allocation view.

**Crux.** Show that claims against being *acted upon* protect a person as a particular person.
Their content cannot be made relative to others' evidence without making her standing depend on how
she appears. Claims about *risk*, by contrast, protect only a fair share of general safety, and
their content is due care. Candidate solution: an independence argument. Also answer Lang's claim
that the driver and the mistaken attacker cannot be distinguished (text to be requested).

**Venue fit.** A named new account with a clear rival (Quong's Moral Status Account) and a
diagnosis of why the field keeps splitting over the fact–evidence choice. It fits 6,000 words: one
principle, one crux, three case clusters.

## Sketch B — The Knowledge-Norm Account (rank 2)

**Thesis.** One is liable to defensive harm when one harms or endangers another on the basis of a
proposition one does not *know*, where knowing it is required for the act to be proper. This is
against evidence-relative and culpability views. The grounds: knowledge norms of action mark acts
that are excusable but improper, and liability tracks impropriety, not blame.

**Premises.** (1) Acts that bear on others' rights are governed by a knowledge norm: treat *p* as
a reason for harming only if you know *p*. (2) The Resident treats "he is the murderer" as a reason
without knowing it; his act is excusable but improper. (3) Improper harming, unlike merely unlucky
harming, leaves the harmer without a claim against the victim's resistance. (4) The careful driver
acts on something he knows, that the risk is small, so he is not liable.

**Payoff.** A principled, epistemology-based line between the Resident and the driver. It speaks to
an audience with strong interests in knowledge and action.

**Main objection.** Probabilistic knowledge. If the Resident may act on the known proposition "he
is very probably the murderer," the Resident is not liable. If he may not, the driver's reliance on
"my car will not fail today" makes the driver liable.

**Crux.** A knowledge norm that tells harms apart from risks without borrowing Sketch A's
distinction. Also, why impropriety grounds *forfeiture* rather than, say, a duty to compensate.

## Sketch C — The Risk-Pool Account (rank 3)

**Thesis.** A faultless threatener is liable only when the risk she imposed lies outside a
reciprocal risk pool her victim shares; deliberate harms lie outside every pool. This is against
both McMahan and Quong on the Conscientious Driver. The grounds: reciprocity of risk (Fletcher's
paradigm in tort law) fixes who must bear a risk that materialises.

**Payoff.** A new criterion for the most disputed accident cases. Drivers threatening drivers are
not liable; a grenade-thrower threatening hikers is. It also explains the dog owner (Tadros).

**Main objection.** Drivers impose far more risk on pedestrians than pedestrians impose on them, so
the account may make the Conscientious Driver liable after all. Frowe (2022) argues that a practice
cannot justify a single risky act, and the account leans on practice-level reciprocity.

**Crux.** Specify "pool" so that it is neither practice-relative (Frowe's objection) nor so broad
that everything is reciprocal. It also needs a separate treatment of deliberate harming, so it
covers only half the ground.

## Sketch D — The Present-Duty Account (rank 4)

**Thesis.** Liability is the enforceability of a *present* duty the target can discharge but is
failing to discharge: to desist, to divert, or to absorb. This is against backward-looking
forfeiture accounts. The grounds: enforcement answers present non-compliance, which also fixes when
liability begins (Ferzan's Fortune Teller and Total Recall problems).

**Payoff.** A clean answer to the timing and act-requirement problems, and a natural account of
scalar liability.

**Main objection.** It cannot separate the Resident from the Cell Phone Operator. Both have a
present fact-relative duty to desist that they can discharge. It also sits close to Tadros's and
Clark's duty-based views.

**Crux.** A non-borrowed way to exempt unknowing agents. Without one, it collapses into Sketch A
or into Tadros.

---

## Ranking rationale

A is ranked first. It alone gives one principle for the whole case set, it answers the most recent
objections to the nearest rival (Clark on Quong; Burri on control), and its crux has a candidate
solution. B is original, but it risks collapsing into A or into the evidence-relative view once
probabilistic knowledge is admitted. C and D each solve part of the problem well but need A's
distinction to finish the job.

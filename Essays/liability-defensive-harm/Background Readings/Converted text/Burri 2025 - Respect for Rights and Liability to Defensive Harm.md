# Respect for Rights and Liability to Defensive Harm

Susanne Burri<sup>1</sup>

Accepted: 3 March 2025 / Published online: 9 April 2025  
© The Author(s) 2025, corrected publication 2025

## Abstract

This paper defends a novel framework for thinking about our rights against being harmed, paying special attention to the conditions under which they are enforceable through a liability mechanism. It contends that a central purpose of our rights is to specify what steps others are required to take, in their role as agents, to avoid harming us. To be geared towards this relational purpose, rights need to be equipped with a subjective component that lays out how an agent ought to deliberate and conduct herself in light of the epistemic constraints she faces. Based on these ideas, the paper develops a hybrid account of rights according to which the *ex ante* and *ex post* duties implied by our rights are entirely subjective. If an agent abides by the duties imposed on her by another person's rights, she *respects* these rights. If she violates the duties, she *disrespects* the rights. The account developed is hybrid because it stipulates that a person's rights protect her against objectively specified harm, even if the duties imposed on others are merely subjective. I argue that this is less paradoxical than it may seem. The paper then defends an account of liability to defensive harm that grounds an agent's liability in her disrespect for others' rights, and which fixes the extent of her liability by reference to what defenders reasonably perceive to be necessary and proportionate defensive harm in light of the agent's disrespectful conduct.

**Keywords** Rights against being harmed · Enforcement · Self-defence · Liability to defensive harm · Evidence-relative permissibility · Second-person standpoint

## 1 Introduction

As human beings, we have rights against being harmed—or so I assume throughout this paper. But what may a rights holder (or those coming to his aid) permissibly do to *defend* his rights<sup>1</sup> against those who would seem to threaten to violate them? And what does it mean, exactly, to threaten a *rights violation*? In this paper, I set out to answer these questions in a novel and unifying manner.

That we have rights against being harmed implies that others, in their role as agents, owe it to us not to treat us in certain ways. Our rights against being harmed thus impose on others duties to refrain from acting in these ways. Most theorists working on rights understand the duties implied by our rights in a “fact-relative”<sup>2</sup> manner, implying that it need not be epistemically accessible to a duty bearer what some duty requires of her in a specific situation.<sup>3</sup> Consider the following case, based on an example by McMahan (2009b, p. 165):

**Ambulance Driver.** Ambulance Driver is transporting a very ill person. Based on Ambulance Driver’s medical knowledge and her other available information, she has good reason to believe—and she actually does believe—that there is a high chance the patient’s life can be saved, but only if she drives aggressively. As she is a skilled and experienced driver, she imposes no undue risks on other participants in traffic even if she drives aggressively. Ambulance Driver thus decides to drive aggressively. Alas, due to an unlucky set of circumstances, she causes an accident. In the accident, Pedestrian is lethally injured.

According to McMahan, Ambulance Driver violates the pedestrian’s right to life by killing him, as she has an *objective* duty towards him *not* to drive aggressively. Ambulance Driver does not, after all, have an objective justification<sup>4</sup> for killing the pedestrian, and the pedestrian’s rights thus normatively protect him against being killed. For McMahan, it follows that Pedestrian’s rights impose on Driver a correlative duty not to kill Pedestrian. That Driver acted as well as she could have under the circumstances fails to imply—both for McMahan as well as for other rights theorists—that Driver complied with all of her moral duties.

I disagree. In this paper, I develop an account of our rights against being harmed in which the moral duties implied by our rights are entirely subjective, or fully tailored to the epistemic situation we happen to find ourselves in when deliberating and acting. I defend my account by arguing that rights are an essentially relational normative concept that give shape to a morality which puts a “second-person standpoint” (Darwall 1996; see also Wallace 2019) at its core and center. We take up such a standpoint “when we make and acknowledge [morally relevant] claims on one another’s *conduct* and *will*” (Darwall 1996, p. 3, emphases added), thus holding each other accountable. Importantly for my purposes, the quality of our will manifests itself in how we deliberate, which is determined not by the causal consequences of our actions, but by the way in which we take, or fail to take, certain considerations into account when deliberating about the right thing to do in light of our beliefs and our evidence. Similarly, how we conduct ourselves—conscientiously or carelessly, thoughtfully or recklessly—is fixed not by how things turn out as a result of our actions, but by the epistemic situation we find ourselves in when making relevant choices. If rights play a central role in how we “make and acknowledge claims on each other’s conduct and will”, the duties they imply are thus plausibly subjective. Accordingly, one of my core proposals in this paper is that we threaten to *violate*, or impermissibly infringe, another’s rights *only if* we fail to abide by the relevant subjective requirements the right imposes on us, thus *disrespecting the right*.

Objective considerations continue to play a role, however, in the ultimately hybrid account of rights that I defend. More precisely, I argue that, in our role as patients, we do, in fact, have rights against being harmed that are specified in fact-relative ways; our rights are not merely rights that others take reasonable steps to avoid harming us. The crux is simply that reasonable caution is all these rights demand of others from an *ex ante* point of view. From an *ex post* point of view, the situation looks different. Once the dust of a possibly quite heated situation has settled, our rights can give rise to claims of compensation, and possibly other claims as well, even if everyone acted reasonably from an *ex ante* perspective.<sup>5</sup> Take again the case of the Ambulance Driver. On my proposed conceptualisation of our rights against being harmed, Ambulance Driver permissibly infringes Pedestrian's rights when she kills him by acting in a subjectively justified, but objectively unjustified manner. Because she commits a faultless mistake that affects others adversely, she acquires certain duties to make good this mistake. She has a duty to apologise to Pedestrian's loved ones if given the opportunity to do so, to give just one example. Pedestrian's family might also have a claim to financial compensation against the ambulance driver's insurance, or against her employer's insurance.<sup>6</sup>

Based on the hybrid account of rights that it develops, this paper is focused on defending an account of permissible *rights enforcement* that is both coherent and compelling. If our rights are enforceable, then there are special permissions to act in ways that serve to prevent their violation. In this paper, I proceed from the assumption that such special permissions are generally grounded in the liability to defensive harm<sup>7</sup> of the person who is threatening another's rights. Liability to defensive harm is a temporary reduction in an agent's moral status that enables others to safeguard a right that the agent seems to be about to violate. By assuming that liability to defensive harm is part and parcel of the enforcement dimension of our rights, I (see Burri 2022) side with thinkers such as Thomson (1991) and Quong (2020), and part ways with those who tie liability to considerations of distributive justice (Montague 1981; McMahan 2003; Otsuka 2016; Gordon-Solmon 2018).

According to the *Disrespect for Rights Account of Liability to Defensive Harm* (DRA) that I propose, an agent's liability to defensive harm is grounded in her disrespect for someone else's rights. The agent disrespects another's rights if she acts against the subjective duties it imposes on her. The amount of harm she renders herself liable to is fixed by what defenders reasonably perceive to be necessary and proportionate in light of the agent's disrespectful behaviour. The DRA is thus a doubly subjective account of liability: it grounds an agent's liability in her violation of the *subjective* requirements that others' rights impose on her, and it delimits the scope of her liability by making reference to the *reasonable beliefs* of defenders. The basic thought behind the DRA is that an agent renders herself liable to the reasonable defensive treatment of defenders if, and only if, she disrespects the rights of others, and this gives defenders reason to believe that she is threatening a rights violation. In this way, the DRA attempts to stay true to the enforcement framing of liability, while also taking seriously the idea that our epistemic constraints put limits on the claims that we can make on each other.

The DRA shares much in common with culpability accounts of liability (see e.g. Montague 1981; Ferzan 2005 and Ferzan 2012). Most importantly, it is equally committed to the assumption that if an agent abides by all of her subjective moral requirements, then she thereby immunises herself against the involuntary rights forfeiture that she would suffer if she became liable to defensive harm. Unlike culpability accounts, the DRA refuses to accept that an agent can immunise herself against liability to defensive harm not only by acting subjectively permissibly, but also by being equipped with a non-epistemic excuse, such as an excuse of duress. I argue that this is an advantage of the DRA, as non-epistemic excuses have no impact on whether an agent threatens to violate another's rights, and thus have no bearing on liability to defensive harm if we accept an enforcement framing of liability (as we have independent reason to do).

Compared to existing accounts of liability other than culpability accounts, I argue that the DRA is preferable because it grounds an agent's liability in a distinctly agential failure, namely the failure to respect others' rights. Relevant other accounts do not do this, thus implying that an agent can sometimes render herself liable to defensive harm even if she acted impeccably under the circumstances. In light of the fact that a liable person has her moral status downgraded *based on how she acted*, this is unsatisfactory.

The DRA is preferable to existing accounts also because it offers a non-disjunctive ground for liability to defensive harm. According to the DRA, it is always an agent's disrespect for others' rights that *grounds* her liability. Existing accounts tend to be disjunctive, arguing that liability can arise either from certain ways of threatening objectively unjustified harm, or from certain ways of merely appearing to threaten harm.

The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. In Section 2, I introduce and motivate the hybrid framework for thinking about rights that the subsequent sections build upon. In section 3, I explain and briefly defend an enforcement framing of liability. In section 4, I introduce the Disrespect for Rights Account of Liability to Defensive Harm (DRA). In section 5, I argue that the DRA is superior to existing accounts. In section 6, I defend the DRA against two objections. Section 7 concludes.

## 2 A Hybrid Account of Rights

According to the hybrid understanding of rights that I lay out and motivate in this section, our rights normatively protect us, in our role as patients, against the imposition of certain objectively specified harm,<sup>8</sup> thus rendering the imposition of such harm unjust.<sup>9</sup> In our role as agents, however, others' rights do not impose on us objective duties not to cause such harm. Instead, they impose on us subjective *ex ante* and *ex post* duties. The *ex ante* duties are geared towards the prevention of unjust harm, and they demand of agents not to maliciously, recklessly, or negligently threaten such harm. The *ex post* duties kick in whenever potentially unjust harm has occurred, and they are geared towards making good at least part of the damage if the harm that has occurred is found to have been unjust.

Consider first the *ex ante* perspective, which constitutes the main focus of this paper. According to my proposed account, we are, in our role as agents, under an obligation to be mindful of the legitimate safety interests of others, and this general obligation implies duties to avoid threatening them with unjust harm in a malicious, reckless, or negligent manner. If an agent abides by all of the subjective requirements imposed on her by another's rights, I say that she *respects* these rights. Where her respectful behaviour nevertheless threatens unjust harm, she is about to commit a permissible rights infringement, but not a rights violation. In cases where an agent's disrespectful behaviour causes no harm, the agent commits neither a rights violation nor a rights infringement, but her disrespectful behaviour is nevertheless wrong—precisely because it is insufficiently mindful of the legitimate safety interests of others.

<sup>8</sup> Our rights do not protect us against the imposition of *all* harm. Some harm we may have no rights against, e.g. the harm that others impose on us without the exercise of any agency, i.e. merely through their bodily movements (though see Thomson 1990 and 1991; see also fn. 11). We can also voluntarily *waive* or involuntarily *forfeit* some of our rights against being harmed, in which case at least some of the protections implied by our rights are at least temporarily lifted.

<sup>9</sup> Unjust harm is harm whose infliction (permissibly or impermissibly) infringes someone's rights. According to most rights theorists, any and all rights infringements wrong the rights holder and entitle him to compensation (e.g. from the agent, or from beneficiaries of her actions).

Most normative ethicists working on the topic of rights in general, and on permissible self-defence in particular, understand the *ex ante* duties implied by our rights in a fact-relative manner. According to such a fact-relative understanding, our rights against being harmed are Hohfeldian claim-rights not to have certain types of harm imposed on us. This means that our rights correlate *directly* with others' duties to refrain from imposing the relevant harm on us, except in cases where there is a lesser evil justification<sup>10</sup> for doing so. With a lesser evil justification, agents may permissibly proceed to impose the relevant harm on us, but they thereby nevertheless infringe our rights, at least in cases where we have done nothing to waive or forfeit the relevant rights. Following Robert Nozick (1974, p. 57) as well as Thomson (1990), one might picture a person's rights as circumscribing a protective sphere around the rights holder that others trespass whenever they harm the rights holder in a way that he has a right against. If the harm inflicted lacks an objective justification, the trespass is a rights violation, i.e. an *impermissible* rights infringement. If the harm inflicted is objectively justified, the trespass is a *permissible* rights infringement. All trespasses are unjust, meaning that they wrong the rights holder and entitle him to compensation, even if there was an objective justification for infringing the right.<sup>11</sup>

If an agent imposes relevant objectively unjustified harm but acts subjectively permissibly, those who assume a fact-relative understanding of the duties implied by our rights argue that the agent has an epistemic excuse for committing what nevertheless remains a rights violation. With respect to the Ambulance Driver case, they would thus claim that by killing Pedestrian, Ambulance Driver blamelessly violates Pedestrian's rights.

<sup>10</sup> We have a lesser evil justification in favour of inflicting some harm if doing so is necessary to prevent a bad outcome that is morally much weightier than the harm we need to impose to prevent it.

<sup>11</sup> There are many different ways in which the duties implied by our rights can be specified in a fact-relative manner, and there is no agreement among rights theorists which way is most appropriate. McMahan (2005, 2009b) and Otsuka (1994) argue that we violate others' rights whenever we engage in a *foreseeably risk-imposing activity* that causes objectively unjustified harm. As aggressively driving an ambulance is a foreseeably risk-imposing activity—even if done carefully, it comes with well-known dangers—Ambulance Driver violates Pedestrian's rights if she kills him. For McMahan and Otsuka, we can violate others' rights only if we are suitably linked, through our morally responsible agency, to some objectively unjustified harm that we cause. To McMahan and Otsuka, engaging in a foreseeably risk-imposing activity forges this necessary link. Thomson (1990, 1991) denies that such a link is required. She claims that we violate others' rights whenever we cause objectively unjustified harm, even if we exercised no voluntary agency in bringing about the harm, or even if the occurrence of the harm was entirely unpredictable. Quong (2020), by contrast, argues that something even stronger than the link argued for by McMahan and Otsuka is necessary to turn the causation of objectively unjustified harm into a rights violation. For Quong (2020), we violate others' rights if, and only if, we mistakenly treat them as if their moral status was diminished, thus harming them without objective justification. In this paper, I presuppose, for reasons of expositional ease, that we have rights against being harmed at least through others' foreseeably risk-imposing agency. The key claims I advance in this paper are not dependent on this assumption, however. What is important for my purposes is that all of the views just sketched share in common the assumption that an agent can violate others' rights even if she deliberated and acted as well as she could have under the circumstances.

At first sight, this fact-relative understanding of the duties implied by our rights seems inescapable. It is both intuitive and simple. But we nevertheless have reason to reject it. Rights are, after all, an essentially *relational* normative concept. To have a right centrally means to have claims against specific others that they (not) treat you in certain ways (Feinberg 1970). Put differently, the duties that are imposed on us by another's rights are *directed* duties that we owe directly to the rights holder. With this very distinct feature, rights give shape to a morality that puts what Stephen Darwall has coined a "second-person standpoint" (Darwall 1996) at its core and center. We take up such a standpoint "when we make and acknowledge [morally relevant] claims on one another's *conduct* and *will*" (Darwall 1996, p. 3, emphases added), thus holding each other accountable.<sup>12</sup>

If we take these ideas seriously, they suggest that rights are centrally intended to regulate how we relate to each other while going about our lives. From an *ex ante* standpoint that we take up before the causal consequences of our actions unfold, they aim to guide our decision-making and our conduct by prescribing how we ought to take into consideration the legitimate safety interests of others—largely by taking suitable steps to avoid harming them, and by signalling to them that we are committed to doing this (cf. Bolinger 2021). But, crucially, both how we *deliberate* and how we *conduct* ourselves is determined by the beliefs we hold and by the evidence that is available to us; neither is determined by the facts as they actually obtain independently of whether we have epistemic access to them.

A key feature of the account of rights that I propose is that respecting others' rights immunises an agent against committing a rights violation. I believe that conceptualising rights violations in this way—we violate others' rights *only if* we violate the requirements that the rights impose on us in light of the information accessible to us at the time of acting—properly pays heed to the idea that our rights centrally aim to regulate how we ought to relate to each other in terms of our willing and our conduct.

This is not to say, of course, that Ambulance Driver's killing of Pedestrian is normatively insignificant according to the account of rights that I propose. Most importantly, the account specifies the rights themselves in a fact-relative manner, and Pedestrian thus has a right against being killed that Ambulance Driver *permissibly infringes* by killing him. Pedestrian has not, after all, waived or forfeited his right to life, and the right thus stays firmly in its place.

According to the hybrid understanding of rights proposed here, there are two different ways in which a right may permissibly be infringed. First, an agent permissibly infringes her victim's rights if she abides by all of the subjective requirements the rights impose on her, but nevertheless harms her victim in an objectively unjustified manner. Second, an agent permissibly infringes her victim's rights if she acts with objective justification, thus threatening her victim with unjust but objectively justified harm. The fact-relative understanding of the duties implied by our rights has conceptual room only for this second type of permissible rights infringement. To put the same point differently, the hybrid account of rights proposed here groups what we might call "reasonable mistakes" together with rights infringements that come with lesser evil justifications, whereas a fact-relative understanding of the duties implied by our rights groups such reasonable mistakes with the culpable actions of blameworthy rights violators.

Admittedly, there is an air of paradox—or at least something suspicious—to the stipulation that, as right holders, we are normatively protected against objectively specified harm, even though our rights impose on others merely subjective duties. Why not admit, one might think, that this degrades the rights to merely subjective rights as well? Against this, I note the following. First, our *ex ante* duties are geared towards the prevention of objectively specified unjust harm. Objectively specified unjust harm is thus what we are required, as agents, to *aim* to avoid. In this way, the objective specification of our rights informs and guides the morally relevant subjective rules that regulate how others, as agents, ought to deliberate and conduct themselves in light of their beliefs and their evidence. More importantly, the objective specification guarantees that our rights were *permissibly infringed* if relevant harm is imposed on us by a conscientious agent who abides by all of her subjective requirements. In cases where our rights were infringed, whether permissibly or impermissibly so, this generally gives rise to *ex post* duties, at least in cases where the relevant facts can be established once the dust of a possibly quite heated situation has settled.

On my proposed conceptualisation of the *ex post* perspective, we all have a general—and possibly undirected—duty to aim to establish the moral facts once some significant harm has occurred. The agent who caused the harm might, in addition, have special duties owed to her victim to participate in this search. These duties to try and sort out what has happened are again grounded in the fact that our rights against being harmed are specified in objective ways. If the evidence suggests that someone’s rights were infringed, this generally gives rise to duties of acknowledgement and compensation. Such *ex post* duties might be owed to the victim for example by the agent who caused the harm, by those who pressured her into imposing it, or by those who (willingly) benefited from its imposition. Where a right was permissibly infringed as opposed to violated, the duties to make amends will generally be less extensive. Where agents act with subjective justification or a lesser evil justification, they may thus become liable to certain restorative duties despite the fact that they acted morally permissibly, though their duties will, as a matter of course, be more limited than the duties of rights violators.<sup>13</sup>

To further clarify the commitments of the hybrid account proposed here, let me explicitly consider, in a final step, the problem of merely apparent harm. Consider first

**Poisoned Cake, Disrespectful Actions With No Actual Threat of Harm.** Baker bakes a cake for Birthday Boy, whom she wishes dead for no good reason. Baker adds white powder to the cake batter. Baker reasonably believes that the white powder is a tasteless and deadly poison, and that Birthday Boy will die if he takes just one bite of cake. Baker bakes the cake, hands it to unsuspecting Birthday Boy, and encourages him to try it. Contrary to Baker’s reasonable beliefs, the white powder has no effect on humans, and her cake offering to Birthday Boy is thus completely innocuous.

In *Poisoned Cake, Disrespectful Actions With No Actual Threat of Harm*, Baker’s disrespectful actions threaten no rights infringement and, *a fortiori*, no rights violation. While Baker shows disrespect for Birthday Boy’s rights in light of her reasonable beliefs, she does not thereby threaten any harm. According to the understanding of rights proposed here, an agent's disrespect for others' rights does not threaten a rights violation unless it does, in fact, threaten objectively unjustified harm. Subjective and objective considerations thus need to operate in tandem for a rights violation to occur.<sup>14</sup>

It still holds true, however, that my proposed account of rights is inconsistent with what an anonymous reviewer for this journal refers to as the *continuity thesis*. According to this thesis, the *ex post* duties a victim’s right imposes on an agent are grounded in the agent’s violation of the *ex ante* duties the same right imposes on her. I suggest that in place of the continuity thesis, we accept a view according to which the occurrence of unjust harm can give rise to restorative duties for anyone who has a morally meaningful connection to the unjust harm (e.g. by being a willing beneficiary of the harm, or by having brought the harm about about in a morally permissible or impermissible manner). Most rights theorists in normative ethics would, I take it, be inclined to agree that a broader grounding along these lines is called for. It is generally assumed, after all, that an agent can acquire restorative duties in cases where she permissibly infringes her victim’s rights based on a lesser evil justification.

In the *Poisoned Cake* case just presented, the agent believes that she is threatening real, and not merely apparent harm. In such cases, the sense in which the agent disrespects her victim's rights is rather obvious. The agent does, after all, have reason to assume that she is being insufficiently respectful of her victim's safety. Things are trickier if the agent knows that she is threatening merely apparent harm.<sup>15</sup> Consider:

**Practical Joke.** Shooter aims her realistic toy gun at Victim and screams "I will kill you!" Shooter knows her gun is a toy gun. She also knows, however, that Victim does *not* know this.

Here we might think that Shooter does not disrespect Victim's rights. Shooter knows, after all, that she is not threatening any harm. But this would be too quick. Our rights against being harmed protect our legitimate safety interests. Centrally, this includes our interest not to have physical harm inflicted on us. But it also includes our interest in *feeling* safe, and we thus have rights against others that they not threaten us even if their threats are fake. By pointing a realistic toy gun at Victim, Shooter knowingly makes Victim feel threatened, and she thereby violates a subjective duty, implied by Victim's rights against being harmed, not to wantonly make Victim feel insecure. Differently from Baker in the *Poisoned Cake* case just mentioned, her disrespectful behaviour towards Victim moreover violates her victim's rights, as it does make Victim feel insecure. The harm inflicted is not a physical harm, but a mental harm that our rights against being harmed protect us against.

## 3 Rights Enforcement and Liability to Defensive Harm

If an individual is liable to defensive harm, she has forfeited some of her usual rights against being harmed, and no defender—i.e. neither the agent's potential victim nor any third party coming to the victim's aid—wrong the liable individual by imposing on her harm to which she is liable. If others inflict on a person harm to which she is liable, she is moreover not entitled to fight back against the infliction of the harm, and is not owed any compensation for whatever setback in her interests she suffers as a result of having the relevant harm inflicted on her.

Becoming liable to defensive harm is normatively highly significant. The liable individual is stripped of some of her rights, thus having her moral status at least temporarily reduced. Her status is, moreover, reduced to enable the more effective protection of the rights of others—to be *defensively* liable means to lack rights against being harmed so that defenders are permitted to try to avert unjust harm to others. In light of the fact that rights-based normative theories are generally committed to the thought that all persons have equal moral rights, this departure from equality implied by liability needs a careful defence.

<sup>14</sup> What if, unbeknownst to an agent, there is a lesser evil justification in favour of inflicting some harm, but the agent inflicts the harm with a malicious intent? Does such an agent violate or merely permissibly infringe her victim's rights? This depends on the vexed question whether intentions are relevant to permissibility. Specifically with respect to lesser evil justifications, a fair number of scholars think that they are (see e.g. McMahan 2009a; Tadros 2020, esp. pp. 116-7; Bazargan 2022, esp. pp. 60-3). If they are, then harm that is inflicted with a bad intention might for that very reason be objectively unjustified, and the agent might thus be committing a rights violation. If they are not, the agent "gets lucky" and merely permissibly infringes her victim's rights despite acting disrespectfully. In this paper, I wish to leave it open whether intentions can make a difference to the extent to which harm is objectively justified.

Theorists working on liability to defensive harm accept without exception that an agent's liability is grounded in features of her *actions*; to have rendered oneself liable is to have behaved in such a way that the rights forfeiture implied by liability becomes an appropriate consequence of what one has done (or is currently doing). Whether an agent has a reasonable complaint against the liability that we saddle her with thus depends on how compelling a rationale we can offer in favour of the claim that she is appropriately stripped of some of her rights in light of what she has done (or is currently doing).

Over the past twenty years, most philosophers working on the topic of permissible self-defence have come to accept a view according to which agents can render themselves liable to defensive harm when and because this is necessary to make it morally permissible to distribute unavoidable harm as justly as possible.<sup>16</sup> Consider a standard defensive situation where Murderer is about to execute Victim unless Victim shoots Murderer first. By threatening Victim, Murderer has voluntarily created a situation where it is inevitable that either Murderer or Victim will die. If Murderer retained all of her rights despite threatening Victim, Victim would not be permitted to defend himself. It would, however, seem more just if Murderer as opposed to Victim were to die in their tragic confrontation. After all, Murderer bears moral responsibility for the fact that, under the circumstances, serious harm has become unavoidable, whereas Victim bears no such responsibility (or so we may assume). If Murderer's actions make her liable to defensive harm because they create a "forced choice" (Montague 1981, p. 215) between lives, this ensures that Victim may be shielded from harm by redirecting it towards Murderer, which is distributively just.

In earlier work, I have argued that we should reject such a distributive justice framing of liability (see Burri 2022).<sup>17</sup> One central reason for rejecting it<sup>18</sup> is that whereas liability to defensive harm is a distinctly *localised* affair—the wider context has no bearing on whether an agent renders herself liable to defensive harm—matters of distributive justice are not similarly local. To see this, consider again Murderer and Victim, and assume, as Montague (1981) does, that a comparative desert view of distributive justice is correct. On the face of it, it seems more just if Murderer as opposed to Victim should suffer harm. She, unlike Victim, is culpable with respect to the threatening situation, after all. It is quite possible, however, that Victim is a selfish and privileged individual who rarely in his life has had to pay for his petty cruelties and sweeping carelessness, whereas Murderer's criminal activities can be causally linked to her traumatically abusive childhood. In such a case, it is unclear whether Murderer is, in fact, more deserving of harm than Victim. Crucially, however, their past histories and the qualities of their characters are simply irrelevant to whether Victim may defend himself against Murderer's attack (Burri 2022; Benbaji and Statman 2020). For a dissenting opinion, see McMahan (2004, p. 722).

For this and other reasons, I suggest that we reject tying liability to considerations of distributive justice, and accept an *enforcement framing of liability* instead. Its key tenets are simple (Burri 2022, p. 546): First, we all have rights against being harmed. These rights impose on others duties (not) to treat us certain ways. Second, it is constitutive of our rights against being harmed that they have an enforcement dimension. If we reasonably assume that someone is about to violate them, there are certain things that we—or third parties acting on our behalf—are permitted to do (that we would not otherwise be permitted to do) to prevent the would-be rights violator from going through with her actions. Third, liability to defensive force is part and parcel of this enforcement dimension. More precisely, a person who violates the duties imposed on her by the rights of others *thereby* forfeits some of her own rights, thus strengthening the normative standing of the person whose rights she is acting against. It is in this way that the permissions we have to enforce our rights go beyond the usual permissions we enjoy in other circumstances.

<sup>16</sup> McMahan (2003, p. 402, fn. 122) credits Phillip Montague (1981) with this "distributive justice" framing of liability. McMahan (2003, 2005) is probably most famous for defending the framing, but it is widely endorsed. For an especially careful exploration, see Gordon-Solmon (2018).

<sup>17</sup> Quong (2020, see esp. pp. 5-8) agrees, and sketches an enforcement framing that looks similar to the one put forward by Burri (2022). Unlike a framing of liability in terms of distributive justice, such an enforcement framing suggests that whether an agent renders herself liable to defensive harm is settled by highly localised considerations, as is intuitive. In addition, it implies that stripping one person of rights is appropriate when and because the person did not respect the rights of others, and these others now have reason to believe that this threatens them with unjust harm. On an enforcement view of liability, what grounds liability to defensive harm are thus considerations *internal* to the logic of rights, not considerations coming from without (such as, for example, from the realm of distributive justice). This seems attractive not least because it is unclear why considerations coming from without the logic of rights would have sufficient normative force to override this logic.

## 4 The Disrespect for Rights Account of Liability

The Disrespect for Rights Account of Liability to Defensive Harm says the following:

**Liability to Defensive Harm According to the Disrespect for Rights Account (DRA).** An agent renders herself liable to what defenders reasonably consider necessary<sup>19</sup> and proportionate harm to stave off the rights violation the agent’s disrespectful behaviour reasonably appears to them to threaten if, and only if, the agent disrespects another’s rights by acting against the subjective requirements the rights impose on her.

The key features of the account are as follows.

**(1) An agent is not liable to defensive harm unless she acts against the subjective requirements another’s rights impose on her, thus disrespecting the rights.** If an agent always acts permissibly in light of her epistemic situation at the time of acting, her actions cannot render her liable to defensive harm. Consider again the case of *Ambulance Driver*. The case is constructed in such a way that there is no flaw in Ambulance Driver’s deliberation or in her conduct that we could point to to argue that she does not sufficiently take into account the legitimate safety interests of others (including those of Pedestrian). Driver is thus *not* liable to defensive harm according to the DRA.

<sup>19</sup> With this formulation and throughout this paper, I assume that (reasonably perceived) necessity is internal to liability. Internalist accounts of liability assume that an agent is liable only to necessary harm, and is thus wronged if unnecessary harm is inflicted on her. Externalist accounts assume that an agent can be liable to non-necessary harm as long as the harm is proportionate. For a defence of externalism, see e.g. Firth and Quong (2012); for a compelling reply—though not a defence of a strict internalism—see Frowe (2014, esp. pp. 88–99).

{26}------------------------------------------------

Importantly, the subjective duties imposed on us by others' rights might stretch out over time.<sup>20</sup> To illustrate, suppose that you now see what looks like a crazy person coming at you with a knife. You have only a split second to decide whether you want to defend yourself. You pull out your gun, and you successfully fire a shot at your attacker's shoulder. It might seem that, as a defender, you acted impeccably and did not disrespect your attacker's rights. But now suppose that ten minutes previously, police officers told you that an aggressive mentally ill person was on the loose in your area, and they offered to drive you home. You declined, as you live just across the street. The police officers urged you to go home and lock your doors. You ignored their advice. In this case, the correct thing to say is that you *disrespected* the mentally ill person's rights against being harmed. By risking a confrontation when you could easily have retreated, you failed to take her safety interests sufficiently seriously.

**(2) There are different ways in which we can disrespect others' rights, and acting in one such way is sufficient for liability.** Consider the following two variations of a *Shooter* case:

**Shooter With Immoral Intentions.** Shooter tries to shoot Victim because she wishes him dead for no good reason. She believes that her gun is loaded with lethal projectiles. The evidence available to Shooter strongly suggests, however, that the gun with which she tries to kill Victim is a harmless toy gun. Shooter points her gun at Victim, about to pull the trigger.

**Careless Shooter With Harmless Intentions.** Shooter tries to amuse her guests at a raucous party by "shooting" Victim. Shooter believes that the gun in her possession is a harmless toy gun. The evidence suggests, however, that her gun is both fully functional and most likely loaded. Shooter points her gun at Victim, about to pull the trigger.

On the DRA, Shooter renders herself liable to defensive harm in both scenarios. What Shooter does in the first case reflects worse on her character, and she is more blameworthy. In both scenarios, however, Shooter does not do enough to keep Victim safe. Recall also the *Practical Joke* case introduced at the end of Section 2. Shooter in *Practical Joke* also renders herself liable to defensive harm. While this shooter arguably does enough to keep her victim safe, she fails in her duties not to make her victim feel threatened.

**(3) Duress and other non-epistemic excuses do not preclude liability.** Consider

**Duress.** Mother attempts to shoot Stranger because a terrorist organisation has captured her child and is credibly threatening to torture the child unless Mother shoots Stranger. Mother is holding a gun that she believes to be loaded and is aiming it at Stranger, about to pull the trigger.

According to the DRA, Mother is liable to defensive harm even if she is fully excused, as her actions violate the subjective requirements that Stranger's rights impose on her. The fact that Mother reasonably believes her child's welfare to be at stake does not render her behaviour towards Stranger respectful; it merely renders her disrespectful behaviour towards Stranger understandable.

**(4) It is irrelevant for liability to defensive harm whether there is a *genuine* threat of objectively unjustified harm.** Consider the two variations of *Shooter* introduced in (2) above. In both cases, it is left open whether there is an actual threat of objectively unjustified harm. With respect to physical harm, the specifications of the cases allow, for example, that Shooter's gun might not actually be loaded, that her gun might be a toy gun, or that Victim has for some reason—unbeknownst to Shooter—waived his right not to be shot. With respect to the mental harm of feeling threatened, the cases do not mention that Victim reasonably feels threatened by Shooter's actions. Shooter might, for example, be pointing her gun at Victim from an angle where she remains invisible to Victim. From the point of view of the DRA, all of these considerations are *irrelevant* to Shooter's liability, however.

{27}------------------------------------------------

The DRA claims that disrespecting another's right is sufficient to render an agent liable to harm that defenders (i) *reasonably consider necessary and proportionate to staving off a rights violation that* (ii) *the agent's disrespectful behaviour reasonably appears to them to threaten*. The DRA is thus a thoroughly subjective account of liability: it grounds an agent's liability in her violation of the *subjective* requirements that the rights of others impose on her, and it delimits the scope of her liability by making reference to the *reasonable beliefs* of defenders. The basic thought behind the DRA is that an agent renders herself liable to the reasonable defensive treatment of defenders if, and only if, she disrespects the rights of others, and this gives defenders reason to believe that she is threatening a rights violation. In this way, the DRA attempts to stay true to the enforcement framing of liability, while simultaneously acknowledging that the business of enforcing rights is located firmly within the *ex ante* realm of our rights—a realm where those wishing to act might face significant epistemic constraints.

Admittedly, this raises a number of tricky issues. For one thing, consider

**Shooter With Immoral Intentions and Disrespectful Victim.** Shooter tries to shoot Victim because she wishes him dead for no good reason. She believes that her gun is loaded with lethal projectiles. Shooter points her gun at Victim, about to pull the trigger. As it happens, and unbeknownst to Shooter, killing Victim is the only way of preventing him from pushing a button that will kill five innocent and unsuspecting bystanders.

In this case, Victim is disrespectfully threatening the rights of the five bystanders. According to the DRA, this makes Victim liable to defensive harm that *defenders reasonably perceive to be necessary and proportionate to averting the threat to Bystander*. Shooter, however, does not aim at averting any threat whatsoever, and we may assume that it is not epistemically accessible to her that Victim is about to execute Bystander. In this case, the DRA yields that Shooter is liable to be defensively harmed by Victim, and that Victim is not liable to the harm that Shooter is about to inflict on him, even though the harm that Shooter is about to inflict on Victim is objectively necessary and proportionate to averting a rights violation that Victim is threatening.

I grant that this is a counterintuitive result at first sight. There is, after all, a sense in which Shooter gets lucky, and Victim—though not Shooter—is aware of this. Victim knows (or should know) that he is about to commit a rights violation, and it is reasonable for him to assume that the harm that Shooter threatens him with would serve to avert the rights violation. Why, then, should he be permitted to fight back against its infliction? The best answer, it seems to me, runs as follows. Matters of self- and other-defence are located firmly within the *ex ante* realm of rights. In this realm, we owe each other respectful treatment. This is determined by our beliefs and the evidence that we have access to, not by the facts as they actually are. If we act disrespectfully, this does not make us “fair game” to be targeted for malicious reasons. Instead, we become liable only to *respectful* defensive harm inflicted on us by those who are aiming to avert the rights violation our actions reasonably appear to them to threaten. In the case at hand, Victim thus owes it to the innocent bystanders not to push the button, but he does not owe it to disrespectful Shooter to suffer through Shooter's attack without fighting back. On careful reflection, this result, though intricate, strikes me as intuitively correct.

{28}------------------------------------------------

A second tricky issue is the following. If, as the DRA claims, the extent of an agent's liability is fixed by the subjective perspective of would-be defenders, there is a sense in which it is out of the agent's hands how much harm she becomes liable to when acting disrespectfully. Even if her liability is grounded in her agential failure to respect other's rights, the *extent* of her liability might well be disproportionate to that failure. This might appear worrisome, as I have praised it as an important merit of the DRA that it ties an agent's liability closely to her failure to respect others' rights.<sup>21</sup>

Things are not as bad as they may seem, however. For one thing, while the DRA ties the extent of an agent's liability to what things look like from the subjective perspective of would-be defenders, it also restricts the relevantly admissible evidence to that provided by the agent's behaviour. By building this restriction into the DRA, I follow a proposal by Jorgensen (Bolinger 2021). Jorgensen (Bolinger 2021) argues—compellingly, in my view—that defenders act subjectively permissibly when taking defensive measures only if they respond to what she dubs *aggression-signalling behaviour*. If defenders act based on evidence that is not tied to such behaviour, they act disrespectfully. If you feel threatened by someone walking home late at night simply because of their physical appearance, for example, this fact alone cannot make it morally permissible for you to use your pepper spray. Something analogous holds for would-be defenders who rely entirely on the testimony of third parties. Consider

**Mistaken Attacker.**<sup>22</sup> The identical twin brother of a notorious serial killer is driving through a stormy night in a remote area when his car breaks down. Reasonably unaware of the fact that his brother has recently escaped from prison and is known to be hiding in this same area, he knocks on the door of the nearest house, seeking to phone for help. On opening the door, Resident justifiably believes that Harmless Twin is the killer. Resident has been warned by the authorities that the killer will attack anyone he meets on sight, and so Resident lunges at Harmless Twin with a knife.

According to the DRA, Resident acts disrespectfully when lunging at Harmless Twin, even if she reasonably fears that her life is at risk. If authorities warn you that there is a killer on the loose who will attack anyone he meets on sight, the DRA implies that you best not open your door to any visitors unless you know that they are innocuous.

It does not follow, of course, that there will never be a mismatch between an agent's agential failures and the liability she thereby acquires according to the DRA. To see this, consider

**Eggshell Skull Eddie.** Puncher tries to punch Eggshell Skull Eddie to blow off some steam after a frustrating day at work. Unbeknownst to Puncher—and, we may suppose, in the absence of any evidence to this effect—Eggshell Skull Eddie suffers from a rare health condition that makes any blow to his head life-threatening. Had Puncher known that she was putting Eddie's life at risk, she would have refrained from picking a fight. As things stand, Eddie can save his own life only by killing Puncher.

The DRA rules that Puncher is liable to be killed by Eddie.<sup>23</sup> I admit that this is a harsh ruling. It does not seem to me to undermine the DRA, however. More precisely, it is simply invalid to infer that if the ground of liability is an agent's disrespect for rights, then the extent of her liability should match the badness of her agential failure. To assume that such an inference is valid is to assimilate liability with desert, or with some other justice-based notion. In Section 4, I have argued that we should reject such an understanding in favour of an enforcement view of liability.

According to an enforcement view of liability, an agent's disrespect for another's rights triggers the enforceability dimension of these rights: those wishing to defend the rights are permitted to inflict on the agent whatever harm they reasonably deem necessary and proportionate to keeping the victim as safe as he would have been had the agent respected the victim's rights. The agent is not wronged by what reasonable defenders do to her not because she "had it coming" in light of her misdeeds (cf. point 3 further above in this section). Instead, she is liable to the reasonable defensive treatment of others simply because she did not live up to the recognisable duties another's rights imposed on her, and this fact implies that others may now try to secure, at the agent's expense, what the agent's duties meant to secure in the first place—that the agent not harm her victim.

### (5) Agents render themselves liable only with respect to victims whose rights their actions disrespect.

**The Harm Triangle.** Shooter tries to shoot Victim without subjective justification. Shooter's actions threaten no harm to Victim. They do, however, threaten harm to nearby Bystander through a chain of events that Shooter did not foresee.

According to my interpretation of the DRA, Shooter may or may not be liable to defensive harm aimed at keeping Bystander safe. If there is a *mismatch* between the rights that Shooter subjectively disrespects and the objectively unjustified harm that she seems to threaten, then she is not liable to defensive harm. At first sight, it might appear that this is the case in the set-up as specified. After all, Shooter tries to harm *Victim*, but it is *Bystander* whom she is putting at risk. Against this, one might argue that by going after her innocent victim with a gun, Shooter shows insufficient respect not just for Victim's rights, but also for the rights of people more generally, at least for those of the people who happen to be in her vicinity. It is, after all, a well-known fact that guns are dangerous, and that we risk harming someone we did not intend to harm if we wield a gun. It might thus be argued that wielding a gun without any subjective justification in favour of doing so is insufficiently careful towards *anyone* one thereby puts at risk. Whether Shooter is liable to harm aimed at keeping Bystander safe therefore depends on how we settle what rights an agent subjectively disrespects by acting in a particular manner. As I see it, the most promising strategy for settling this issue relies centrally on a distinction made in tort law between harmful outcomes that are "within the risk" of an activity, and others that are not (see e.g. Hart 1985). If, for example, Shooter's actions threaten Bystander because Bystander happens to stand behind Victim (whom Shooter is pointing her gun at), the harm that Bystander is threatened with is unambiguously within the risk of Shooter's actions. That we might miss our target and shoot someone else standing close by is a well-known danger associated with wielding a gun. If, by contrast, Shooter's actions threaten Bystander because her running after Victim startles a venomous snake that then goes after Bystander, the harm that Bystander is threatened with is not similarly foreseeable, and thus most likely not within the risk of Shooter's actions.

## 5 What Makes the DRA Preferable to Competing Accounts

If we accept the enforcement framing of liability and the hybrid understanding of rights sketched, respectively, in Sections 3 and 2 above, the DRA emerges as a uniquely attractive account of liability to defensive harm. Consider the enforcement framing of liability first. If we accept it, we will also want to accept the following *desideratum* for any account of liability to defensive harm:

**The fact-relative test case.** Where everyone has access to all the morally relevant facts, an agent renders herself liable to objectively necessary and objectively proportionate defensive harm if, and only if, she threatens a rights violation.

The enforcement framing of liability claims that an agent renders herself liable to defensive harm by threatening a rights violation. Where everyone has access to all the morally relevant facts, an agent who threatens a rights violation will thus render herself liable to whatever harm is objectively necessary and proportionate to preventing the violation.

The DRA satisfies this fact-relative desideratum. Culpability accounts of liability do not. Such accounts are committed to the thought that culpability is a necessary condition of liability. Plausibly, however, culpability is *not* a necessary condition for threatening a rights violation.<sup>24</sup> In *Duress*, for example, Mother plausibly threatens to violate Stranger's rights even if the immense pressure that she is under is fully exculpating. If we accept an enforcement framing of liability and reject culpability as a necessary condition for threatening a rights violation, we should thus reject culpability accounts of liability.

This is not to say, of course, that culpability accounts are without appeal even if we accept an enforcement framing of liability. Their key advantage is that if we accept one of them, then liable individuals have no reasonable complaint against the reduction in their moral status. The justification that defenders of culpability accounts can offer to a liable agent runs something like this: "As a culpable agent, you have failed to abide by moral requirements in a way that we can hold you to account for. As a direct consequence of your failure, another individual is now threatened with harm. One way of holding you to account is by enforcing the rules that you have culpably ignored. We do this by imposing on you the costs that are necessary and proportionate to ensuring that others are not negatively affected by your non-compliance." The key thought behind this justification is that culpability implies accountability, and that enforceability, as well as the temporary reduction in moral status that it implies, are part of accountability.

If we accept the DRA, this same justification is not available to us. It seems to me, however, that a similarly strong justification is possible. In a nutshell, even if culpability implies accountability, not all accountability—and, in particular, not the accountability implied by an agent's liability—presupposes culpability. According to the DRA, what grounds an agent's liability-as-a-type-of-accountability is her disrespect for the rights of others. An agent shows such disrespect if she goes against the subjective requirements imposed on her by others' rights, thus deliberating or conducting herself in a flawed manner. The agential flaws that we can point to plausibly ground an agent's liability even in cases where it would be inappropriate to blame her. *Pace* culpability accounts, the relevant question is thus not whether we can blame an agent for her behaviour. The relevant question is, instead, whether there are significant flaws in how the agent deliberated or in how she conducted herself. In *Duress*, we might thus say to Mother: “Your actions towards Stranger do not make you blameworthy given the difficult situation you are in. They are nevertheless disrespectful of Stranger’s rights. By taking aim at Stranger when you know him not to be liable, you are showing insufficient concern for his well-being, even if this lack of concern is completely understandable under the circumstances. To hold you to account for this is not to blame you; it is merely to insist that you are conducting yourself in a flawed manner, and that this makes it appropriate to hold you to account, in the liability sense, for the unjustified harm you thereby seem to us to threaten.”

<sup>24</sup> Defenders of culpability accounts do not claim that it is (see e.g. Ferzan 2005, p. 714), as they do not accept an enforcement framing of liability.

{31}------------------------------------------------

There are influential existing accounts of liability other than culpability accounts, in particular Thomson’s Causal View (Thomson 1991), the Moral Responsibility Account defended most centrally by Otsuka (1994) and McMahan (2005), as well as Jonathan Quong’s Moral Status Account (Quong 2020).<sup>25</sup> Unlike the DRA, these accounts all imply that an agent can sometimes appropriately be held to account, in the liability sense, for objectively unjustified harm not connected to any agential failure of hers. They all imply, that is, that an agent may sometimes render herself liable despite deliberating flawlessly, and then conducting herself accordingly. In such cases, there is nothing the agent could have done better in light of her epistemic situation at the time of acting, but her actions nevertheless threaten objectively unjustified harm. As I see it, this implies that the DRA is preferable to the Causal View, the Moral Responsibility Account, and the Moral Status Account. By failing to tie an agent’s liability to a relevant agential failure, these accounts imply that an agent will sometimes have a reasonable complaint against the reduction in her moral status implied by her liability. The agent might reasonably complain, that is, that her liability is grounded in “what she did”, yet she cannot be faulted for what she did, given the circumstances she found herself in.

### 6.2 Objection 2: The DRA Fails to be Action-Guiding

The DRA ties liability to defensive harm to what things look like from the perspective of the person who renders herself liable. As a would-be defender, however, I will not always have epistemic access to what things look like from that particular point of view. Suppose, for example, that I am a combatant in what I reasonably believe is a just war. I will then not usually know to what extent a particular enemy combatant has abided by the subjective requirements that my and others' rights impose on that combatant. According to the DRA, however, the enemy combatant is liable to be harmed only if she has acted against some of these requirements.

This, then, gives rise to the worry that the DRA fails to yield an action-guiding account of permissible self-defence (see Frowe 2010, p. 251; van der Vossen 2016). To know the extent to which I am permitted to defend myself, so the worry can be spelled out, I need to know whether the person who seems to be threatening me is liable to defensive harm. But if this depends on information that may not be available to me at the time of acting, the account of permissible defence implied by the DRA is impractical. This impracticability is a major problem, as any worthwhile account of permissible self-defence should tell would-be defenders what they are permitted to do *given the circumstances they find themselves in*.

Bas van der Vossen (2016) takes practicality worries extremely seriously, and this leads him to defend an entirely subjective account of permissible self-defence. In addition, he assumes that liability to defensive harm is best understood as the conceptual flipside of permissible self-defence, such that we may permissibly defend ourselves if, and only if, as well as precisely to the extent that, an agent is liable to defensive harm. Based on this, he argues that an agent's liability, as well as whether the agent is about to violate any rights, turns exclusively on the information that is available to the potential victim. For van der Vossen (2016), it is thus possible that an agent violates others' rights even if she does not disrespect anyone's rights, and even if she threatens no harm. To me, this is unacceptably counterintuitive. Van der Vossen, by contrast, insists that his account remains attractive despite its counterintuitive implications. He argues that we can provide a more intuitively pleasing conceptualisation of liability only if we allow that the conditions of liability and those of permissible self-defence can come apart. If we do this, however, we rob the notion of liability of any practical significance.

{34}------------------------------------------------

Frowe (2010, p. 247) disagrees. She argues that we need to decouple the notion of permissible self-defence from the notion of liability to get an action-guiding account of permissible self-defence. According to Frowe (2010), the presumed liability of an agent plays *no role* whatsoever in determining whether a victim may permissibly use force against his threatener; the threatener's liability and what the victim may do are "logically independent" (Frowe 2010, p. 264). For Frowe, a victim may permissibly defend himself whenever he is not culpable for the situation that he finds himself in, and he reasonably believes that the threatener will harm him unless he defends himself. That her account of permissible self-defence might rob the notion of liability of much of its significance is not something she worries about.

Frowe's logical independence claim seems to me too strong. More precisely, if we assume a fact-relative universe where both threatener and victim have epistemic access to all the morally relevant facts, liability and permissible self-defence do seem to be two sides of the same coin (as laid out in Section 5 above). It is only once we introduce epistemic limitations—once we assume, that is, that a potential victim is unsure whether his threatener is liable to defensive harm—that what the potential victim may permissibly do in self-defence no longer turns on the threatener's (actual) liability, but on the victim's or other defenders' reasonable perception of it.

I thus defend an intermediate position between the positions of van der Vossen (2016) and Frowe (2010). I share their assumption that any plausible account of permissible self-defence has to provide useful practical guidance to would-be defenders. *Pace* van der Vossen and Frowe, however, I claim that this neither implies that a highly counterintuitive account of liability is called for (van der Vossen's position), nor does it imply that we should embrace Frowe's logical independence thesis. Instead, I defend a view according to which (hopefully intuitive) considerations of liability inform, but do not determine, what would-be defenders may permissibly do given the circumstances that they find themselves in.

The DRA, together with the hybrid understanding of rights that grounds it, implies that we can always ensure, as defenders, that our defensive actions are permissible and, therefore, at most permissibly infringe the rights of the person we are targeting. All we have to do is make sure that we are acting respectfully, where this is an entirely subjective notion. More precisely, defenders act respectfully towards the person they are targeting if (i) they base their decision to take defensive measures on the reasonable perception that the targeted person's seemingly disrespectful conduct is threatening a rights violation, and (ii) they inflict only what they reasonably deem both necessary and proportionate harm to averting the relevant threat. Defenders who abide by these constraints act permissibly even if they are mistaken in their assessment of the situation, and they therefore do not render themselves liable to defensive harm according to the DRA. This, I contend, provides them with as much normative assurance as anyone can reasonably hope for.

{35}------------------------------------------------

Admittedly, on the account of rights and liability that I have provided in this paper, it is always possible that a respectful defender ends up targeting a non-liable person, in which case the non-liable person is permitted to fight back, and might be owed compensation and an apology *ex post*. Some might wish for an account of permissible self-defence that enables defenders to rule out, at the time of acting, that this will be the situation they find themselves in. Such a wish, however, seems to me to ask for more assurances and guarantees than our moral rules are able to deliver. That we might make reasonable mistakes—mistakes that might, moreover, have unpleasant normative consequences—appears to me an ineliminable fact of our moral lives. We are not, after all, omniscient beings.

## 7 Conclusion

In this paper, I have argued for a novel and hybrid understanding of our rights against being harmed, and I have developed a *Disrespect for Rights Account of Liability to Defensive Harm* (DRA) to complement it. On my proposed account of rights, an agent threatens to violate her victim’s rights only if she acts against the subjective requirements imposed on her by these rights, thus disrespecting the rights. I have also argued that such disrespect for rights grounds an agent’s liability to defensive harm. This way of looking at things, I have claimed, allows us to take seriously a number of key considerations, including the thought that rights are centrally intended to guide our deliberation and our conduct, as well as the thought that an agent’s liability to defensive harm ought to be grounded in her relevant and recognisable agential failures.

Much work remains to be done, of course. I accept that I have left the notion of “respect for rights” rather vague, and that more effort is needed to clarify what it means to live up to the subjective requirements that are imposed on us by the rights of others. I have, for example, mentioned only in passing that whether we act respectfully depends on the kinds of evidence we base our targeting decisions on (see Section 4, point 4). I have, moreover, not even attempted to settle what we may permissibly do to protect ourselves against a permissible rights infringements (see Section 6.1), limiting myself instead to enforceability considerations tied to threatened rights violations.

What I hope to have provided in this paper is an exciting new way for thinking about rights and their enforceability. If I have succeeded, it will be obvious why tackling the work that remains to be done is a worthwhile task.

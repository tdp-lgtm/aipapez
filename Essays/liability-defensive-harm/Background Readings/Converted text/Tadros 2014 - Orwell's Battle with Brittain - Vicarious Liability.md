# Orwell's Battle with Brittain: Vicarious Liability for Unjust Aggression

I aim to defend the spirit of George Orwell in his battle with Vera Brittain. In her pamphlet *Seeds of Chaos*, Brittain complained about the British and US policy of “obliteration bombing” executed against the Germans in World War II. Obliteration bombing, according to Brittain, involved the targeting of civilians to “soften up” an area before a ground invasion. Had the British public known that civilians were systematically targeted, Brittain claimed, these deaths would have weighed heavily on their consciences.<sup>1</sup> The targeting of civilians by Britain and the United States was not restricted to the infamous bombing of Dresden.<sup>2</sup> Civilians were targeted in more than fifty German cities, causing widespread death and destruction. In total, well over three hundred thousand civilians were killed by obliteration bombing in Germany alone, not to mention the 330,000 civilians killed as a result of US targeting of civilians in Japan, most notoriously in the nuclear attacks on Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

Orwell's objection to Brittain is not well defended. The most significant weakness is that he implies that the permissibility of killing A rather than B depends simply on whether it is **better** that A is killed than B. However, the permissibility of an action does not depend only on its consequences. Most importantly, other things equal, if A but not B is **liable** to be killed, A rather than B ought to be killed, even if it is no better that A rather than B is killed. Hence, Orwell laid himself open to an obvious response. Those defending a strict principle of noncombatant immunity typically argue that only combatants are liable to be killed. Hence, only they may be targeted.<sup>6</sup>

I will not defend Orwell's views as he stated them, nor will I appeal to Orwell's arguments. I defend Orwell's spirit. Orwell's complaint is better understood as **a complaint about fairness**. It is unfair sharply to discriminate between combatants and noncombatants. Discriminating in this way is unfair even if combatants act wrongly in prosecuting the war, and even if they are negligent in deciding to fight. If the war would end more swiftly, with fewer casualties overall, noncombatants rather than combatants ought sometimes to be killed to achieve this end.

To defend this view, I owe a response to the claim that only combatants are liable to be harmed. One familiar but controversial response is that some noncombatants are liable to be harmed in virtue of the causal contribution they make to the threats that combatants pose.<sup>7</sup> This argument, even if successful, applies only to noncombatants who make the relevant **causal contribution** to the war.

The argument that I offer does not depend on the causal contributions of noncombatants. I argue that some noncombatants are **vicariously liable** for the wrongs of combatants. **Vicariously liable does not depend on causation.** Fairness determines the distribution of liability, and fairness militates against a strict causation requirement on liability.

It is relatively uncontroversial that considerations of **fairness** determine who is liable to be harmed to avert a threat and to what degree.<sup>8</sup> For example, suppose that X knowingly takes a small risk that Y will be harmed in the course of performing an act done for X's own benefit. Relative to the evidence that he has, let us suppose, X acts permissibly. As a result of **bad luck**, the act results in Y facing a threat, which it was very difficult for Y to avoid. X is typically liable to be harmed to avert the threat. In virtue of X's liability, the constraint on intentionally harming X to save Y does not apply, or does not apply as stringently. The underlying argument that warrants X's liability to be harmed is that **it is fair that X internalizes the costs of his own risky activities, at least if those activities are performed for his own benefit.**

Here I consider a broader set of fairness-based considerations to show that **noncombatants are sometimes liable** to be harmed to avert the unjust threats that combatants pose. **I will not show that noncombatants are vicariously liable to be killed, only that they are liable to be harmed, to avert these threats.** Even this more modest liability makes a significant difference to the permissibility of killing them. The fact that a person is liable to be harmed to some degree can contribute to a more complete argument for the permissibility of killing that person. It follows that the sharp discrimination between combatants and noncombatants that Britain perpetuated, and that many endorse, is unjust.

My rejection of noncombatant immunity will not vindicate the kind of targeting that Britain abhorred, however. Obliteration bombing violates a stringent (though not exceptionless) principle against **manipulative harming**. This principle prohibits terrorizing a population to undermine the war effort on the unjust side.<sup>9</sup> This prohibition has nothing to do with noncombatant immunity: it also applies to many combatants. The prohibition on manipulative harming is more stringent than the prohibition on another kind of intentional harming, what is called **“eliminative harming,”** which involves intentionally harming a person to eliminate the threat that the person poses.<sup>10</sup> We should abhor obliteration bombing primarily in virtue of the **mode of agency** it involves rather than the identity of its target.<sup>11</sup>

Using a person as a means in a way that harms a person is typically referred to as **“opportunistic harming”** rather than my preferred label, **“manipulative harming.”** This more familiar usage is misleading. Harming is opportunistic if the harm that is imposed on a person occurs in the course of exploiting an opportunity to benefit others that exists only in virtue of that person’s presence.<sup>12</sup> But the question of whether harming is opportunistic in this sense is orthogonal to the question of whether a person is used as a means: as we shall see, a person may be used as a means to avert a threat that she poses. Doing this would not be opportunistic in the following sense. If the person is used as a means to avert a threat that she is causally responsible for, her overall contribution to the situation will be only to be neutral. Overall, her presence will not have provided an opportunity for the person who harms her to improve his situation, or the situation of others, when compared with her not being present.

Quinn, and those influenced by him, run the distinct ideas of opportunism and manipulation together,<sup>13</sup> but their moral force is distinct. **Opportunism** is concerned with **benefiting from a person's presence** in a way that harms her. **Manipulation** is concerned with the distinct idea that using a person as a means to an end is morally similar to requiring that person to act in service of that end without her consent, something that it is typically permissible to do only if that person has a **duty to serve that end** at the relevant cost.<sup>14</sup> Hence, it is better to use the label "manipulative harming" to refer to using a person as a means in a way that harms her.

In Section V, I briefly show why rejecting noncombatant immunity does not yield a permission to terrorize noncombatants.

## I. TWO CLASSES OF LIABILITY

The idea of **vicarious liability** relies on the idea of liability.<sup>15</sup> Philosophical work on liability is in its infancy. My focus is on moral liability, not legal liability, whether that is understood positivistically or normatively. Understood positivistically, legal liability is liability that is enforced by law. Understood normatively, legal liability is liability that may or must be enforced by law. That X is morally liable to  $\nu$  obviously does not imply positivistic legal liability. It does not even imply normative legal liability. Moral liability need not be legally enforceable.

13. See, for example, *ibid.*, p. 191.

14. For further argument for the distinct moral force of these two ideas, see V. Tadros, "Responses," *Law and Philosophy* 32 (2013): 289–91.

15. My account of liability draws on the influential discussion in McMahan, *Killing in War*, chap. 1.1. My account of liability is broader than McMahan's in that it is not restricted to rights-forfeiture. This is primarily a matter of terminology: McMahan agrees that there are cases where a person lacks or loses a right against being harmed, and hence harming her does not wrong her, even though she has not forfeited her rights. See J. McMahan, "Individual Liability in War: A Response to Fabre, Leveringhaus and Tadros," *Utilitas* 24 (2012): 292–94.

Although “moral liability” is a term of art, it refers to a familiar moral idea, that **harming a person need not wrong that person, even pro tanto**. The idea of liability is, though, more specific than that. A person who waives her right not to be harmed, or who consents to being harmed, does not render herself liable to be harmed. Yet harming such a person does not wrong her.<sup>16</sup> **If a person is liable to be harmed, harming her against her will does not wrong her.**

**Liability claims** typically take the form: **X is liable to  $\nu$  for  $g$ . X is a person,  $\nu$  is something burdensome that, subject to some conditions, may be imposed on X, and  $g$  is a goal for the sake of which  $\nu$  may be imposed on X. If X is liable to  $\nu$  for  $g$ , some agent, Y, has standing to impose  $\nu$  on X for  $g$  under some conditions.** Y may be anyone, but may be restricted to particular agents—the state, for example.

Although liability is importantly connected to permissibility, liability does not imply permissibility, nor does permissibility imply liability. Liability does not imply permissibility in that it is sometimes wrong, all things considered, to give a person what she is liable to. For example, if making me pay compensation will give me a heart attack, or will render me unable to support my children, compelling me to pay compensation is typically wrong even if I am liable to pay it. Permissibility does not imply liability in that a nonliable person’s rights against being harmed may be overridden. For example, it may be permissible to harm non-combatants as a side effect of the pursuit of a just cause even if they are not liable to be harmed.

Here is a serviceable account of liability:

*Liability:* X is liable to  $\nu$  **by Y** in order to  $g$  if **Y does not wrong** X by imposing  $\nu$  on X for  $g$  without X’s consent.

*Liability* captures standard usage of the term “liability” in legal and moral contexts. For example, X’s liability to pay taxes implies that the state (Y) does not wrong X by taking X's taxes without X's consent. X's liability to be conscripted implies that the state does not wrong X by conscripting X without X's consent.

While *Liability* is workable, it may need refining. Perhaps there are circumstances where Y harms X against X's will **without wronging X and yet X is not liable to be harmed**. Here is a controversial example. Y sets up a business that puts X out of business. It is controversial whether Y imposes a burden on X. X is certainly not liable to be put out of business, though. If this is a case of imposition, *Liability* needs amendment, perhaps by restricting liability claims to claims about **intentionally affecting a person in a way that burdens her**. If X were permitted intentionally to drive Y out of business, it seems apt to claim that Y is liable to be driven out of business by X.

A general normative theory of liability aims to provide **a general explanation of the fact that we lack certain rights against interference**. My own view is that in central cases of liability, the liable person has **an enforceable duty to serve certain ends**, or **would have such a duty were she able to serve those ends, even if executing the duty is burdensome**. When a person has such a duty, or would have such a duty, she **lacks a complaint** that she would otherwise have against having burdens imposed on her in service of that end: the complaint that she may decide for herself what ends to pursue.<sup>17</sup> I do not claim that there are no cases where a person is liable to be harmed and yet lacks an enforceable duty to harm herself, only that enforceable duties provide the explanation of liability claims in central cases.

Not everyone accepts this view of liability, and I do not rely on it here. Those who disagree about the best general normative theory of liability can agree about the facts that render a person liable. For example, we might agree that being **responsible** for posing a threat typically renders a person liable to be harmed to avert the threat, while disagreeing about whether this is so in virtue of the fact that the person who is responsible for posing a threat incurs an enforceable duty to avert it. Though I will occasionally refer to the duties that noncombatants have to avert threats posed by combatants, the force of the arguments that I offer does not depend on the best general theory of liability.

The conceptual account of liability that I offer is controversial in another way: **it implies that some people who are liable to be harmed have not done anything to forfeit their rights.** It might be argued that there is something special about **rights-forfeiture** that the idea of liability aims to capture. To respond to this concern, *Liability* can be divided into two subclasses. One subclass, the one that has attracted the most attention from just war theorists, concerns **rights-forfeiture**:

*Liability (Forfeiture)*: X is liable to  $v$  by Y in order to  $g$  if Y does not wrong X by imposing  $v$  on X for  $g$  without X's consent **in virtue of the fact that X has forfeited his right not to have  $v$  imposed on him by Y for  $g$ .**

*Liability (Forfeiture)* captures standard cases of self-defense: the liability of culpable attackers to be harmed corresponds to the forfeiture of their rights.

There is room for dispute about how rights are forfeited, but whatever the right view, *Liability (Forfeiture)* is an incomplete account of liability. It does not capture taxation or conscription, and the term “liability” is naturally used in its moral sense in these contexts. Citizens become liable to pay taxes when they reach a certain wage threshold. They become liable to be conscripted, in states where conscription is legitimate, when they reach a certain age. Yet these citizens do not forfeit their rights.

Although we need not use the term “liability” to describe these cases, I find the term apt, for imposing burdens on others without their consent, in these contexts, does not wrong them. Hence, I distinguish another subclass of liability:

*Liability (Nonforfeiture)*: X is liable to  $v$  by Y in order to  $g$  if Y does not wrong X by imposing  $v$  on X for  $g$  without X's consent **even though X has not forfeited his right not to have  $v$  imposed on him by Y for  $g$ .**

*Liability (Nonforfeiture)* helpfully allows us clearly to ask some difficult familiar questions about **preventive harm**. For example, consider whether nonresponsible threats, such as those blown down wells, are liable to be harmed to avert the threats that their bodies pose. They have not forfeited their rights. **Forfeiting a right requires action.** But this does not imply that harming nonresponsible threats wrongs them. They may have **lost their rights** in virtue of the fact that their bodies pose threats to others.<sup>18</sup> Whether or not they have lost their rights depends on the answer to a substantive moral question: **whether a person can lose a right against preventive harm without acting**. It is handy to be able to ask this question by asking whether nonresponsible threats, just like their responsible counterparts, are liable to be harmed. Asking this question in this way depends on *Liability (Nonforfeiture)*.

## II. WHAT IS VICARIOUS LIABILITY?

Vicarious liability is a species of liability without forfeiture. It arises when one person loses a right in virtue of someone else's actions.<sup>19</sup> Here is an account of vicarious liability:

*Vicarious Liability:* X is vicariously liable to  $v$  in virtue of a bad event, E, that has occurred, or that will occur, if and only if:

- (1) X is liable to  $v$  in virtue of E;
- (2) X is not liable to  $v$  in virtue of his causal or moral responsibility for E;<sup>20</sup>
- (3) X is liable to  $v$  in virtue of **X's relationship with another person**, Y; and
- (4) Y is, or will be, causally and morally responsible for E.

If I am right that taxation is a case of liability, a person may be liable to suffer a loss without having lost a right—the person who reaches a certain wage threshold, and hence becomes liable to pay a higher rate of taxation, has not lost any right that she previously had.

Liability, in clause (1), could be liability to preventive, compensatory, or punitive harm. Our focus is on preventive harm.

Though vicarious liability has something of the flavor of collective liability, they are distinct. In the parallel clauses of *Collective Liability*, Y is a collective rather than an individual and X is a member of that collective. Much of the large body of work on collective responsibility considers difficult metaphysical and moral questions about whether collectives can satisfy the conditions of agency that render blame appropriate. Attention has also been paid to the question of whether it is fair to distribute the burdens of collective action among members of the collective.<sup>21</sup> Clearly, membership in a collective does not render a person *blameworthy* for the wrongful actions of the collective. It does not follow that membership is insufficient for liability.<sup>22</sup>

I am unsure whether collective agency makes a difference to liability. I have some doubts.<sup>23</sup> Fairness may render citizens of a state liable for actions orchestrated by state officials. Whether this is so may not depend on whether state institutions have the qualities sufficient for collective agency.<sup>24</sup> Whether or not collective liability is independently important, though, vicarious liability merits more philosophical attention than it has received.

Vicarious liability arises through *the relationship* between the vicariously liable person (X) and a perpetrator (Y). This illuminates the central puzzle of vicarious liability: *why is X liable to be harmed in virtue of E when Y, and not X, is responsible for E?* Two sets of considerations of fairness might militate in favor of vicarious liability. Victim-centered considerations concern fairness between X and the (potential) victims of E. Perpetrator-centered considerations concern fairness between X and Y.<sup>25</sup>

To see the distinction between these sets of considerations, consider:

*Work:* X and Y work for a firm. In the course of his work, Y negligently harms V.

Here are four nonexhaustive possibilities:

- (1) Neither X nor Y is liable to compensate V.
- (2) Y is solely liable to compensate V.
- (3) X is solely liable to compensate V.
- (4) X and Y are jointly and severally liable to compensate V.

Joint and several liability allows V to claim against either X or Y for the full amount. Typically, V may claim against either X or Y, but if V claims against X, X may claim against Y for part of the cost.

Determining which possibility is true depends in part on the following ideas. Unless others compensate V, if (1) is true, Y's act leaves V worse off through no fault of his own. (2) is typically preferable to (1): given that Y negligently caused the harm, it is fair that he, rather than V, bears the cost. However, **victim-centered considerations may militate in favor of (3) or (4) rather than (2).** If V is not entitled to claim against X, V may be left uncompensated, for Y may **not be able** to compensate V. For various reasons, such as the fact that X and not V systematically benefits from Y playing his role, X may be **expected at least to share** the costs with V if Y cannot be made to pay.

Perpetrator-centered reasons may also favor (3) or (4) over (2). X and Y each may systematically benefit from Y conducting his role. Furthermore, given their respective roles, harm may be much more likely to be caused by Y's negligence than by X's. **If X does not share liability for Y's act, X will benefit without being exposed to the risk of liability.** Y, in contrast, is exposed to the risk of liability for his negligence. It is not easy to avoid being negligent throughout the whole of one's career. Given this, Y might legitimately complain against bearing sole liability for his negligence.

For example, suppose that X and Y work for a small roofing company involving only the two. There are two roles in the company: one does the books, the other fixes roofs. Suppose that X is better at the books and Y is better at fixing roofs. Suppose also that they benefit equally from the joint activity. As the books are scrutinized by an independent accountant, X is unlikely to harm anyone if he is negligent. In contrast, Y's negligence is likely to cause harm. It seems unfair for Y to bear all of the costs of his negligence.

These reasons for X's vicarious liability must defeat countervailing reasons. Liability may be very costly for X, and it will be impossible for him to avoid liability other than by leaving the company. Furthermore, other people could share the costs of compensating V for the harm that he has suffered. Answers must be offered to these complaints. If there are good answers, X and Y should share the costs of Y's negligence, for example by contributing equally to an insurance scheme.

These sketchy remarks need further development. I develop them in the context of liability to preventive harm in war, where vicarious liability is more controversial.

## III. NONCOMBATANT IMMUNITY AND THE RISK TO COMBATANTS

Suppose that a state starts a war. Some citizens fight in that war. Others do not. The question is whether it is fair that liability is borne solely by those who fight. In the spirit of Orwell, I will argue that it is not. My focus is on perpetrator-centered considerations of fairness: those between combatants and noncombatants of the same state.<sup>26</sup> I leave aside victim-centered considerations of fairness between citizens of states on opposing sides of the war.<sup>27</sup>

The argument in favor of noncombatant liability depends on the claim that combatants benefit from such liability. When one country unjustly goes to war with another, combatants on the unjust side become liable to be harmed to avert the threat they pose. I focus my attention on the unjust side, since it is uncontroversial that combatants on the unjust side are liable to be harmed. It is a matter of dispute whether combatants on the just side are also liable to be harmed.<sup>28</sup> If they are, my argument can, with some adjustments that I do not outline here, be extended to the just side.

The standard prohibition of harming noncombatants is restricted to intentional harming: harming noncombatants as a side effect is permitted if doing so is proportional to the good that the just side wishes to advance.<sup>30</sup> The vicarious liability of noncombatants is also relevant to determining when harming them as a side effect is proportionate. In the remainder of this section I elaborate these points.

### A. Intentional Targeting of Noncombatants

Targeting noncombatants is only plausibly permitted where doing so averts a greater harm that others will otherwise face. *Whether to Delay:* During a war, some noncombatants produce fuel for tanks that unjust combatants drive. The just side has two options: either target these noncombatants, or wait until their supplies reach the front line and target combatants. If it targets these noncombatants, fewer unjust combatants will be killed to secure victory, since the unjust side will have fewer operational tanks. Furthermore, the death toll on the unjust side will be lower overall than the death toll if these noncombatants are not killed. The prospects of the just side are unaffected by the decision.

The last feature of the case may seem impossible. It is not. Suppose, for example, that the just side is developing a devastating weapon. If it kills noncombatants on the unjust side, it will use conventional weapons. As these noncombatants are poorly equipped to defend themselves, the just side will suffer few casualties. If it waits until the fuel reaches the front, the devastating weapon will be available. The only way to secure victory will be to use this weapon. Doing so, let us suppose, will be proportionate even though many unjust combatants will be killed. This will also result in few casualties on the just side. These two methods may be equally effective in averting the threat and have equal prospects of success.

It might be argued that the prospects of success will inevitably be greater if the noncombatants are targeted now, as the just side will still have an opportunity to use the devastating weapon later. But this need not be true either—targeting the noncombatants may deprive the just side of the opportunity to use the devastating weapon, or may make its use disproportionate when compared with other methods of prosecuting the war.

Notice two features of this case. First, as the prospects of the just side are unaffected by their decision, it isolates perpetrator-centered considerations of fairness. Second, if noncombatants are targeted, they are not harmed manipulatively. The question of noncombatant immunity is often confused with the question of whether terrorism is permitted. For example, Michael Walzer claims that “the refusal to make ordinary people into targets, whatever their nationality or even their politics, is the only way to say no to terrorism.”<sup>31</sup> Walzer conflates two distinct issues here: whether noncombatants may be targeted and whether noncombatants (or combatants, for that matter) may be killed as a means of terrorizing others.

We can see from *Whether to Delay* that a strict principle of noncombatant immunity may increase combatant casualties. It may also increase the risk that each combatant faces. And it may increase the overall death toll. This reveals the error in another claim that has been thought to support a principle of noncombatant immunity: that adhering to this principle reduces the overall death toll. For example, Avashai Margalit and Michael Walzer claim that the doctrine of noncombatant immunity should be endorsed because “violence is evil, and . . . we should limit the scope of violence as much as is realistically possible.”<sup>32</sup> As *Whether to Delay* illustrates, it is a contingent matter whether adherence to a principle of noncombatant immunity reduces the death toll.<sup>33</sup>

Perhaps adhering to a strict principle of noncombatant immunity will tend to reduce the death toll of wars in practice. Doing so might result in fewer deaths than attempting to consider each set of circumstances on its merits. ### *B. Liability and Side-Effect Harming*

A strict principle of noncombatant immunity increases the risk to combatants on the unjust side in a further way. If a person is liable to be harmed to avert a threat, harming that person does not wrong her. This can be true if the person is harmed intentionally or as a side effect. It follows that if there is a threat to be averted, and some people must be harmed as a side effect of averting it, the liable rather than the nonliable typically ought to be harmed.

Consider:

*Bomb Disposal:* One hundred villains together execute a plan to plant a bomb that will kill several thousand people if nothing is done. I can detonate the bomb before these deaths occur, but this will certainly result in a loss of life. If I detonate the bomb now, all one hundred villains will be killed. If I detonate it later, ten innocent bystanders will be killed.

I ought to detonate the bomb now rather than later, even though doing so has a greater death toll. Since the villains are liable to be killed, their deaths do not weigh as heavily in the balance as the innocent bystanders.

If combatants but not noncombatants are liable to be harmed, adhering to principles of *jus in bello* will sometimes increase the number of casualties overall.

Consider:

*End the War:* The unjust side in a war is developing a weapon of mass destruction. If this weapon is destroyed, the unjust side will capitulate immediately in the face of certain defeat. The just side has discovered the location of the weapon and can destroy it. It has two options: (1) if the WMD is targeted while it is in transit, ten noncombatants will be killed as a side effect; (2) if it is targeted at the barracks where it is stored, one hundred combatants will be killed. The prospects of the just side are unaffected by this decision.

If it is impermissible to kill the ten noncombatants, the combatants may permissibly be killed to avert the threat that the bomb poses. Some may think this is because they are liable to be killed. In my view, if they make reasonable mistakes about the permissibility of their actions, or even if they are negligent, they are liable to be harmed to some degree less than death. Killing them is nevertheless permitted: their liability, combined with a lesser-evil justification, renders killing them permissible. If *only* the combatants, but not the noncombatants, are liable to be harmed, there are powerful reasons for the just side to choose (2). This is so even though doing so increases the death toll. While we should tolerate a larger number of deaths in cases such as *Bomb Disposal*, I argue, we ought to minimize the death toll in cases such as *End the War*.

## IV. A FAIRNESS-BASED ARGUMENT FOR NONCOMBATANT LIABILITY

If the just side adheres to principles of *jus in bello*, relaxing the principle of noncombatant immunity reduces the death toll in some circumstances. Given that combatants are typically the main targets in war, relaxing this principle also typically distributes the risk of being maimed and killed more evenly. These facts themselves significantly support noncombatant liability. However, given that it is primarily combatants who pose threats, is noncombatant liability fair? I think so.

My case for the vicarious liability of noncombatants draws on a wide range of considerations. I proceed as follows. One important factor in a perpetrator-centered case for vicarious liability concerns the degree of responsibility that combatants have for posing threats. I will investigate that factor in subsection A. In subsection B, I will consider features of the relationship between combatants and noncombatants that weaken the complaint that noncombatants have against sharing liability with combatants. Subsection C is concerned with the question of whether, in deciding to serve in the armed forces, combatants voluntarily accept that they, rather than noncombatants, will be targeted in war. These three subsections complete my case for vicarious liability of noncombatants.

{139}------------------------------------------------

Subsection D is concerned with implications of the fact that noncombatants are vicariously liable to be harmed, but not to be killed.

### *A. Avoidability and Attribution*

The justification of vicarious liability depends in part on the responsibility that perpetrators bear for wrongfully posing threats or causing harm. The more powerful the claim that the perpetrator is responsible for the harm that she causes, the weaker her claim that others share liability for that harm with her. The degree of responsibility of perpetrators depends in part on how difficult it is to avoid posing the threat or causing the harm. The easier it is for the perpetrator to avoid posing an unjust threat, the weaker her case that others share liability with her to avert the threat. For this reason, those who act reasonably, or who are negligent, have more powerful claims that others share liability with them than those who are highly culpable.

For familiar reasons, it is difficult for a combatant to avoid posing unjust threats to others on some occasions over the course of her career. First, relative to the evidence they have, some unjust combatants are justified in threatening others. Evidence-relative justifications do not typically vitiate liability. A person who mistakenly threatens me with lethal force is liable to be harmed to avert the threat that she poses even if her mistake is reasonable.<sup>39</sup>

Combatants are often not in a good position to identify the facts relevant to determining whether threatening others is justified. Even where they know the facts, it is difficult for them accurately to evaluate them. Furthermore, there are other people—their commanders or government officials—who have more evidence than they do and more expertise in evaluating it. When these commanders or government officials command them to go to war, they have some reason to trust these officials. At least in good political conditions, combatants sometimes ought to refrain from deciding for themselves whether the war that they are ordered to fight in is just.

Even where combatants do not believe that the war is just, they have a range of familiar excuses for fighting. The temptation to treat commanders and government officials as epistemic and moral authorities for the decision to go to war is great, especially since military training conditions combatants to unquestioningly follow commands. Combatants typically act under duress, and they are often subject to significant social pressure to fight from their peers.

These facts help to explain the intuitive difference between *Bomb Disposal* and *End the War*. In *Bomb Disposal*, it is easy for the villains to refrain from acting wrongly. In *End the War*, this is not true of the combatants. This supports the verdict I endorsed earlier: that the combatants in *End the War* are not liable to be killed. Their liability must be coupled with a lesser-evils justification to warrant killing them. Another implication is that these combatants have a more powerful case against being solely liable to avert the threats they pose.

Against this, some might argue that vicarious liability is less plausible where one person intentionally threatens to harm others, as is typically true in war. The mistakes that combatants make concern facts that affect permissibility, not facts about whether they threaten harm. They include mistakes about the liability of those who are threatened and mistakes about proportionality.

A person who intentionally threatens others, it might be argued, is deeply responsible for the threat she poses. One's degree of responsibility for a threat depends on the relationship between that threat and one's agency. One's intentions are central to one's agency, and hence one is more deeply responsible for the threats that one intentionally poses than the threats that one poses negligently. Since combatants are deeply responsible for the threats they pose, their claims that others should share liability with them are weaker.

Some might reject this argument on the grounds that claims about vicarious liability depend only on facts about those who will be held vicariously liable, and not facts about the person posing the threat.<sup>40</sup> However, whether a person is vicariously liable for a threat or harm that another causes is a comparative matter: the strength of the reasons to share liability among a wider group depends in part on the reasons that the principal has to bear sole responsibility for meeting the threat that he poses or responding to the harm that he causes. If our reasons to respond to a threat depend on the depth of our responsibility for that threat, and those who intend to pose threats are more deeply responsible for those threats, the claim that noncombatants ought to share liability with combatants for the threats combatants pose is somewhat weakened.

Others might reject this argument on the basis that the kind of responsibility that is relevant to a person's liabilities depends solely on avoidability and not on attributability.<sup>41</sup> I think that liability depends both on avoidability and on attributability.<sup>42</sup> The more deeply a bad outcome is attributable to us, the more stringent our obligation to respond to it. The more stringent our obligation to respond to a threat, the easier it is to justify imposing burdens on us to avert it. Hence, I acknowledge that the deep responsibility of combatants for the threats they pose counts against the vicarious liability of noncombatants.

But this consideration, while important, is not decisive. The fact that the threats that combatants pose are deeply attributable to them needs to be balanced against the fact that they usually act either reasonably or with some excuse. The difficulty of avoiding posing threats to others is sufficient to support some measure of vicarious liability, even for threats deeply attributable to the perpetrator.

### *B. The Relationship between Combatants and Noncombatants*

While it is important, the fact that combatants bear more limited responsibility for posing threats to others is insufficient to justify noncombatant liability. Vicarious liability depends on there being a special relationship between the perpetrator and those who share liability. There are several considerations that make relationships sufficiently special.

Vicarious liability is more familiar as a legal than a moral idea. The relationship between employers and employees is central to the law. It has now absorbed the most common case historically: the relationship between masters and servants.<sup>43</sup> Both victim-centered and perpetrator-centered considerations have been important in explaining vicarious liability in the employment context in the law. Although Nozick's case is intuitive, the fact that a person is a beneficiary of the course of conduct that gives rise to the harm is a central part of a more complete case for vicarious liability. To illuminate the considerations that strengthen such a case, contrast *Public Address* with:

The relationship between X and Y in *Liabile Benefactor* is modeled on the relationship between noncombatants and combatants. The role that combatants occupy, as well as the broad set of plans that they execute in carrying out this role, is in large measure created and performed for the sake of noncombatants. Not only do noncombatants benefit *ex ante* from the willingness of combatants to fight, but also they are the intended beneficiaries of this willingness. Combatants on the unjust side typically join the military not, or not only, for their own sake, but for the sake of noncombatants on the unjust side whom they are aiming to protect.

Furthermore, protecting civilians is an important and difficult task. Combatants bear significant risks in performing this task. They reasonably believe that noncombatants would consent to them performing this task, even if noncombatants were required to share liability in the case of negligent mistakes. Finally, combatants cannot seek the consent of each civilian to perform the task of protecting them. The most that they can do is what they in fact do: rely on the decisions of their superiors.

Perhaps it might be argued that those who disagree with the particular war that the combatant is fighting—those who march with placards declaring that the war is not in their name—are excluded from liability. I doubt it. Noncombatants cannot expect combatants to respond to their particular demand that a war is unjust. If noncombatants benefit from a standing army, and cannot expect combatants to respond to their individual judgments about war, they also cannot expect to be excluded from sharing liability on the grounds that they disagree with the prosecution of any war in particular.

{147}------------------------------------------------

Perhaps pacifist noncombatants, who generally oppose a standing army, have a more powerful objection to sharing liability with combatants, at least if they are willing to bear the resulting extra risk of being killed or maimed. Few noncombatants are in this position. And perhaps even pacifist noncombatants are liable to be harmed. Citizens may have an enforceable duty to support a standing military for the sake of fellow citizens. Combatants do what noncombatants would otherwise be *required* to do. Of course, they would not be required to participate in the unjust war that gives rise to liability. But they would be required to take decisions about going to war. Given that, they cannot complain about being liable to be harmed to protect those who stand in the place in which they would otherwise be required to stand.

### *C. Consent and the Military Role*

Some might respond that in choosing to join the military, combatants agree to shoulder the costs of war, and for this reason liability ought not to be shared with noncombatants. This response might further be supported on the grounds that the military role is ennobled if only combatants are liable to be harmed. At least some combatants find their role valuable in part because it involves sacrifice for the sake of others.

I doubt that this consideration decisively rules out noncombatant liability for six reasons. First, this argument would apply only to volunteers. And it would apply only to those who volunteer because they are willing to sacrifice themselves for the sake of others. Many combatants fight in part for the sake of noncombatants. But while they may value their protective role, they need not see the inherent risk involved in their role as part of its value. It may be the end, rather than the risks involved in achieving that end, that they value. Many combatants might regret rather than relish taking these risks.

Second, combatants who value sacrificing themselves for others when joining the military need not be willing to sacrifice themselves for the sake of noncombatants at the moment when they are about to be killed. I doubt that many combatants would be willing to bear this cost at the moment of death. The sentiments of the narrator in military veteran Kevin Powers's novel *Yellow Birds* is, I suspect, more common: I'd been trained to think war was the great unifier, that it brought people closer together than any other activity on earth. Bullshit. War is the great maker of solipsists: how are you going to save my life today? Dying would be one way. If you die, it becomes more likely that I will not.<sup>51</sup>

{148}------------------------------------------------

If there is no good reason to think that combatants are willing to sacrifice their lives for noncombatants at the moment of death, any general consent of combatants to put themselves at risk for the sake of others is irrelevant.

Third, combatants who are willing to sacrifice themselves for the sake of noncombatants may not be willing to do so when this sacrifice increases the overall death toll. They may be willing to sacrifice themselves only on condition that doing so saves lives overall. Compare:

*Fire Fighters:* Several fire fighters have entered a burning building in the first wave of the fire to rescue civilians. They have been overcome by smoke and are incapacitated but not dead. Other fire fighters enter the building with better equipment. They can now either rescue the incapacitated fire fighters, or rescue a smaller number of civilians.

Fire fighters are also willing to bear an increased risk of being harmed or killed for the sake of others. This makes some difference to the decision that the second group of fire fighters must make. Were the numbers equal, perhaps they should rescue the civilians rather than the fire fighters. But this difference is not decisive where the number of fire fighters who can be saved is significantly larger than the number of civilians. The second wave of fire fighters ought to rescue those in the first wave rather than the civilians unless the fire fighters clearly indicate that they would rather be killed even where the resulting overall death toll is larger.

Fourth, the fact that a person has voluntarily entered into a role that has certain expectations attached to it does not imply that those expectations are just. Even if Y had expected to bear all of the costs of his negligent action in *Work*, for example, imposing those costs on him would be unfair.

51. K. Powers, *Yellow Birds* (London: Sceptre, 2012), p. 12.

{149}------------------------------------------------

Fifth, in many jurisdictions, combatants typically come from difficult social circumstances where their employment opportunities are limited. Their choice to join the military is heavily constrained, and unjustly so. This injustice is exacerbated by a strict principle of noncombatant immunity. The idea that the armed forces consent to bear the costs of war may have greater moral significance in more ideal social circumstances. I doubt that it should be given much weight in the real world, where volunteering is often a less than fully voluntary activity.

Sixth, the idea that the role of combatant is ennobled if combatants bear sole liability for the unjust threats they pose is exaggerated. Relaxing the principle of noncombatant immunity would not erode the value of the military role. And it is difficult to believe that those on the just side should choose to kill combatants rather than noncombatants in cases such as *Whether to Delay* for the sake of the nobility of combatants.

### *D. The Implications of Vicarious Liability of Noncombatants*

It is not plausible that typical noncombatants are vicariously liable to be killed, only that they are liable to be harmed. Because they are not vicariously liable to be killed, any noncombatants who are killed in *Whether to Delay* and *End the War* are wronged. Nevertheless, noncombatant vicarious liability to be harmed, even to a degree less than death, has important practical implications.

First, many attacks in war are not certain to be lethal. Even when noncombatants are targeted, the intentions of those who target them may be inchoate. They aim to prevent noncombatants from contributing to the conflict, but causing their deaths is typically not necessary to secure that end. It is sufficient temporarily to disable them so that they can no longer contribute to the war effort. Where combatants intend to harm them to prevent them from contributing to the war, but not to kill them, they may not be wronged by the harm intentionally inflicted on them.

Second, the extent of a person's liability to be harmed makes a difference to the permissibility of killing that person. As I suggested earlier, the permissibility of killing a person does not rely on her being liable to be killed. The fact that killing a person wrongs her does not imply that it is wrong, all things considered, to kill her. A person's liability alters the proportionality calculation, helping to justify killing the person.

{150}------------------------------------------------

Combatants are liable to be killed only if they have a high degree of culpability and causal responsibility for the threats they pose. It is one thing to lose one's right against being harmed to some degree. It is quite another to lose one's right not to be killed. I doubt that the right to life is so fragile that it can be altogether lost by taking a reasonable risk that one will pose an unjust lethal threat to others, or even by being negligent in posing a lethal threat to others.

Suppose that noncombatant liability is significantly less than combatant liability in cases such as *Whether to Delay* and *End the War*. This may be true, given that combatants are directly responsible for posing threats. It may nevertheless be right to kill noncombatants rather than combatants if the number of combatants who must be killed is significantly greater than the number of noncombatants who must be killed to achieve the same result. Which decision is right will depend in part on the comparative magnitude of the liability of combatants and noncombatants.<sup>52</sup>

### *E. Young Children*

It might be objected that the argument presented supports the verdict that young children of a state that has perpetrated an unjust war are vicariously liable to be harmed to avert the threats that unjust combatants pose. Some may find this verdict implausible. Orwell expressed the view that children are a special case, though he failed to explain why. While there are some reasons to differentiate between children and adults, I doubt that children are altogether excluded from liability.

Children may occupy any of the roles that we have been considering so far. They may be combatants. They may contribute to the war, for example by helping to build munitions. And they may be noncombatants. It is not very plausible that it is wrong to kill child soldiers on the unjust side of a war if doing so is necessary to avert the threats that they pose to a greater number of children (or, for that matter, adults). Child soldiers are nonresponsible, or partially responsible, attackers. While some doubt the intuitive view that it is permissible to kill one nonresponsible person to protect one person from being killed,<sup>53</sup> it is not wrong to kill a nonresponsible attacker to prevent that person from killing a greater number. The same thing is arguably true of child soldiers.

Furthermore, it is not always wrong to target children to prevent them from contributing to unjust threats. For example, suppose that a child is about to hand a gun to his father, who will then use it to shoot many innocent people. It is not wrong to shoot the child if doing so is the only way to avert the threat that his father will otherwise pose. We might extend this argument to children who manufacture munitions during war.

Some may regard children as a special case in virtue of the fact that children are incapable of owing duties to support combatants in their roles. If a person's liabilities depend, at least in part, on the duties they owe to others, the liabilities of adults and children may differ in virtue of the fact that only adults and not children have these duties. Consider *Liable Benefactor*. It might be thought that X's liability to share the costs of averting the threat that Y unjustly poses to Z depends, in part at least, on the duty that X has to share these costs. Were X a child, X would lack these duties, and hence X could not be liable to be harmed on this basis.

The relationship between a person's liabilities and her duties, if those duties are enforceable, is powerful.<sup>54</sup> However, I doubt that this argument for excluding the liability of children is successful. The fact that a person lacks the capacities required to have certain duties does not in and of itself render that person immune from having costs imposed on her. An argument against the vicarious liability of children depends on the special reasons that we have to protect children from harm, and not on the fact that they lack the capacities necessary to have duties.

What reasons are there to harm the adult rather than the child, and are these reasons decisive? Perhaps the most important reason is that killing a child deprives her of a real chance at life. She is deprived of all of her adult life, and that is the part of her life during which she can pursue her own projects in a fully autonomous way.<sup>55</sup> That may render killing a child especially wrong. It is one of the reasons why the death of a child seems a greater tragedy than the death of an adult. It may also make the killing of a certain number of children disproportionate where killing the same number of adults in similar circumstances would be proportionate. I doubt, though, that children are altogether immune to sharing the costs of war.

## V. THE PROHIBITION ON TERRORISM

The vicarious liability of noncombatants to avert the threats that combatants unjustly pose to others provides at least some grounds to minimize the number of casualties in war by diverting threats away from combatants and toward noncombatants, for example in cases such as *Whether to Delay* and *End the War*.

This argument may seem to imply the permissibility of even more unconventional and extreme measures to end wars, where taking these measures reduces the number of casualties on the unjust side overall. For example, suppose (as was not true) that there were only two ways for the United States to secure victory against Japan in World War II: to launch a nuclear strike against civilians in Hiroshima and Nagasaki or to mount a conventional military attack against Japanese armed forces. Suppose, further, that victory was equally certain in either case.

Obviously, the fact that a conventional military attack would result in a greater number of US casualties would motivate the adoption of the first route. Let us suppose that this reason was not sufficiently powerful to justify the nuclear attack on civilians if these civilians were not liable to attack. If noncombatants are vicariously liable to be harmed, and the death toll would be lower overall, would it not follow that the nuclear strike against civilians is permitted?

In the introduction, I outlined the main argument against this conclusion. If a nuclear strike is used, killing civilians terrorizes the government into ending the war. In *Whether to Delay* and *End the War*, in contrast, the noncombatants who are killed are not killed in order to terrorize others. They are targeted to eliminate the threats they pose, or as a side effect, not as a means. Nonconventional warfare, such as the targeting of innocent civilians with nuclear weapons, is ruled out not because of the identity of the target, but in virtue of the fact that the target will manipulatively be harmed.

{153}------------------------------------------------

There is plenty of disagreement about whether and why terrorism is distinctively wrong. My view is familiar: terrorism uses innocent people as a means in a way that harms them. The stringency of the prohibition on using a person as a means to pursue the good has significant implications for the permissibility of harming combatants as well as noncombatants. Harming combatants to terrorize others to avert threats that they pose also sometimes seems wrong. That is not to say that it is always wrong to terrorize combatants (or, indeed, noncombatants). But the prohibition on terrorism is stringent, even if it is not without exception.

Against this, it might be argued that it is implausible that it is generally wrong to use combatants as a means to the greater good by killing them in order to terrorize other combatants. A standard way to resist an enemy attack involves the killing of some combatants in a unit so that other combatants in the unit flee. And wars typically end because the killing of a large number of combatants demoralizes the armed forces. Hence, the prohibition on terrorism has plausible application only if there is some morally important distinction to draw between combatants and noncombatants.

There is a great deal to say about the permissibility of harming combatants manipulatively, much more than I have space for here. I restrict myself to these brief comments. First, as I have already noted, some combatants, especially those with a high level of culpability for posing threats to others, are liable to be killed manipulatively. Second, if it is permissible to kill particular combatants in order to eliminate threats that they pose, it may also be permissible to kill them in a way that will achieve good results, such as causing the remaining members of their unit to flee. In many circumstances, this will justify the killing of combatants in circumstances where doing so also has the advantage that the enemy will be demoralized. Third, it does seem plausible that the restriction on manipulative harming applies to combatants: it is difficult to justify the killing of a combatant in order to terrorize other combatants where that combatant could otherwise be captured without harming him to eliminate the threat that he poses, at least if that combatant is not highly culpable for posing a threat to others.

{154}------------------------------------------------

While there is plenty of argument to be had about the application of the prohibition on manipulative harming to combatants, there is room for optimism that the application of a principle prohibiting manipulative harming will not have implausible implications for war without relying on the combatant/noncombatant distinction.

## VI. CONCLUSION

Obviously not all noncombatants of states that prosecute unjust wars are vicariously liable to be harmed. Some of the factors that I argued support vicarious liability will not be present in all cases. For example, the considerations just outlined are almost completely absent with respect to Jewish people living in Nazi Germany. Such people did not benefit from the existence of a standing army and would have no reason to authorize the military to make decisions whether to fight. They were not the intended beneficiaries of that military. And the Nazi cause was obviously unjust. The fact that some Nazis acted under duress seems insufficient on its own to support a case for vicarious liability.

In other circumstances, though, a stringent principle of noncombatant immunity is unfair to combatants on the unjust side. Noncombatants sometimes owe it to combatants to bear some of the risks of averting the unjust threats of war to protect noncombatants from death. This position may seem radical. It is not, I think, as radical as it at first seems.

It is not at all implausible that citizens of a state that wrongly occupies territory of another state are liable to bear the costs of economic sanctions imposed on that state.<sup>56</sup> Furthermore, vicarious liability of citizens to pay reparations for unjust wars perpetrated by their states is commonly accepted.<sup>57</sup> It would be surprising if a person who has a duty to bear a cost to compensate the victims of some harmful event does not also have a duty to bear an equivalent cost to avert the threat posed by that event. Duties to prevent, ameliorate, and compensate others for harm that they have suffered are closely related. Given this, the idea of vicarious liability of noncombatants to preventive harm should not be so surprising.

Sometimes it is morally permissible to seriously harm or kill people in self-defense, or in defense of others. In many of these cases, an important part of what makes the imposition of defensive harm morally permissible is the fact that the person on whom the harm is imposed is *liable* to bear that harm. You are liable to some defensive harm when you have forfeited your rights against the harm being imposed, and thus you are not wronged if the harm is imposed on you.<sup>1</sup> Liability to defensive harm is only one type of liability, however, and we must not assume that things true of liability to defensive harm are necessarily true for other types of liability. Other kinds of liability—for example, the liability to pay taxes, pay damages, or suffer punishment—may have very different normative bases. This article's focus is exclusively on moral liability to defensive harm.

Determining when a person is morally liable to defensive harm has important implications for the individual morality of self-defense and the principles of just conduct in war. First, because imposing harm on people who are liable to bear the harm does not wrong those people, it is much easier to justify imposing these sorts of harms than it is to justify

It is much more difficult to understand how Betty could permissibly kill an innocent bystander if this were the only way to save herself from Albert's attack; the innocent bystander has done nothing to make himself liable, and so Betty would be violating an innocent person's rights to save herself.<sup>2</sup> Second, whether a person is liable to defensive harm is crucial in determining whether that person may permissibly engage in counterdefense. If, for example, Albert makes himself liable to defensive harm by maliciously attacking Betty, then he is not entitled to engage in counterdefense when Betty defends herself from his initial attack. Third, determining who is liable to defensive harm has important implications regarding when third parties may intervene in a violent conflict. If a person is not liable to suffer some harm, but that harm is nonetheless imposed, then that person has at least a *prima facie* claim to compensation since the harm imposed was wrongful. Harms imposed on people who are liable to bear them do not appear to have the same capacity to ground claims of compensation.

What *makes* a person morally liable to defensive harm? More specifically, what must a person do to forfeit his right against the imposition of proportionate defensive harm?<sup>3</sup> I canvas three answers to this question, but I argue that all three answers are flawed.<sup>4</sup> Section I considers the *culpability account* of liability. On this view, a person only becomes liable to defensive harm when he is culpable for an unjust threat of harm against others. The culpability account suffers from two main problems. First, whatever interpretation we adopt, the culpability account yields a variety of counterintuitive results. Second, it is unclear why culpability should be the grounds for liability to defensive harm, given that culpability does not play a central role in other judgments about liability and rights forfeiture. In Section II, I assess Jeff McMahan's *moral responsibility account* of liability. McMahan argues, roughly, that a person can be liable whenever an unjust threat of harm arises as a result of his choices, provided this unjust threat was a reasonably foreseeable consequence. This account, however, yields counterintuitive results in certain cases where a person acts in a way that seems permissible based on the evidence, but through brute bad luck ends up posing a threat of harm to innocent others. Worries about this problem for the moral responsibility view might lead us to embrace an *evidence-relative account* of liability, whereby a person can only become liable when she is responsible for a threat of harm to innocent others as a result of action that is morally impermissible on the basis of the available evidence. In Section III, however, I argue that this view is also suspect because it yields counterintuitive results in certain cases where a person justifiably but mistakenly attacks an innocent person.

Before proceeding to the main arguments, some clarifications will be helpful. I will make use of Derek Parfit's distinction between three different standards of moral evaluation.<sup>5</sup> An act is wrong or impermissible (I will use these terms interchangeably) in the *fact-relative* sense when it would be wrong if we knew all the relevant facts. An act is wrong in the *belief-relative* sense if it would be wrong if our beliefs about the facts were true. Finally, an act is wrong in the *evidence-relative* sense when our act would be wrong if the relevant facts were what the available evidence gives us sufficient (or apparently sufficient) reason to believe they are. I will use the term *justified beliefs* to indicate that a person's beliefs are rational in light of the available evidence, that is, to indicate when a person believes what she has sufficient (or apparently sufficient) reason to believe.

To illustrate, suppose Betty believes that Albert is going to murder her in order to inherit her fortune, and that the only way to save her life is by killing Albert. If Betty acts on the basis of these beliefs, then we can say that her act is permissible in the belief-relative sense. If Betty's beliefs are also justified, then Betty's act is also permissible in the evidence-relative sense (but her act would be wrong in the evidence-relative sense if her beliefs were not justified). If Betty's beliefs are true, then her act of self-defense is also permissible in the fact-relative sense (but her act would be wrong in the fact-relative sense if it turned out she was mistaken and Albert posed no threat to her). Only in those cases where the different standards diverge will we need to make use of the distinctions above. Otherwise readers can assume that the three standards converge.

Finally, it is important to note that a person is never liable to defensive harm *simpliciter*: a person is always liable to a particular *proportionate* level of defensive harm. How the issue of proportionality ought to be determined is an important and difficult question, but I set it aside entirely in the discussion that follows. In this article, I focus only on the conditions for liability to defensive harm: the question of what a person must do in order to be liable to any degree of defensive harm.

## I. THE CULPABILITY ACCOUNT

Suppose Albert is about to murder Betty in order to inherit her fortune. Most people believe that Albert is now liable to defensive harm at the hands of Betty, or a third party who could come to Betty's defense, should this be necessary to save Betty. But notice that if Betty attacks Albert in self-defense, *she* does not thereby become liable to Albert's "defensive" harm: she does not forfeit any of her rights against harm by defending herself from Albert's initial attack. This asymmetry between Albert and Betty seems to show that what matters in determining liability to defensive harm is whether one person is threatening another person's rights. Because Albert's initial attack threatens to violate Betty's right not to be killed, Albert is now liable to defensive harm. Because Albert is now liable to defensive harm, he no longer possesses a right against having that harm imposed, and so when Betty attacks him in self-defense she does not threaten any of Albert's rights, and thus she does not become liable to defensive harm herself.

How do we know when someone who is threatening another person's rights forfeits his rights against the imposition of defensive harm? The *culpability account* of liability declares that only those persons who are responsible for the threat of impermissible harm against innocent persons, and who are also culpable for this threat, have forfeited rights against defensive harm.<sup>6</sup>

I will assume that an agent is *fully culpable* for a threat of harm when the following conditions are met:<sup>7</sup>

- (1) the agent acts in a way that results in a threat of impermissible harm to an innocent person (or persons);<sup>8</sup>
- (2) the agent intends or foresees this harm, or else is acting recklessly or negligently;<sup>9</sup>
- (3) there are no relevant excusing conditions (e.g., blameless ignorance, duress, or diminished responsibility).

If Albert's threat meets these conditions, then Albert is fully culpable for the harm he is threatening, and the culpability account declares him to be liable to defensive harm. Of course, it will often be the case that one or more excusing conditions are present, and when this is true the agent cannot be fully culpable, though he may be *partially* culpable depending on the nature and extent of the excusing conditions.<sup>10</sup> Suppose, for example, that Carl is a sadist and makes the following credible threat to Albert: unless he (Albert) cuts off Betty's legs, Carl will break Albert's legs. Albert really does not want his legs broken, and so he reluctantly proceeds to attack Betty. In this case Albert, though still culpable for his attack on Betty, is partially excused on account of duress: he is not *as* culpable as he would have been had he simply attacked Betty out of a malicious desire to see her suffer. On one version of the culpability view, the extent of a person's liability to defensive harm will vary in accordance with that person's culpability: fully culpable people are fully liable, whereas partially culpable people are only partially liable. I will call this the *variable culpability account*.

This account, however, generates counterintuitive results. Consider the example where Carl threatens to break Albert's legs unless Albert cuts off Betty's legs. The variable culpability account says that Albert is not fully culpable for his attack on Betty, and this means that his degree of liability to defensive harm is lower than it otherwise would be. Let us assume that if Albert were fully liable to Betty's defensive harm, he would be liable to be killed by her in self-defense if this were necessary for Betty to prevent his attack, and for simplicity let us give this level of harm a numerical score of 100. But since Albert is only partially culpable, let us suppose this means he is only liable to defensive harm up to the level of 70. Any harm imposed on Albert beyond 70 is harm to which he is not liable: harm beyond this point is equivalent to harm imposed on an innocent person. But now suppose that the only way Betty can save herself from Albert's attack is by killing him: by imposing a defensive harm of 100. The variable culpability account tells us that Betty cannot save herself without infringing or violating Albert's rights since he has only forfeited his right against the imposition of defensive harm up to the level of 70.<sup>11</sup> This conclusion is very difficult to accept. Even if Albert is partially excused for his attack on Betty, his attack is still fully wrongful: he is only partially *excused*, not partially *justified*, in acting as he does. Moreover, we can stipulate that Albert is aware of the wrongfulness of his act. I do not think it is plausible to suppose that Albert can intentionally decide to cut off Betty's legs, knowing this to be wrong, and yet not be liable to be killed by Betty if this is the only way she can save herself from his attack. Betty would not, I submit, in any way *wrong* Albert if she were to kill him under these circumstances, but the variable culpability account does not appear able to reach this conclusion.

To avoid these kinds of counterintuitive results, perhaps we should instead say that whenever a person meets some minimal threshold of culpability for a threat of wrongful harm, the person can be fully liable to defensive harm regardless of the person's degree of culpability. Let us call this the *threshold culpability account*. This account, however, will also generate counterintuitive results. If the culpability threshold is set very low, then this account will declare that a fully culpable murderer is liable to exactly the same extent as someone who kills only under extreme duress. Although I do not believe this conclusion is necessarily a mistake, I doubt there is a plausible explanation that a proponent of the threshold culpability account can offer that would explain both why culpability is the primary basis for liability to defensive harm, and why radical differences in degrees of culpability are completely irrelevant to liability. Why should culpability only matter up until some very low threshold?

But if we set the culpability threshold higher in order to avoid this problem, a different problem emerges: we will end up treating two moderately culpable people who differ only slightly in culpability very differently. Consider:

*Delia's Risky Behavior:* Delia wants to get a closer look at what is going on behind a construction site wall. There are large signs saying, "Do Not Enter: dangerous work being done—unauthorized entry may endanger the construction workers within." Delia sees the sign but decides to ignore its warning because she is overcome by curiosity and really wants to know what is going on inside. She enters the site and triggers a rockslide that threatens to seriously harm several workers below.

*Eric's Risky Behavior:* Eric is out for a walk, absorbed in his own thoughts. He knows there is a construction site nearby with large signs saying, "Do Not Enter: dangerous work being done—unauthorized entry may endanger the construction workers within," but he negligently fails to pay attention to where he is walking and enters the site. His entry triggers a rockslide that now threatens to seriously harm several workers below.

Both people are culpable, but it seems right to assume that Delia's behavior is more culpable than Eric's. Delia deliberately imposes an unjustified risk on others to satisfy her own curiosity, whereas Eric is merely negligent. Yet it seems difficult to believe that Eric is not liable to roughly the same level of defensive harm as Delia: they both acted in ways that are wrongful and both were given a fair opportunity to avoid acting in this wrongful way. But if Eric falls below the culpability threshold and Delia does not, then the threshold culpability view will yield the bizarre result that Delia is fully liable to defensive harm whereas Eric is not liable at all. Wherever we choose to set the threshold, it will be possible to construct a pair of cases that yield this sort of counterintuitive result.<sup>12</sup>

Finally, there are cases that pose a problem for any version of the culpability account: cases where a person is not culpable at all, and yet the person in question appears liable to at least a certain level of defensive harm. Consider:

*Mistaken Attacker:*<sup>13</sup> The identical twin brother of a notorious mass murderer is driving during a stormy night in a remote area when his car breaks down. Unaware that his brother has recently escaped from prison and is known to be hiding in this same area, he knocks on the door of the nearest house, seeking to phone for help. On opening the door, Resident justifiably believes the harmless twin is the murderer and lunges at him with a knife (Resident has been warned by the authorities that the mass murderer will almost certainly attack anyone he meets on sight).

Because Resident acts on the basis of justified beliefs, his act of “self-defense” is permissible in the evidence-relative sense. I therefore think he cannot be culpable (he has a full epistemic excuse), and so the culpability account declares that he is not liable to any harm the identical twin might impose in self-defense. This conclusion, however, does not seem right. After all, the identical twin is the victim of an unprovoked (in the fact-relative sense) and intentional attack by Resident. Surely the person who intentionally initiates the fact-relative wrongful attack—Resident—is liable to bear a certain level of defensive harm?

I have pointed to various cases where the culpability account generally, or a particular version of this view, yields what I believe are counterintuitive results. Of course, it is open to the proponent of the culpability account to bite the respective bullets in the face of each objection: to insist that whether or not the result is counterintuitive, it is a result we ought to accept because the culpability account is the best account of liability to defensive harm. But the plausibility of this reply depends on the view that culpability is otherwise (apart from the counterintuitive results) a plausible basis for determining liability. It is not clear, however, that it is a plausible basis given that culpability is so closely tied to, if not synonymous with, being *blameworthy*. Blame and moral entitlements, however, come apart in many contexts. Individuals can be blameworthy for acting on bad motives, but so long as they do not violate anyone's rights, their moral rights are unaffected. And conversely, people can often act in ways that are not blameworthy (because their actions are justified or excused), but nevertheless they can sometimes be held substantively responsible—that is, can be required at the bar of justice to pay costs or suffer damages—for the consequences of those blameless actions.<sup>14</sup> Given the lack of a clear rationale for tying liability to defensive harm so closely to being blameworthy, and given the counterintuitive results that the culpability account generates, I conclude we ought to look elsewhere for the basis of liability to defensive harm.

## II. THE MORAL RESPONSIBILITY ACCOUNT

In light of the problems with the culpability account, maybe what we need is a thinner basis on which to rest our account of liability, one that does not come with all the evaluative baggage of culpability. The *moral responsibility account* developed by Jeff McMahan seems to fit the bill. According to McMahan, a person can become liable to defensive harm if that person is morally responsible for an unjust threat of harm to others.<sup>15</sup> On this view if (a) a person performs an action that it is reasonable to foresee might result in a threat of harm to others, and (b) the reasonably foreseeable threat of harm to others eventuates and is fact-relative impermissible, then (c) the person is liable to defensive harm.<sup>16</sup> Moral responsibility on McMahan's account is thus a fairly thin concept. It does not entail responsibility in the sense of being an appropriate subject of praise, blame, or other reactive attitudes. Rather, McMahan is using the term to describe all those situations where the consequences of a decision can be traced to a person's voluntary agency because those consequences were reasonably foreseeable by the agent who made the choice.

The moral responsibility account has a number of further features. First, it allows that a person's moral responsibility can be diminished by excusing conditions such as blameless ignorance, duress, or diminished responsibility.<sup>17</sup> When a person's moral responsibility for an unjust threat is partially but not fully diminished, this person is liable to a lesser degree than he would be if he were fully responsible for the unjust threat. So the moral responsibility account, like the variable culpability account, allows for degrees of liability, and for that reason will be vulnerable to similar objections pressed against the variable culpability account in the previous section.<sup>18</sup>

But unlike any version of the culpability account, the moral responsibility view allows that individuals can be liable to defensive harm even when they are not culpable. For example, the moral responsibility view can explain why Resident in Mistaken Attacker is liable to defensive harm. Even though Resident has an epistemic excuse that is sufficient to render him entirely nonculpable, he still made the choice to attack another person, and a reasonably foreseeable consequence of attacking another person whom one believes to be liable is that one might be mistaken. The more general idea is that when we engage in actions that we can reasonably foresee impose a risk of harm to innocent people, we can be liable to defensive harm if that risk eventuates. But if we cannot reasonably foresee that our actions pose any risk of harm to innocent people, then we cannot be liable should any unforeseeable harm result from our actions. If, for example, Albert's flipping of a light switch in his home starts a chain of unforeseeable events that causes Betty (in another house) to suffer a severe electric shock, the harm Betty suffers cannot be attributed to Albert's voluntary agency since he could not foresee it would result from his actions, and thus he cannot be liable to any defensive harm.<sup>19</sup>

At the heart of the moral responsibility account is an appeal to *distributive fairness*. If Albert engages in some action or activity that he knows or can reasonably foresee might result in harm to innocent others, and if that risk of harm eventuates and there is now harm to be distributed, it is only *fair* that Albert should be the person who bears that harm. After all, why should anyone other than Albert have to bear the harm that Albert's own decisions have created? Here we find the familiar and powerful luck egalitarian intuition that individuals ought to bear the costs for their own choices, but should not be held liable for the costs of brute luck or the responsible choices of others.

Though this idea has a great deal of appeal, it also generates odd results in certain cases.<sup>20</sup> Consider:

*Conscientious Driver*: Driver, who always keeps his car well maintained and always drives carefully and alertly, decides to drive to the cinema. On the way, a freak accident occurs that causes his car to veer out of control in the direction of a pedestrian. The out-of-control car will now kill the pedestrian unless the pedestrian destroys the car with a grenade, thereby killing Driver.

Driver is liable to defensive harm on McMahan's view because Driver knows (or can reasonably foresee) that there is always a small risk of one's car veering out of control when one chooses to drive. Driver is thus morally responsible when bad luck strikes and his car veers toward the pedestrian. In Dworkin's terms, Driver suffers bad option luck, not bad brute luck.<sup>21</sup> If the only way for the pedestrian to save her life is to destroy the car with a grenade and thereby kill Driver, Driver is liable to this harm since Driver is morally responsible for the unjust threat of harm to the pedestrian. But this conclusion—that Driver has forfeited his rights against the imposition of defensive harm—strikes many people as counterintuitive. After all, Driver has acted with all due care and precaution, and is engaged in an activity that is generally permitted. *Ambulance Driver:* An Emergency Medical Technician (EMT) is driving an ambulance to the site of an accident to take one of the victims to the hospital. She is driving conscientiously and alertly, but a freak event occurs that causes the ambulance to veer uncontrollably toward a pedestrian. The out-of-control ambulance will now kill the pedestrian unless the pedestrian destroys it with a grenade, thereby killing the EMT (the victim whom the EMT was going to pick up will be fine).

Here the EMT is not merely engaged in a generally permitted activity; she has a positive (and powerful) moral reason to be driving. I assume that many readers will find the conclusion that the EMT is nevertheless liable to defensive harm to be deeply counterintuitive.

McMahan is aware that many people may find these conclusions counterintuitive, yet he seems to believe that this is a price worth paying: that the alternative solutions to these cases are even more problematic.<sup>22</sup> In particular, McMahan points out that it seems intuitively permissible for the pedestrians in these examples to defend themselves. McMahan believes this intuition is sound, and that it indicates that Driver and the EMT are liable to defensive harm. This argument can be stated as follows:

- (1) In both Conscientious Driver and Ambulance Driver, it is permissible for the pedestrians to impose harm on Driver or the EMT in self-defense.
- (2) It is not permissible to save one innocent person from harm if doing so involves imposing a harm of equal or greater magnitude on another innocent (i.e., nonliable) person.
- (3) Therefore, Driver and the EMT must be liable to the defensive harm the pedestrians can permissibly impose.

The second premise in this argument reflects the important and widely accepted moral distinction between doing harm versus allowing harm. If we accept this premise and the further intuitive premise that the innocent pedestrians in these examples are permitted to impose harm in self-defense if necessary, then it seems hard to avoid McMahan's conclusion that Driver and the EMT are liable to defensive harm.

I believe, however, that we should resist the conclusion by rejecting (or rather modifying) the second premise. Most people do not believe it is *always* impermissible to impose harm on one innocent person to avoid an equivalent level of harm befalling another innocent person. Most people believe, for example, that it is permissible to kill a *Nonresponsible Threat* who has been unexpectedly thrown by a gust of wind toward the bottom of a well where you are trapped if this is the only way to save yourself from being killed by the Nonresponsible Threat's falling body. Similarly, I believe it would be permissible to redirect a runaway trolley away from one's child or spouse onto a sidetrack, even if this will foreseeably kill an innocent person trapped on that sidetrack.

I believe these judgments indicate the existence of an *agent-relative prerogative* that permits us, under certain conditions, to override the general presumption against doing harm in order to avoid allowing harm. Although I cannot defend this claim here,<sup>23</sup> it is plausible and coheres with many people's intuitive judgments in a range of cases. If this claim is correct, then we do not need to believe that Driver or the EMT in our two examples must be liable in order to believe that the pedestrians are morally permitted to impose defensive harm on them. It is more plausible to see these cases as instances of symmetrical defensive harm, where two innocent people are tragically locked in a lethal conflict, and where each one has an agent-relative permission of self-defense. If the pedestrians in these cases impose harm in self-defense, this is morally permissible, but does *infringe* the rights not to be harmed or killed possessed by both Driver and the EMT. Harming or killing Driver or the EMT ought to be seen as entirely different (not merely different in degree) from harming or killing a culpable murderer, yet the moral responsibility account has difficulty drawing this obvious moral distinction.<sup>24</sup> Drawing this distinction has dramatic practical implications, since it determines what third parties might permissibly do in such cases. On McMahan's view, a third party who observes the events in Conscientious Driver may permissibly intervene and kill Driver (who is liable on McMahan's view) to save the nonliable pedestrian, but cannot permissibly kill the pedestrian in order to save Driver. I believe, however, that because Driver is not liable it would not be permissible for a third party (with no special relationship to either person) to intervene in the conflict: there is no reason for the third party to treat Driver any differently than the pedestrian, and since both are nonliable, the doctrine of doing and allowing prohibits killing one innocent stranger to save another.

23. I defend and develop this view in Jonathan Quong, "Killing in Self-Defense," *Ethics* 119 (2009): 507–37.

{238}------------------------------------------------

In sum, although the moral responsibility account is a coherent position, I think it is an unsatisfying one. It is incredible to suppose that, in cases like Conscientious Driver and Ambulance Driver, Driver or the EMT have forfeited their rights against defensive harm and may be liable to be killed if necessary. Moreover, we do not need to accept the moral responsibility account in order to believe that the pedestrians in these examples are permitted to defend themselves: we only need to believe that sometimes individuals have an agent-relative permission to infringe or override the rights of innocent people in order to save themselves from severe harm or death.<sup>25</sup>

## III. THE EVIDENCE-RELATIVE ACCOUNT

The moral responsibility account is implausible partly because it says that in certain cases people, like Driver or the EMT, who act with evidence-relative permission can nevertheless be liable to defensive harm. An obvious solution would be to adopt an *evidence-relative account* of liability.<sup>26</sup> On this view, a person can only become liable to defensive harm when he acts in a way that:

- (1) meets the minimum conditions of voluntariness (or moral responsibility in McMahan's sense);
- (2) is evidence-relative impermissible; and

The evidence-relative account differs from the culpability account because it is insensitive to excusing conditions other than full epistemic excuses. That is, even if Albert is partially excused for his attack on Betty for reasons of duress or diminished responsibility, so long as his attack meets the three conditions above, he is liable to the proportionate level of defensive harm that his threat warrants. What matters is voluntarily performing an evidence-relative impermissible act, not one's degree of culpability for that action. In this way the evidence-relative account avoids some of the objections that plagued the culpability view. It also avoids the counterintuitive implications of the moral responsibility view; neither the driver in Conscientious Driver or the EMT in Ambulance Driver is liable on the evidence-relative account since both act in ways that are morally permissible given the evidence available to them. Furthermore, it reflects the commonsense intuition that it seems somehow wrong or unfair to hold people liable when they did exactly what any rational person would have believed was permissible given the available evidence.

However, the evidence-relative view still faces one of the same key difficulties as the culpability account. Consider a slightly different version of Mistaken Attacker:

*Police Intervention:* The identical twin brother of a notorious mass murderer is driving during a stormy night in a remote area when his car breaks down. Unaware that his brother has recently escaped from prison and is known to be hiding in this same area, he knocks on the door of the nearest house, seeking to phone for help. On opening the door, Resident justifiably believes the harmless twin is the murderer and lunges at him with a knife (Resident has been warned by the authorities that the mass murderer is almost certain to attack anyone he meets on sight). Just at that moment, a police officer arrives on the scene who *knows* the person being attacked by Resident is the murderer's innocent twin. The police officer realizes the mistake that Resident has made, and now sees that the only way to save the twin from being killed is to shoot and kill Resident first. Alternatively, the police officer could let Resident kill the twin.

I believe it is clear that the police officer is morally permitted to shoot Resident in order to save the twin. My intuition about this case is firm, but suppose that proponents of the evidence-relative account are unmoved: they insist that Resident is not liable to defensive harm, and consequently the police officer is not permitted to intervene. Maybe this bullet-biting gains some plausibility from the fact that the police officer must either kill Resident or allow Resident to kill the twin. But suppose the police officer has the following two choices, both of which will end the conflict: impose a moderate level of harm on the twin, *or* impose a slightly more serious (but nonfatal) level of harm on Resident. I think it is clear that the police officer ought to impose the slightly more serious harm on Resident, but this intuition cannot be justified unless Resident bears some liability: otherwise, the officer ought to impose the lesser harm.

Now consider the following case:

*Duped Soldiers:* A group of young soldiers are successfully fooled by a totalitarian regime into believing that the regime is good and just, and is under repeated attacks from their evil neighbors, the Gloops. The regime's misinformation campaign is subtle and absolutely convincing: the soldiers are justified in believing what they are told by the regime. Once the misinformation campaign is complete, these Duped Soldiers are given orders to attack and destroy a Gloop village on the border which, they are told, is really a Gloop terrorist camp plotting a major attack. In fact, everything the regime has said is a lie, and the Gloop village contains only innocent civilians. The Duped Soldiers prepare to shell the village and are about to (unknowingly) kill all the innocent civilians in it. It is deeply implausible to hold, as the evidence-relative account must do, both that the Duped Soldiers and the Gloops are equally nonliable to attack, and consequently the neutral peacekeeping force has no reason to treat an attack on the soldiers any differently than the attack on the Gloops. If, for example, the number of soldiers and the number of Gloops are equivalent, then the distinction between doing and allowing, in conjunction with the evidence-relative view of liability, requires that the peacekeepers allow the Duped Soldiers to kill the Gloops, since it is not permissible to kill nonliable people in order to save an equivalent number of nonliable people (absent some agent-relative prerogative or other special consideration). The evidence-relative account gets the wrong answer in this case, and I think this provides us with strong reasons to reject it.<sup>27</sup>

The two examples in this section show that individuals who are not culpable on grounds of blameless ignorance can nevertheless sometimes be liable to defensive harm, but this is inconsistent with the evidence-relative account.

## IV. THE MORAL STATUS ACCOUNT

When is it fair to hold someone liable to defensive harm for the threat of harm arising from his or her actions? The moral responsibility account tells us it is fair to do so whenever the threat of harm is reasonably foreseeable. But this answer, I have suggested, is too broad. In cases like Conscientious Driver or Ambulance Driver, it does not seem fair to hold Driver or the EMT liable for the harms that result from their choices, even though the risk these harms might eventuate was reasonably foreseeable. But what is the underlying rationale for this reaction? I think the explanation is this: whether we are liable to defensive harm for the harm our action may impose on innocent people depends on whether that latter harm has already been counted in the correct way when determining the evidence-relative permissibility of our action.

27. I am here following McMahan in assuming that the liability to defensive harm of individual soldiers can be determined in broadly the same manner as the liability of individuals in nonwartime scenarios. Consider the decision to engage in prudent driving (i.e., driving in accordance with the fairly designed rules of the road). It is foreseeable that even if everyone observes the rules, a certain number of accidental fatalities will occur every year. But, even though we know that the activity will result in a certain number of innocent people being killed each year, it is widely believed that the practice of prudent driving is morally permissible. Let us assume this moral judgment is correct: let us assume that the general practice of prudent driving is morally permissible because the risk of harm arising from accidents is foreseen but not intended, it does not fall disproportionately on any particular person or group, and the risk of harm is acceptable in light of the benefits everyone derives. Moreover, in Western industrialized societies like our own, almost everyone benefits from the social practice of prudent driving, and it is difficult to say that any given person benefits much more (or less) than others. Even those people who do not own cars derive significant benefits from the practice of prudent driving: they rely on others (family, friends, and bus drivers) to drive them places, they receive goods and services that are delivered by trucks, and they derive major benefits from local and global economies heavily reliant on the practice of driving.<sup>28</sup>

If these assumptions are true, then when any particular instance of prudent driving results in unintended harm to innocent people, this cost has already been counted in our judgment about the evidence-relative permissibility of that particular instance of driving. If (a) the best moral theory has already determined that permitting the practice of prudent driving is permissible *despite* the harms that will result to innocent people,<sup>29</sup> and (b) there is not much reason to believe that anyone who happens to be driving at a particular moment derives greater benefits from the general practice of driving than someone who happens to be a pedestrian at that same point in time, then I believe (c) it is unfair to hold individual prudent drivers liable to defensive harm for the particular harms that result from their driving.

Paradigmatic cases of liability to defensive harm, however, are different. In these cases, the person who is liable to defensive harm, A, acts in a way that foreseeably might result in harm to some other person (or group), B, and the risk-imposing action that A performs would not be evidence-relative permissible unless those who might be harmed are liable to bear the harm. Consider the paradigm case of liability to defensive harm: A intends to kill B and attacks B with lethal force. A's action is evidence-relative impermissible *unless* B is liable to bear the lethal harm that A is about to impose. If this is not the case—if B is not liable to lethal harm—then A is liable to defensive harm. Or consider a case where A recklessly drives too fast in order to get to the movies, thereby imposing a risk of harm on pedestrian B. Reckless driving is evidence-relative impermissible precisely because it imposes an unacceptable risk of harm on people who are not liable to bear harm. Because A's act of reckless driving could only be evidence-relative permissible if we assumed that B was liable to the harm, A ought to be liable to defensive harm if his reckless driving turns out to pose a threat to nonliable B.

Put another way, these cases involve one person (the agent who imposes harm) treating another (the person on whom harm is imposed) either *as if* the latter lacks certain important rights that all persons are generally assumed to possess, or else *as if* those rights or claims do not have the stringency or moral weight that they do in fact possess. My proposal is that when one person, A, behaves in this way with regard to another person, B, A assumes a special kind of responsibility for harms that B might suffer. By that, I mean that A acts in a way that she knows (or reasonably ought to know) would impose impermissible harm *but for* the assumption that B lacks claims against the harm, and this means A ought to be liable to defensive harm should this assumption be mistaken.<sup>30</sup>

30. There is an important moral difference between engaging in some action that carries some risk of harm to innocent others but which the correct moral theory declares to be evidence-relative permissible given the balance of costs and benefits, and acting in a way that would not be evidence-relative permissible unless we assume that certain others lack rights that all people normally possess. In the case of prudent driving, we could say that the risk-imposing agent accords each person the concern and respect they are owed as individuals who possess rights against harm. The fact that he imposes a certain risk of harm against others is consistent with everyone's moral status, since the risk of harm is (we are assuming) justifiable to all persons in light of the balance of costs and benefits of the practice of prudent driving.<sup>31</sup> But in paradigmatic cases of liability to defensive harm, things are different. The risk-imposing agent does not treat everyone with the concern and respect they are normally due: she acts as if some people are liable to harm and thus have fewer rights (or less stringent rights) or moral claims relative to others. Since the risk-imposing agent has made this choice, it does not seem unfair to say that she is liable to some defensive harm should her assumption about the liability of others turn out to be mistaken. This rationale appeals to an idea of *reciprocity*: that our standing to press claims against others depends in part on how we treat those others.<sup>32</sup> If I treat you with the concern and respect that you are owed, as determined by the best moral theory, then you owe me nothing less. However, if I treat you as if you lack some rights, then I have forfeited some of the claims I would otherwise have against you should my judgment about your rights turn out to be mistaken.

One objection to my proposal is that it is unfair to those who act with evidence-relative permissibility, but whose actions turn out to threaten fact-relative impermissible harms. If A does what she justifiably believes to be permissible given her epistemic situation, why should she be liable to defensive harm should the facts turn out to be different than the evidence gave her reason to believe? Surely this imposes an unfairly demanding standard of conduct on A: she will be liable for the consequences of her actions even when the evidence available to her gave her no grounds to believe she was acting wrongly, indeed when the available evidence may indicate there is no reasonable alternative other than using lethal force (as it appears to Resident in Mistaken Attacker).

Although this objection might seem tempting, I think it should be resisted. It would indeed be unfair to blame A, or criticize her, if her actions were evidence-relative permissible. Such negative reactive attitudes are only appropriate when someone has acted in a way she could have known was wrongful. But liability to defensive harm is not a reactive attitude. Unlike negative reactive attitudes, a person can be liable for the consequences of her actions even when she is not blameworthy or subject to criticism. Even when we do not act wrongly in the evidence-relative sense, we sometimes gamble with the moral rights of others in the sense described above: we act in ways that we know (or ought to know) would be wrong but for the assumption that certain others lack important rights. Though it does not make sense to blame us when we act in these ways, it does make sense to say that we may forfeit some of our rights when we take these sorts of risks.<sup>33</sup>

The critic who insists that what matters for liability is only whether the agent acted with evidence-relative permission fails to notice that not all evidence-relative permissible actions are alike. Some actions are evidence-relative permissible because the best moral theory tells us that the action is permissible even though it imposes a risk of harm on innocent people. In these cases, each person's claims or interests have been taken fairly into account in determining that the action is evidence-relative permissible, and consequently performing the action is consistent with showing appropriate regard for each person's actual moral status, a status that includes a certain degree of inviolability. Other actions are evidence-relative permissible, however, only because the evidence gives us reason (or apparent reason) to believe that some person has lost his standing to press claims against us, or at least that his claims have less weight. In these cases if we proceed with the risk-imposing activity, we treat those whom we might harm differently than we do in the former cases: those individuals are not accorded the same inviolable status that innocent persons are normally accorded. I believe this difference in treatment makes an important moral difference for liability.<sup>34</sup>

Liability to defensive harm should depend on whether, in imposing a threat of harm on others, we treat those others as having fewer rights, or less stringent rights, than people normally possess. When we take such a risk, it is only fair that we—as opposed to the innocent person who now faces a threat of harm—may be liable to defensive harm when our judgment about the moral status of others turns out to be mistaken.<sup>35</sup>

Second, I believe the moral idea underpinning the moral status account is independently plausible. To treat others as if they lack moral rights against having harm imposed is a grave matter, and so it is plausible to suppose that when we act in this way, we must accept a certain substantive responsibility for our actions. If we treat others as if they are liable to harm, it seems only fair to suppose that we may become liable to defensive harm should that judgment be mistaken. But when our risk-imposing actions do not treat anyone else as lacking moral rights against harm—when we have an independent moral justification for the evidence-relative permission to impose harm or risk of harm on others that does not depend on this assumption—then the moral picture seems very different. Liability to defensive harm, on this view, is grounded in a particular conception of what it means to treat others with the concern and respect they are due. When we treat others in ways that are consistent with their actual moral status (as determined by the best moral theory), it would be unfair and wrong to hold us liable to defensive harm if our actions unfortunately threaten harm against innocent others. It is only those cases where we treat certain others as if they have a lesser claim to rights against harm than they in fact possess where we can be liable to defensive harm for our actions.

Thus, even though Bomber is threatening the civilians' rights, he does not treat them as having anything less than the appropriate moral status we accord to persons, and so he remains nonliable. The moral status conception of liability can explain this conclusion, since on this view what matters is whether the evidence-relative permissibility of Frank's action depends on the assumption that Greta is liable to the harm that Frank intends to impose. Because Frank acts *as if* Greta lacks rights against the harm he threatens to impose, Frank cannot avoid liability to defensive harm when, despite the fact that Greta is not liable to his attack, it fortuitously turns out that his action is all things considered permissible in the fact-relative sense. But, unlike the evidence-relative account of liability, the moral status view does not implausibly declare that a person who acts with evidence-relative permission can *never* be liable to defensive harm. Instead, the moral status conception declares that what matters is whether a person, judged from the evidence-relative standpoint, acts as if others lack rights against harm. When, and only when, our actions have this particular feature—treating others with something less than the concern and respect they are due—do we make ourselves liable to defensive harm.

42. See McMahan, "The Basis of Moral Liability," pp. 399–400; McMahan, *Killing in War*, p. 43. It is not clear whether McMahan would in fact endorse the view described above, since he says only that fact-relative permissibility is *necessary* to defeat liability, while remaining neutral on whether it is *sufficient* to do so.

## V. FURTHER OBJECTIONS

The moral status conception of liability, recall, can be stated as follows:

A person is liable to defensive harm for choosing to do X when that choice results in a threat of harm to innocent people if and only if: (a) choosing X meets the minimum conditions of moral responsibility, and (b) the evidence-relative permissibility of choosing X depends either on the assumption that those who are harmed (or might foreseeably be harmed) by choosing X are liable to the harm, or else on false moral beliefs.

In this section, I confront three further objections that might be pressed against this view.

### A. *Culpable versus Responsible Threats*

Like McMahan's responsibility view, the moral status account holds that people—like Resident in Mistaken Attacker—who are merely responsible but not culpable can be liable to defensive harm. But this, a critic might object, means that the moral status account fails to correctly distinguish between culpable and merely responsible threats. The view assumes that the moral status of Resident is the same as that of a similarly situated culpable attacker, but this seems false. Surely Resident retains some rights that a culpable attacker lacks? After all, Resident does not seem to act *as wrongfully* as a culpable attacker, and so should he not be liable to a lesser degree than a culpable attacker? In support of this objection, the critic will claim that, other things being equal, it seems intuitively permissible to inflict a greater degree of defensive harm on a culpable threat as opposed to a merely responsible threat. If this intuition is sound, does this not support some version of the culpability account when compared to the moral status view?

{252}------------------------------------------------

There are three replies to this objection that, taken together, are decisive. First, the moral status account is not as susceptible to this line of objection as McMahan's responsibility account since it only holds responsible but nonculpable threats to be liable in certain contexts such as mistaken acts of self- or other-defense, or mistaken attacks in war. It does not hold people like Driver in Conscientious Driver to be liable, and so it avoids the most obviously counterintuitive problems that the responsibility account faces.

Second, the moral status view could allow culpability to play a role in determining what counts as *proportionate* harm in cases of defensive violence, even though it denies that culpability is a necessary feature for liability. That is, once the moral status account has deemed a person liable to defensive harm, that person's degree of culpability might then play a role in determining what level of harm would be proportionate to impose. Although I wish to remain neutral regarding the way in which judgments of proportionality are determined, nothing in the moral status account precludes culpability from playing this role.<sup>43</sup>

43. Note, however, that if we allow culpability to play this role in determining proportionality, we may encounter some of the same objections that plagued the culpability account of liability. Whether these objections do apply will depend on the extent to which culpability is allowed to determine proportionality. If culpability only affects proportionality under limited conditions—for example, when other things are equal and we ask how great a burden the defensive agent must bear to avoid imposing defensive harm—the objections pressed against the variable culpability account might not apply to the proposal here. Either way, the proposal here does not make culpability the primary basis for liability, and thus some of the objections pressed against the culpability account will clearly not apply.

{253}------------------------------------------------

Third, the moral status view can also account for the intuitive difference between culpable and nonculpable threats by appeal to moral desert.<sup>44</sup> Here is how the argument goes:

1. There are impersonal reasons (i.e., reasons unconnected to individual rights) to avoid imposing harm, or allowing harm to be imposed, on people who do not deserve to suffer harm.
2. People who are not blameworthy for any serious wrongdoing do not deserve to suffer harm, but the same is not true of people who are blameworthy for serious wrongdoing.
3. Therefore, there are impersonal reasons (i.e., apart from considerations of liability) to avoid imposing harm on responsible but non-culpable threats that do not apply in the case of culpable threats.

This argument can explain the intuitive belief that, other things being equal, it is permissible to impose a greater degree of defensive harm on a culpable threat as opposed to a nonculpable threat without requiring that we revise our views regarding the respective liability of these threats. Both threats can be, as the moral status view insists, liable to defensive harm. But we have additional impersonal reasons to avoid imposing harms on the nonculpable threat because he is innocent of any blame, reasons not present in the case of culpable threats.

### *B. Nonresponsible Threats*

What conclusions does the moral status account yield with regard to cases involving a *Nonresponsible Threat*? These cases involve one person posing a threat to another, but the person who poses the threat has exercised no agency whatsoever (e.g., an unforeseeable gust of wind picks up the Threat and renders him into a lethal projectile that will kill someone below). Clearly, the moral status view says that the Threat is not liable to defensive harm since he exercises no agency and thus cannot be morally responsible. But does this mean that if Betty is about to be killed by a Threat, she is not permitted to shoot and kill the Threat in self-defense should this be necessary? Elsewhere I have argued the answer is no.<sup>45</sup> I argue that we each possess an agent-relative prerogative to harm or even kill nonliable people in defense of our own life under certain limited conditions, and consequently Betty is morally permitted to kill the Threat, though if she does so, she will be infringing the rights of an innocent person.

44. This line of response can also be deployed in defense of McMahan's responsibility account. But this argument cannot help the responsibility account avoid the objection to which I claim it is vulnerable, namely, its failure to distinguish between cases like Conscientious Driver as opposed to cases like Duped Soldiers.

{254}------------------------------------------------

This raises a different question: what does the moral status conception tell us about *Betty's* liability? If she attacks the Threat in self-defense, does she treat the Threat as if he lacks rights against harm, and thereby make herself liable to defensive harm? Here too the answer is no. Although Betty intentionally threatens harm against an innocent person, the moral permission for her action does not depend on the assumption that the Threat is liable to this harm. Rather, Betty's action is justified by appeal to an agent-relative prerogative, along with a particular conception of what it means to use someone as means to saving oneself or others from harm.<sup>46</sup> Betty does not treat the Threat as if he lacks rights against harm, because her action is morally permissible *even though* the Threat is nonliable, and thus she does not make herself liable to any defensive harm. Cases like this are thus cases of symmetrical self-defense, like *Justified Bomber*, where neither party is liable to defensive harm, but each party has an agent-relative permission to use defensive force against the threat posed by the other. On this view, it is also true that since neither party is liable to defensive harm, it would be impermissible for third parties (absent some agent-relative permission) to intervene on behalf of either person by killing the other, since in doing so the third party would be—*contra* the doctrine of doing and allowing—imposing harm on an innocent person to avert an equivalent harm befalling another innocent person. I believe this offers the right account of cases involving Nonresponsible Threats, but a full defense of this conclusion is not possible here.

### C. *Treating Mistakes about Rights Differently*

Consider the following example:

*Mistaken Bomber*: Bomber acting in a just war is about to drop a bomb on an enemy target. According to the best evidence available to Bomber, the bombing is morally permissible: the enemy target is important to the war effort, and only a proportionate number of innocent civilians (ten) will be killed as a foreseen but unintended consequence of the bombing. Unfortunately, Bomber's beliefs, although justified, are mistaken. The bomb will actually kill two hundred civilians below, though this fact is utterly unforeseeable for Bomber. The bombing is thus not fact-relative proportionate according to the correct principles of *jus in bello*. The civilians have an antiaircraft gun which they could use to shoot down and kill Bomber before he is able to drop the bomb.

45. See Quong, "Killing in Self-Defense."

46. *Ibid.*

{255}------------------------------------------------

In this example, Bomber is not liable to defensive harm according to the moral status conception of liability. This is because the evidence-relative permissibility of Bomber's action does not depend on the assumption that the civilians below are liable to the harm that Bomber will impose. From Bomber's point of view, he is morally permitted to drop the bomb *even though* doing so will foreseeably kill ten nonliable people below. Because, from his point of view, he does not act as if the civilians below lack rights against harm, the moral status view declares he is not liable to the defensive harm those civilians might impose when he turns out to be mistaken about the number of civilians below.

Some may object that this result is counterintuitive. Surely Bomber ought to be liable to defensive harm given that he intentionally drops a bomb on civilians below, and that this act is also fact-relative impermissible? I believe, however, that this response confuses our intuitive reactions regarding two distinct questions. One question is whether Bomber is liable to defensive harm, that is, whether he has acted in a way that results in the forfeit of his rights against the imposition of defensive harm. But the other question is this: are the civilians or neutral third parties morally permitted to shoot down Bomber to save the civilians? The answer to the latter question is yes, but we do not need to believe that Bomber is liable in order to reach this conclusion. First, I believe the civilians have an agent-relative prerogative to defend themselves against Bomber. This would be true even if Bomber were acting with fact-relative permission, that is, even if his bombing were proportionate. Second, given that Bomber's action is fact-relative disproportionate, the civilians or neutral third parties would be morally permitted to shoot Bomber by appeal to a "lesser evil" justification (i.e., it is sometimes permissible to impose harms on a nonliable person or group in order to avert a much greater harm to innocent persons). So there are two independent and sufficient explanations of why it is permissible to shoot down Bomber that do not appeal to Bomber's liability. It is thus no longer clear why we ought to believe that Bomber is liable to defensive harm. We do not need this conclusion in order to believe that it is permissible to shoot down Bomber, and this conclusion is also inconsistent with the moral status view of liability, a view that I have argued is independently appealing and explains our judgments in a range of other cases.

{256}------------------------------------------------

Some may continue to object. In particular, some may believe there is no relevant normative difference between a case like Mistaken Bomber and a case like Duped Soldiers. In both cases, the agent who imposes the harm (Bomber or the Duped Soldiers) has justified beliefs that turn out to be mistaken. Bomber is mistaken about the *number* of civilians below, whereas the Duped Soldiers are mistaken about the *liability* of the Gloops whom they are about to attack. But, the critic protests, why should this difference matter? Why should mistakes about the moral rights of others carry special significance? In answer to this question, I can only repeat what has already been said in defense of the moral status view. Making a justified mistake about whether one's actions will result in harm to anyone, or to a disproportionate number of people, need not involve treating others with anything less than the concern and respect they are due: it need not involve treating others as if they lack important rights against harm. But making a mistake about other people's liability to harm is different. When you assume that others are liable to bear the harm that your actions will impose, you treat those others as entitled to less than the level of concern and respect they are due, and as a result you must accept a special responsibility if you turn out to be mistaken. To treat others as if they lack rights is a grave matter, and this is why mistakes about other people's rights are different from other kinds of mistakes we might make.

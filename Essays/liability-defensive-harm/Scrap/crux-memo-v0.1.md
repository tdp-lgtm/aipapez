# Crux memo v0.1 — the Conferral Account

Working notes, 2026-09-24. AI-generated. Purpose: solve the sketch's crux before the Plan.

## The crux

Why is the mistaken attacker's entitlement a *harm-entitlement* that only the target's own acts,
the balance of claims, or public authority can confer (so it fails when the facts are otherwise),
rather than an evidence-based *licence to take defensive risks*, of the kind Bolinger (2021) and
Ripstein (1996) defend as a fair allocation of the risk of mistake? And where exactly does "acting
upon a person" end and "imposing risk" begin?

## Proposed solution, in five steps

**1. The conferral principle.** A person's claims against being harmed can be *altered* only by her
own acts (consent waives; wrongful conduct forfeits), *overridden* only by others' weightier claims,
and *fixed on the evidence* only by legitimate public procedures. No private agent's evidence about
her is among these. (Support: you cannot consent for another, and a reasonable belief in consent is
not consent; forfeiture is by definition a consequence of the forfeiter's own conduct; Kantian
independence, "no one is judge in his own cause".)

**2. Acting upon versus imposing risk.** An agent *acts upon* a person when his plan aims at her
harm, knowingly (with practical certainty) brings it about, or interferes with her body. Such
conduct is permissible only on an entitlement over that particular person, which by step 1 is
conferred by facts about her or about claims, never by the agent's evidence. An agent who merely
*imposes risk* on whoever may be affected acts on a licence whose content is due care. A licence to
impose risk must be satisfiable by someone who does not know how things will fall out. A licence
that lapsed whenever the risk materialised would be no licence at all.

**3. The independence argument (why targeting claims are fact-relative).** If the twin's claim
against being targeted had due care as its content, his protection *as the particular person he is*
would depend on how he appears to others: on his brother's crimes, the warning, the storm. A claim
that protects a person only as she appears protects her appearance, not her. Risk claims are
different. They never singled her out; they protect a fair share of general safety among everyone
exposed to an activity. So due care is their natural content.

**4. Whose act changes whose position.** Apply step 1 to each pair of parties:
- Resident and twin: only the Resident *acts upon* anyone. His act is his own, so it can change his
  position (liability). The twin's appearance is not his act, so his position is unchanged.
- Driver and pedestrian: each acts, but neither acts upon anyone, and each stays within the due-care
  licence. Neither position changes, and the conflict is symmetric.
- Falling man and the person below: no relevant act on either side, so again symmetric.
- Bluffer and defender: the bluffer's knowing manifestation of an attack is his own act. It changes
  his position toward those who reasonably rely on it (estoppel). The twin never manifested
  anything.
The same principle explains why the Resident is liable, the twin is not, and the bluffer is
estopped.

**5. The contractualist check (reply to Bolinger and Ripstein).** Compare two principles for
mistaken targeting:
- (F) the target keeps his claim. The mistaken defender keeps his evidence-relative permission and
  is blameless, but may not overpower the innocent's resistance, and owes repair.
- (A) the target's claim is only to reasonable treatment, so a reasonably mistaken defender wrongs no
  one.
Under (A), a person can be killed, with no right to resist, for another person's appearance, which
is a burden he could do nothing to avoid. Under (F), the defender bears only the loss of any claim
to prevail over an innocent's resistance. He could avoid even that by not taking another's fate
into his hands. So (A) is reasonably rejectable and (F) is not. The parallel comparison for careful
risk goes the other way: neither party decided anyone's fate, so the symmetric principle is apt.

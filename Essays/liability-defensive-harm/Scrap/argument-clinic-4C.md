## The most serious problem, stated plainly

The load-bearing distinction, acting upon versus merely imposing risk, was defined with a third
clause ("singles her out to bear a risk") that breaks on a simple case. A police sniper fires at a
hostage-taker, knowing there is a 5% chance of hitting the hostage. The sniper singles the hostage out
to bear a risk, so on the v0.2 definition he acts upon her and needs a fact-relative entitlement. If
the shot goes astray, the facts (in hindsight) do not favour the shot, so he would be liable to her
defence and would have wronged her, however good his judgment. That is the wrong result, and it
comes from treating a known *risk* to a particular person as if it were a known *harm*. **Clear
gap.** Fix below (F1).

## Reconstruction (strongest form the outline supports)

- (P1) A responsible agent is liable to defensive harm exactly when she is about to violate another
  person's claim and he has standing to enforce it.
- (P2) If she acts upon him, she violates his claim exactly when no entitlement conferred by his own
  acts, by the balance of claims, or by a legitimate authority permits what she does. Her evidence
  confers no such entitlement.
- (P3) If she merely imposes risk on him, she violates his claim exactly when she fails to take due
  care.
- (P4) Any conduct that threatens him either acts upon him or merely imposes risk on him.
- (P5, suppressed) He lacks standing to enforce his claim against her exactly when he has knowingly
  or carelessly behaved like an attacker and she acts in reasonable reliance on that appearance.
- (C) She is liable exactly when she acts upon him without such an entitlement, or imposes risk on
  him without due care, and P5's condition does not hold.

Check against the outline: Display 2 in v0.2 has P1–P4 and a conclusion that mentions standing, but
no P5. The sections make the P5 argument (§5.2–5.3), but the displayed argument does not state it.
**Clear gap** (Coverage Map A5). Fix F2.

## Validity

With P5 added, (C) follows, given two readings the essay must make explicit:
- "Violate" means fact-relative violation (an infringement no entitlement permits). P1 is neutral on
  what counts as a violation; that is why the rivals can share it (§2.6). **Referee might press**;
  one clause in 2.6 or 3.1 (F3).
- Claims are claims against *conduct* (what others do), not against outcomes. Otherwise P3 is false
  for the pedestrian, who plainly has a claim not to be killed. The account's split of claims by
  conduct type presupposes this, and it is also what keeps the falling man, who does nothing, from
  being liable. This is the account's real point of departure from Thomson. **Clear gap:** it is
  presupposed but never stated. Fix F3.

## Premise by premise

- **P1 (enforcement).** Rejected by those who treat liability as distributive justice (McMahan 2008;
  Gordon-Solmon 2018; both via Clark 2025, 442). v0.2 already says so and cites Burri's reply (2022,
  545–46). Adequate for a premise shared with all three main rivals. No change.
- **P2 (conferral).** The crux. Three stress points:
  - *The prerogative gap.* When the pedestrian kills the careful driver to save herself, she acts
    upon him. What entitles her? Not his acts (he is not liable), not authority. If there is an
    agent-relative prerogative (Quong 2009, 517), it must enter somewhere, or the display's list of
    conferrers silently rules it out. Repair: a prerogative, if there is one, is a weighting of her
    own claim in the balance of claims. The modularity claim in 4.4 then holds exactly: with a
    prerogative she is entitled; without one (Tadros's priority of the status quo) she is not, and
    her defence would make *her* liable. Either way the driver is not liable. **Referee might press**
    (Quong 2009 is about precisely this). Fix F4.
  - *Authority's evidence.* P2 says evidence confers nothing, yet §5 lets a legitimate authority act
    on evidence. Why may a procedure's evidence do what a private person's may not? Repair: the
    source of the guard's entitlement is not the evidence but the procedure's authority over the
    prisoner. The evidence fixes what the authority permits, not whether anyone may decide. A private
    person has no authority over another, so her evidence, however good, decides nothing about him.
    **Referee might press.** Fix F5 (one sentence in 5.6).
  - *Unknowing justification.* Settled in v0.2 (4.5): ignorance does not cancel an entitlement.
    Consistent with P2, which makes entitlements depend on facts about the other person and the
    claims in play, and not on the agent's mind in either direction. No change.
- **P3 (due care).** The parity objection is the strongest attack on the crux and v0.2 answers it
  only in §6 (C), thinly: "the pedestrian's protection also turns on facts not her doing, so by the
  independence argument her claim should be fact-relative too, and the driver liable." This is also
  Lang's line, as far as the abstract shows (#2). Repair: the reply belongs where the independence
  argument is made (3.8). Claims are against conduct. Conduct directed at a particular person (aimed
  at her, or knowingly harming her) raises the question whether *she* may be treated so, and that
  depends on facts about her. Conduct directed elsewhere raises only the question how the agent goes
  about his business around her, and what that can ask of him is care for whoever may be affected.
  The outcome is equally bad for the twin and the pedestrian; the conduct is not the same. **Clear
  gap** (the reply is in the wrong place and too thin). Fix F6.
- **P4 (exhaustiveness).** True by definition since v0.2 (merely imposing risk is the complement of
  acting upon). The work is in the definition, which is where the most serious problem sits (F1).
- **P5 (standing).** Why "knowingly or carelessly"? v0.2 answers from Ferzan's n. 72: the lesser
  upshot (lost standing, not a lost claim) suits the lesser fault. There is a better, internal
  answer. Behaving like an attacker creates a risk that others will be misled into harming or being
  harmed; like any risk, it is governed by due care (P3). So the careless bluffer's standard is not
  added to the account. It is P3's standard, applied to appearances. The upshot is estoppel rather
  than liability because the harm his conduct risks is others' reasonable action against him; there
  is nothing to defend against. **Referee might press** (the "ad hoc" charge). Fix F7.
  - The asymmetry this leaves: acting upon without entitlement makes a careful agent liable (the
    resident), while behaving like an attacker costs a careful person nothing (Frowe's unaware
    actor). Is that consistent? Yes: acting upon someone takes something from her that only she (or
    the balance of claims, or authority) can give, so care is beside the point. Creating an
    appearance takes nothing from anyone; it only risks misleading, and for risks care is the whole
    point. Holds; no change beyond F7.

## Equivocation, term by term

- **"Claim"**: generic in P1; against being acted upon in P2; to due care in P3. No shift of meaning,
  provided claims are against conduct (F3).
- **"Violate"**: fact-relative throughout. Burri uses the enforcement premise with a subjective
  notion of violation, so the essay must say P1 is neutral on this (F3).
- **"Entitlement" / "licence"**: a licence is the entitlement to impose risk. Consistent; the
  displays use "entitlement" only for acting upon. No change.
- **"Acting upon"**: see F1. After the fix its three triggers are aiming to harm, restrain or use, and
  knowingly harming.
- **"Standing"**: undefined in v0.2. Clark's list of what the bluffer loses (permission to defend,
  to be defended, to compensation, to demand not to be harmed: Clark 2025, 442) gives the content.
  **Clear gap** (a term the displays rely on is never defined). Fix F8.
- **"Liable to him"** (Display 1's exception): could be read as "liable to his defence, but still to
  a third party's defence of him". The account's view is that third parties defend *his* claim, so
  they are barred too (Clark's verdict: others may not defend the bluffer). Fix F8 (wording: "not
  liable to defensive harm on his behalf").

## The strongest objection (the one a hostile specialist raises first)

*"The fact/evidence switch is triggered by the agent's own mind. Whether she acts upon someone
depends on what she aims at and what she knows. So the agent's standpoint, expelled at the front
door, returns at the back: the resident is liable because of his intention, the hiker is not because
of his ignorance."*

Candidate reply (adopted, F1): the agent's aims and knowledge fix what *kind* of conduct she engages
in, and so which entitlement it needs; they do not fix whether that entitlement exists. That is the
difference from Quong, whose test makes liability depend on the agent's evidence about the
entitlement itself. Private law draws the line in the same place: it counts as intended whatever the
actor desires or believes "substantially certain to result" (Restatement (Second) of Torts §8A;
wording confirmed by web search 2026-09-24), and it excuses no good-faith mistake about the
entitlement to an intended interference (*Ranson v. Kitner*). This does not narrow the thesis. It
makes the classification rule explicit. It costs about 40 words in 3.3.

## Counterexamples and cases (minimal pairs)

1. **Sniper / hostage** (known 5% risk to a particular person). v0.2: acts upon, which gives a bad
   verdict. After F1: merely imposes risk on the hostage (his conduct is aimed at the hostage-taker,
   and he does not know he will harm her), so due care governs. Right result. Held in reserve as an
   illustration if a referee probes the definition.
2. **Human shield**: aims to use her body, so acts upon her; needs her consent or the balance of
   claims. Right.
3. **Mistaken rescuer** who pulls a stranger back from a road where no car is coming: aims to
   restrain her, so acts upon her without entitlement. She may resist if she knows the road is clear,
   and he owes an apology or repair for any injury. Plausible; reserve.
4. **Forged consent form**: a surgeon reasonably believes the patient consented. Acts upon her
   without entitlement, so she may resist on waking, and he owes repair. Right, and it is the consent
   parallel (3.4) in miniature.
5. **Apparent consent**: the patient herself holds out her arm for a vaccination she did not want.
   Estoppel by manifestation; she cannot complain against the nurse's reasonable reliance. It mirrors
   the bluffer exactly. Reserve (a candidate replacement for the contract example in 5.2 if a referee
   finds that example remote).
6. **Twin who knows** his brother is loose and knocks anyway, in an emergency. Not estopped:
   resemblance is not conduct, and knocking is not attacking. If he knew the killer's method was to
   knock and then attack, knocking might be careless attacker-like behaviour. That is a hard case,
   and the account gives a principled answer (it depends on his care). Reserve.
7. **Careless rehearsal** versus **unaware rehearsal** (Frowe's Actor): in v0.2 (5.3). Holds.

## Reserve replies (not in the outline; for the referee rounds)

- **Tort privilege for reasonable mistaken self-defence** (the law protects reasonable defenders
  from damages). Candidate reply: the privilege is the law's form of the fair-allocation view,
  answered in §6 (A); it concerns damages and blame, and the same law refuses the good-faith excuse
  for intended interference with property (*Ranson*). Would need verified sources before use.
- **Mutual mistake**: whoever first acts upon the other without entitlement is liable; truly
  simultaneous attacks make both liable. No new machinery.
- **Omissions**: out of scope (1.5).

## 6. Objections

The strongest objection holds that reasonable mistakes are part of a fair scheme for sharing the risk of unjust harm. On this view, reasonable defensive mistakes are permissible "as the byproduct of a justified social practice for fairly managing the risk of suffering unjust harm" (Jorgensen 2021, 143 n. 3, as quoted in Clark 2025, 451; verify — #3). Ripstein asks, in the same spirit, whether it is reasonable to make the apparent aggressor bear the risk that the defender is mistaken (Ripstein 1996; verify — #5). The thought is an appealing one. Everyone benefits from a practice that lets people defend themselves on reasonable evidence, since anyone may need to. The rare innocent who is mistakenly attacked bears a risk that the practice spreads across everyone, just as the practice of driving spreads its risks. If so, the twin's claim was only ever a claim to reasonable treatment, which the resident gave him.

I have two replies. The first is in the Conferral Account's own terms. A social practice could confer an entitlement over the twin only if it were an authority that bound him to submit to reasonable defenders. A practice that exempts reasonable defenders from blame and punishment is not that kind of authority, and the Conferral Account already grants them that exemption. The second reply compares burdens. On the objector's principle, the twin's claim is only ever a claim to reasonable treatment. On mine, the twin keeps his claim in full. The resident keeps his permission to act and his innocence, but he may not prevail over the twin's resistance, and he owes him repair. The objector's principle makes the twin bear a lethal cost, with no right to resist, for an appearance he did nothing to create. Mine makes the resident bear a lesser cost: the loss of any claim to prevail over an innocent person he has attacked. That burden arises only from his own choice to act upon another. The stakes are lethal on either view, and my principle places them on the person who chose to act upon another, not on the person acted upon.

The objector may press further. Defenders must act on their judgment in the dark, often in seconds, with no chance to check. A principle that exposes them to the resistance of those they reasonably mistake for attackers seems to burden exactly the people who have done their best. It seems to deny them any assurance that acting well will keep them in the right.

But my principle leaves them everything that acting well can earn. Their permission to act is untouched, so they do nothing wrong in acting. Their blamelessness is untouched too, and so is their full right against real attackers. In the ordinary case, where the attacker is real, they are in the right. What the principle withholds is an assurance that was never theirs to have: a guarantee that, once they have done their best, an innocent stranger must submit to them. No one's best efforts could earn that guarantee. It would amount to a power over the stranger, and his claim is not theirs to dispose of.

A second objection appeals to control and fairness. Liability, it says, should rest on some failure by the agent, something he could have avoided by acting better (Burri 2022, 550–51; Burri 2025, §5; Ferzan 2012, 682). The resident failed in nothing. Ferzan adds that he could not even avoid gambling: if he had not defended himself, he would have gambled with his own life instead (Ferzan 2012, 682 n. 49). I accept both points, and they explain why the resident is permitted and blameless. But his liability is not a sanction for failure. It is the other side of the twin's intact claim, and nothing about the resident's choice, however unavoidable, gave him a say over that claim. Ferzan's own example shows the same structure. A person who takes an umbrella, believing it to be his, may be blameless, but he must still give it back (Ferzan 2012, 690).

I concede that culpability matters for other questions. It plausibly bears on how much harm a liable person may be made to bear, so that a culpable attacker may be liable to more than the resident is. It also bears on which of several liable people should bear a harm when not all can be spared (Tadros 2021, 3506, 3510). The Conferral Account settles only whether a person is liable at all, and it is compatible with any reasonable answer to these further questions.

A third objection comes from the other side. Where the first two say that the resident should not be liable, this one holds that the careful driver is liable after all. McMahan's reason is that the driver "voluntarily chose to set a couple of tons of steel rolling as a means of pursuing his ends", knowing that this carried a tiny risk to others. Since the cost of that choice cannot be divided, "it is fair that he should suffer" it rather than the pedestrian (McMahan 2005a, 394). Lang argues more generally that no principled line separates the careful driver from the resident, so that any account must treat them alike (Lang 2022; verify — #2).

The reply is that nothing in the driver's conduct needed an entitlement over the pedestrian. McMahan's argument assumes that the driver "will therefore *violate* the pedestrian's right not to be killed" (McMahan 2005a, 403). On the Conferral Account he does not. Her claim against his driving was a claim to his due care, and he gave it. Choosing to impose a risk that due care licenses is exactly what the licence covers. The parity objection fails for the reason given in Section 3.

McMahan has a second argument. The first premise is that the pedestrian may kill the driver in self-defence. The second is that one may not kill a person who is not liable in order to save oneself. So the driver must be liable (as reconstructed by Quong 2012, 57). The Conferral Account answers it either way. If there is an agent-relative prerogative, the second premise is false. If there is not, the first premise is false instead: the pedestrian may not kill the driver after all (Section 4). McMahan himself calls the difference he relies on "of comparatively slight moral significance" (2005a, 394). Finally, Lang's arguments target Quong's way of drawing the line, through the dependence test, not the line drawn here (verify — #2).

## 7. Conclusion

I have argued that liability to defensive harm follows the entitlement a person's conduct needs. Entitlements to act upon a person come only from her own acts, the balance of claims, or a legitimate authority over her. A licence to impose risk comes from due care. And behaving like an attacker, knowingly or carelessly, alters a person's standing but not his claim. One principle underlies all three claims: between private persons, no one's judgment binds another, and a claim changes only by its holder's own acts. That principle explains why the resident is liable and the twin is not, why the careful driver is not liable, and why the prankster, though not liable, cannot complain of what his own performance brought on him.

The account leaves some questions open. It says only whether a person is liable, not how much harm he may be made to bear, and it leaves the role of culpability in fixing that degree where Tadros put it. It says nothing about liability for failing to rescue. Within its scope, though, it gives the three cases with which I began their familiar verdicts from a single ground. It also explains two things its rivals have had to stipulate: that justification defeats liability, and that liability requires a responsible agent.

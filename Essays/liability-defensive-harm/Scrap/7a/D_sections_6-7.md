## 6. Objections

The strongest objection holds that reasonable mistakes are part of a fair scheme for sharing the risk of unjust harm: reasonable defensive mistakes are permissible "as the byproduct of a justified social practice for fairly managing the risk of suffering unjust harm" (Jorgensen 2021, 143 n. 3, as quoted in Clark 2025, 451; verify — #3). Ripstein asks, in the same spirit, whether it is reasonable to make the apparent aggressor bear the risk that the defender is mistaken (Ripstein 1996; verify — #5). The thought is an appealing one. Everyone benefits from a practice that lets people defend themselves on reasonable evidence, since anyone may need to, and the rare innocent who is mistakenly attacked bears a risk that the practice spreads across everyone, as the practice of driving spreads its risks. If so, the twin's claim was only ever a claim to reasonable treatment, which the resident gave him.

I have two replies. The first is in the account's own terms. A social practice could confer an entitlement over the twin only if it were an authority that bound him to submit to reasonable defenders. A practice that exempts reasonable defenders from blame and punishment does not do that, and the Conferral Account keeps the exemption. The second reply compares burdens. On the objector's principle, the twin's claim is only to reasonable treatment. On mine, the twin keeps his claim, while the resident keeps his permission and his innocence but may not prevail over the twin's resistance, and owes him repair. The objector's principle makes the twin bear a lethal cost, with no right to resist, for an appearance he did nothing to create. Mine makes the resident bear only the loss of any claim to prevail over an innocent person he has attacked, a burden that arises only from his own choice to act upon another. The stakes are lethal either way; my principle places them on the person who took another's fate into his hands.

The objector may press further. Defenders must act on their judgment in the dark, often in seconds, with no chance to check. A principle that exposes them to the resistance of those they reasonably mistake for attackers seems to burden exactly the people who have done their best, and to deny them any assurance that acting well will keep them in the right.

But my principle leaves them everything that acting well can earn. Their permission to act is untouched, so they do nothing wrong in acting, and their blamelessness is untouched too. So is their full right against real attackers, so that in the ordinary case, where the attacker is real, they are in the right. What the principle withholds is an assurance that was never theirs to have: a guarantee that, when they have done their best, an innocent stranger must submit to them. No one's best efforts could earn that guarantee, because it would be a power over the stranger, and his claim is not theirs to dispose of.

A second objection appeals to control and fairness. Liability, it says, should rest on some failure of the agent's, something he could have avoided by acting better (Burri 2022, 550–51; Burri 2025, §5; Ferzan 2012, 682). The resident failed in nothing. Ferzan adds that he could not even avoid gambling, since had he not defended himself he would have gambled with his own life (Ferzan 2012, 682 n. 49). I accept both points, and they explain why the resident is permitted and blameless. But his liability is not a sanction for failure. It is the other side of the twin's intact claim, and nothing about the resident's choice, however unavoidable, gave him a say over that claim. Ferzan's own example shows the structure: a person who takes an umbrella, believing it to be his, may be blameless, but he must still give it back (Ferzan 2012, 690).

I concede that culpability matters for other questions. It plausibly bears on how much harm a liable person may be made to bear, so that a culpable attacker may be liable to more than the resident is. It also bears on which of several liable people should bear a harm when not all can be spared (Tadros 2021, 3506, 3510). The Conferral Account settles only whether a person is liable at all, and it is compatible with any reasonable answer to these further questions.

A third objection comes from the other side and holds that the careful driver is liable after all. McMahan's reason is that the driver "voluntarily chose to set a couple of tons of steel rolling as a means of pursuing his ends", knowing that this carried a tiny risk to others. Since the cost of that choice cannot be divided, "it is fair that he should suffer" it rather than the pedestrian (McMahan 2005a, 394). Lang argues more generally that no principled line separates the careful driver from the resident, so that any account must treat them alike (Lang 2022; verify — #2).

The reply is that nothing in the driver's conduct needed an entitlement over the pedestrian. McMahan's argument assumes that the driver "will therefore *violate* the pedestrian's right not to be killed" (McMahan 2005a, 403). On the Conferral Account he does not, since her claim against his driving was a claim to his due care, which he gave; a choice to impose a risk that due care has licensed is exactly what the licence covers. The parity objection fails for the reason given in Section 3. McMahan has a second argument: the pedestrian may defend herself, and one may not kill a person who is not liable to save oneself, so the driver must be liable (as reconstructed by Quong 2012, 57). The account answers it either way: if there is an agent-relative prerogative, the second premise is false, and if there is not, so is the first (Section 4). McMahan himself calls the difference he relies on "of comparatively slight moral significance" (2005a, 394). And Lang's arguments target Quong's way of drawing the line, through the dependence test, not the line drawn here (verify — #2).

## 7. Conclusion

I have argued that liability to defensive harm follows the entitlement a person's conduct needs. Entitlements to act upon a person come only from her own acts, the balance of claims, or a legitimate authority over her; a licence to impose risk comes from due care; and behaving like an attacker, knowingly or carelessly, alters a person's standing but not his claim. Beneath these three claims lies one principle: between private persons, no one's judgment binds another, and a claim changes only by its holder's own acts. That principle explains why the resident is liable and the twin is not, why the careful driver is not liable, and why the prankster, though not liable, cannot complain of what his own performance brought on him.

The account leaves some questions open. It says whether a person is liable, not how much harm he may be made to bear, and it leaves the role of culpability in fixing that degree where Tadros put it. It says nothing about liability for failing to rescue. Within its scope, though, it gives the three cases with which I began their familiar verdicts from a single ground, and it explains two things its rivals have had to stipulate: that justification defeats liability, and that liability requires a responsible agent.

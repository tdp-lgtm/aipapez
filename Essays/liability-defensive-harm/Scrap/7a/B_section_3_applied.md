## 3. The Conferral Account

> **The Conferral Account.** A responsible agent is liable to defensive harm when, and only when, (a) she acts upon another person without an entitlement conferred by his own acts, by the balance of claims, or by a legitimate authority over him, or (b) she imposes risk on him without due care. The one exception: she is not liable to defensive harm on his behalf if he has knowingly or carelessly behaved like an attacker and she acts in reasonable reliance on that appearance.

A claim, on this view, is a claim against what another person does. Conduct violates the claim when it does what the claim forbids without the entitlement that conduct needs. "Violation" is meant in the fact-relative sense; "permission", where I grant it, is meant in the evidence-relative sense. The account yields three statuses. A liable person's claim yields to necessary and proportionate defensive force. A person who is not liable has a fully protected claim. And a person who has behaved like an attacker keeps his claim but may not resist, may not be defended by others, and cannot demand repair (Section 5).

The account turns on one distinction. An agent *acts upon* a person when she aims to harm, restrain or use that person, or knowingly harms him. She *merely imposes risk* when her conduct threatens someone without acting upon him. The resident acts upon the twin, since he aims to stab him. The thrower in Clark's warehouse case acts upon the hostages, since he knows his grenade will injure them. The careful driver and the hiker merely impose risk, since neither aims at anyone or knows that anyone will be harmed. A police marksman who fires at a hostage-taker, knowing there is a small chance of hitting the hostage, acts upon the hostage-taker but merely imposes risk on the hostage.

The agent's aims and knowledge settle which kind of conduct she engages in, and so which entitlement that conduct needs. But they never settle whether the entitlement exists. That is why the agent's standpoint classifies her conduct without deciding whether she is liable. McMahan remarks in passing that the resident, unlike someone who threatens others unknowingly, "at least knows that he poses a threat, and thus is responsible for ensuring that it is not an unjust threat" (McMahan 2005a, 402). The Conferral Account turns that remark into a principle, and the argument from authority explains why it holds. Tort law draws the line in the same place. It treats a consequence as intended when the actor desires it or believes it "substantially certain to result" from his act (Restatement (Second) of Torts, §8A). I concede that there are borderline cases. A vaccination programme certain to kill someone, though no one in particular, knowingly harms no particular person. But as the known probability of harm to a particular person approaches certainty, the line blurs.

Why should acting upon a person need an entitlement that the agent's evidence cannot supply? The first argument is from authority. Evidence can give an agent permission to act. The resident's evidence overwhelmingly indicates that the man at the door is the murderer, so it is permissible for him, in the evidence-relative sense, to defend himself. But permission to act is not power over another person. For the twin to lose his claim against the knife, the resident's judgment that he is the murderer would have to settle, as between them, what the twin may be subjected to, and no private person's judgment has that authority over another. Each person is independent of every other's choice; Kant made this the one innate right, "independence from being constrained by another's choice" (Kant 1797, 6:237, verify — #20; see Ripstein 2009, verify — #21). So the resident's evidence, however strong, does not strip the twin of his claim. Only three things can settle the twin's position: his own acts, by which he binds himself; the balance of claims, which is no one's say-so; and a legitimate authority, whose judgment does have authority over him. Consent is the familiar case. No one can decide for another that she consented, and a reasonable belief that she consented is not her consent (Ferzan 2012, 690 n. 70).

The three sources work differently. Forfeiture follows the forfeiter's own conduct. This answers Renzo's demand for a principle explaining how "by acting in certain ways we alter our normative status" (Renzo 2017; verify — #12): among private persons, only the holder of a claim has authority over it. The balance of claims overrides a claim without removing it. The claim is infringed rather than violated, and it survives as a claim to repair (McMahan 2005a, 388; Quong 2012, 51 n. 11). Legitimate authority is the subject of Section 5.

The second argument, from appearance, makes the same point from the twin's side. If the resident's reasonable judgment bound the twin, the twin's protection would reach only as far as his appearance to others. Whether he kept that protection would then depend on his brother's crimes, the warning and the storm, none of them his doing. A claim that protected a person only as she appeared would protect her appearance rather than her. Burri herself holds that the protection a right gives its holder is objective (Burri 2025, §2), and the constraint on those who act upon a person cannot be looser than that protection. So the twin's protection cannot depend on how he appears to the resident. One might reply that the balance of claims also binds a person without any act of hers. But it outweighs her claim without removing it. That is why she keeps her claim to repair.

Merely imposing risk is different. The careful driver passes no judgment on what the pedestrian may be subjected to. He judges only his own activity, whether and how to drive, as each of us must be free to do. He conducts it on terms of care that make everyone's freedom to act compatible with everyone's safety. Those terms are shared; they are not his to set. So the pedestrian is subject to no one's judgment, and her claim against the driver is that he keep those terms. A licence to impose risk must also be usable by someone who cannot know how things will turn out, since a licence that lapsed whenever the risk materialised would be none. An activity imposes risk in this sense when it may be pursued only under duties of care (Burri 2020; verify — #8). A single trip is justified by its own prospects under due care (Frowe 2022; verify — #4).

Here is the obvious objection, which I will call the parity objection. The pedestrian's protection also turns on facts that are not her doing, namely the freak failure of the car. If the second argument works for the twin, it seems to work for her, and then the driver is liable too. The reply is that the cases differ in subjection, not in luck. If the resident's judgment bound the twin, the twin would be subject to another person's judgment about him. The pedestrian is subject to no one's. This also meets the thought that morality can ask of an agent only that he act reasonably (Ferzan 2012, 682 n. 49). It asks only that of the resident, which is why he is permitted and blameless. But whether the twin must submit is a question about the twin's claim, and another person's reasonableness gives him no reason to submit. The pedestrian had the driver's due care, which is all her claim against him asked. So nothing of hers was violated. Whether she may defend herself is a further question (Section 4).

The distinction is not new. Private law does not excuse a good-faith mistake about whose property one is intentionally using or destroying. Hunters who shot a dog they took for a wolf owed its owner its value (*Ranson v. Kitner*, 31 Ill. App. 241 (1889); verify), whereas accidental harm answers to the standard of negligence. Ripstein treats trespass and battery as wrongs of using what is another's (Ripstein 2016; verify — #19). The law does excuse reasonable mistakes about the need for self-defence (e.g. Restatement (Second) of Torts, §63). That is the fair-allocation view answered in Section 6. The parallel shows only that the line is familiar. It does not show that private law's cheap remedies carry over, since here the stakes are lethal on both sides. What is new is the line's application to defensive liability, the argument for it, and its extension to appearances (Section 5).

The account also explains why liability requires a responsible agent. One might object that consent and forfeiture cannot share a source, since consent is chosen and forfeiture is not. But both are ways in which a person's own acts alter her claims, and both require the same capacity. Young children and the deluded lack that capacity. So the man pushed off the cliff, who does nothing at all (Thomson 1991, 287), is not liable. Neither is the driver drugged by a villain into trying to run you down (284). Neither case needs a separate stipulation.

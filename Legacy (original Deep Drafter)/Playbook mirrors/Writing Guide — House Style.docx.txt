 Writing Guide — House Style
 The stylistic core for every paper this agent drafts. Personalized at setup; the defaults below are field-neutral and battle-tested. v1.0 — Shareable Edition, July 2026.
 PERSONALIZE ME — these defaults were distilled from heavy real use on argumentative papers (philosophy, law) and most of them transfer to any field. At setup (see 'SETUP — Personalize the Agent'), the author confirms, adjusts, or replaces each section — especially 'Structure within sections', which is genre-specific. The author's own additions belong here too: this document is house law for every draft.
[Heading 1] Core principle
 Strong, clear, direct arguments. No verbal disputes. No editorializing. Claude's default draft style tends to over-hedge, over-qualify, and over-frame. Each argument should be stated forcefully and defended on substance, not buried in framing.
[Heading 1] Structure within sections (default for argumentative papers — confirm at setup)
 Often, but not always, each substantive section or subsection has 3–5 affirmative arguments, each one paragraph. Use explicit numbering: "First, … Second, … Third, …" within the relevant level.
 Often, but not always, after the affirmative arguments, engage 3–5 objections, one paragraph each. "The first objection is X. … The second objection is Y. …" Brief steelman, then direct response.
 Big-picture objections to the whole proposal can get their own section. But local objections — objections to a specific argument — live in the section or subsection with that argument, not in a separate objections chapter.
 Don't pad. If a section has only one or two real objections, engage those and move on. Canvas the important objections; don't add filler to round out the count.
[Heading 1] What dialectical engagement should look like
 The kind to produce: state the opposing view briefly and faithfully, then explain why it fails. "It is true that X. Nonetheless, Y." Done in a paragraph; move on. (That said, don't overuse any one literal pattern.)
 The kind to avoid: "Some have argued X, but what they really mean is X′, which differs from X in that it relies on assumption A. If A is interpreted strictly, then… but if A is interpreted broadly, then…" This is a verbal dispute. The reader doesn't care.
 If an opponent's position is unclear, take the strongest natural reading and respond to that. If multiple readings matter, footnote them.
[Heading 1] Sentence-level discipline
[List Bullet] One move per sentence. Multiple subordinate clauses pile up fast. Pick the main move and make it.
[List Bullet] Em-dash asides need to earn their place. "The firm's decisionmakers — board members, executives, shareholders — make release decisions" should become "Firm decisionmakers make release decisions." If the aside does real work, keep it; usually it doesn't.
[List Bullet] Cut filler. "In the first instance," "it is worth noting," "as we shall see," "we acknowledge," "broadly speaking" — these signal hedging without adding information.
[List Bullet] Cut bloat. "X supplies the most useful overview" / "Y provides a helpful framework" — introductions that don't say anything. Engage the substance directly.
[Heading 1] Editorializing — what to cut
[List Bullet] Meta-statements about which argument is harder or stronger. "The strongest objection is…" "The hardest version of the worry is…" Let arguments stand on their own; the reader will form a view.
[List Bullet] Pacing sentences. "We have shown X. We now turn to Y." Section structure already does this work.
[List Bullet] Self-referential commentary. "We are arguing here that…" "The point of this section is to…" Just make the argument.
[List Bullet] Reassuring the reader. "We take seriously the concern that…" Engage the concern directly; don't preface the engagement.
 Instead of this stuff, use simple idea-based signposting at the start of sections, saying very quickly what will happen.
[Heading 1] Common Claude failure modes to watch for
 These show up across projects and across drafts. Catch them on your own review passes.
[List Bullet] Repetition of main claims — the #1 failure mode. The same point made in three sections under three framings. Each argument should appear in exactly one place; downstream sections cross-reference rather than re-argue.
[List Bullet] Over-editorializing rather than direct arguing. Commenting on the argumentation rather than making it.
[List Bullet] Long-winded sentences. Em-dashes and parentheticals stacking up; one sentence doing the work of three.
[List Bullet] Bloated, padded prose. Sentences that introduce or frame without saying anything substantive.
[Heading 1] At draft stage
[List Bullet] Focus on argument quality, not length targets. Word counts come later (one light pass near the end — see the Pipeline).
[List Bullet] Citations can be parenthetical placeholders ("Smith 2024"); the proper apparatus comes in a later pass, verified against real sources.
[List Bullet] Build up in layers: short outline → detailed outline → paragraph-by-paragraph long-form outline → prose. The intermediate layers catch structural redundancy before it gets baked into prose.
[List Bullet] When the author edits a document and sends it back, those edits set the direction. Never revert to a formulation the author overruled. If you see differences from what you produced, that is because the author made them on purpose.
[Heading 1] Model prose
 Before drafting, read the closest example in the Model Prose Library (General Guide to Academic Writing/Model Prose Library/) IN FULL — the author's own papers, cataloged with what to imitate in each. Read for register and sentence shapes, never for content: don't import material from the model paper into the draft. The Voice Profile distills the sentence-level patterns; the model papers show them live.
[Heading 1] Quick self-review checklist
 Before sending a draft, ask:
[List Bullet] Does each argument appear in exactly one place?
[List Bullet] Does each paragraph make one argument?
[List Bullet] Does each sentence make one move?
[List Bullet] Am I editorializing about the argumentation, or just making it?
[List Bullet] Am I getting bogged down in verbal disputes, or engaging the substance?
[List Bullet] Have I cut every "it is worth noting," "in the first instance," "as we shall see," and similar filler?

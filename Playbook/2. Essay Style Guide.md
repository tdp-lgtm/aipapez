# Essay Style Guide — Competition Edition

The single style reference for competition essays. It merges the legacy Writing Guide (house style),
the Philosophy Paper style module, the Analytic Philosophy Prose companion, and the Prose Register
guide (all preserved in `Legacy (original Deep Drafter)/`), adapted to one venue: the **AI Philosophy
Competition** — a 6,000-word analytic philosophy essay graded by academic philosophers on clarity,
argumentation, significance, originality, engagement, and accuracy. **Style is not graded for its own
sake. Every style rule here serves one end: make the philosophy easy to assess.**

## Core principle

Strong, clear, direct arguments. No verbal disputes. No editorializing. State each argument
forcefully and defend it on substance; don't bury it in framing. The target register is simple,
clear, even boring academic prose — invisible prose, where the reader notices the argument and never
the writing.

## Length and mechanics

- **≤ 6,000 words excluding the bibliography — everything else counts** (abstract, footnotes,
  appendices, displays). Check with `word_count.py` (Build scripts); the competition's count governs.
- Abstract ~120–200 words, written last.
- 5–7 numbered sections. Front-loaded thesis: it appears in the abstract, the first paragraph of the
  introduction, and the first paragraph of the body.
- **Citations:** author-date in text ("Smith 2024, 12") with a bibliography at the end. Footnotes
  sparingly — genuinely substantive asides only, never the main citation vehicle. If a point matters,
  main text; if it doesn't, cut or footnote (prefer footnoting to hedging).
- Heavy formalism goes to an appendix (which still counts toward 6,000) — keep the main text readable
  by a smart generalist.
- Solo-authored register: "I" throughout ("I argue," "I defend") — the essay's author is the AI, but
  the prose uses the ordinary first person of a philosophy paper. Never break the fourth wall: no
  reference to being an AI, to prompts, to the competition, or to the drafting process anywhere in
  the essay. (That material belongs in the methodology report.)
- **Anonymized:** no names, affiliations, emails, or self-identifying references.
- **No prompt injection:** nothing anywhere in a submitted file may address or instruct an AI reader.

## Structure conventions (analytic philosophy)

- ~2–4 affirmative arguments per section, each its own paragraph, explicitly numbered ("First, …
  Second, …"). Don't pad to hit a count; a section with one real argument makes it and moves on.
- Named arguments with stable 1–3-word labels, reused verbatim across abstract, intro, headings,
  cross-references, and conclusion. One name per thing, one thing per name.
- Local objections live with their argument; only the 1–3 biggest cross-cutting objections get a
  dedicated section. Brief steelman in the objector's voice, then direct reply. Only the strongest
  few objections (3–4 total for a 6k essay).
- **Displays** (indented, bold-label blocks) for: every named principle (stated in one or two plain
  sentences under its bold name); the essay's own view, in full, early (the reader should be able to
  quote the thesis from the display alone); every named case (a 2–4-sentence self-contained vignette
  containing every stipulation the analysis will use); and **the central argument, once, in premise
  form** — simple premises that visibly imply the conclusion, no enthymemes at the load-bearing
  joint, with the defense location of each premise named ("Premise one was defended in §3; this
  section defends premise two"). In Markdown, use a blockquote with a bold label:

  > **The Reciprocity View.** One or two plain sentences stating the view in full.

- A concrete example, case, name, or number wherever the work is abstract. Open with a real case told
  plainly before any theory appears; inside sections, prefer example, then machinery.

## Sentence-level discipline

- One move per sentence. Plain declarative sentences dominate; a longer one is fine when its spine
  stays simple.
- Plain verbs of assertion: says, holds, argues, requires, implies, shows. If a verb is doing style
  work ("the theory goes quiet"), replace it.
- The whole transition kit: "First / Second / Third," "But," "So," "Yet," "Now," "Consider," "Take X
  first," "Why? Because…," an occasional question as a pivot. Nothing fancier.
- No rhetorical fragments. No drama adjectives ("extraordinary," "striking," "remarkable"). If a
  sentence would sound clever read aloud, flatten it — wit lives in the examples, never the diction.
- **Dash discipline:** chained dashes and dash asides that don't earn their place are removed — use a
  colon, a comma, parentheses, or a new sentence. (Hyphenated compounds and page ranges stay.)
- Cut filler ("it is worth noting," "in the first instance," "as we shall see," "broadly speaking")
  and bloat ("X provides a helpful framework" introductions that say nothing).

## Explain before use

Nothing appears in an argument before the reader has it: no term, formal device, named case,
doctrine, or allusion is used first and explained later. Assume a smart philosopher with zero
background in any imported apparatus (game theory, economics, statistics, doctrine) and define it
from scratch, in the body, in a sentence or two. An allusion the reader may not share is developed
properly or cut. Machinery is introduced at the point of use, never previewed; roadmaps name what
each section shows, not the tools it will use.

## No metaphors doing argument work

A metaphor may restate a point the prose has already made plainly; it may never carry the point.
Test: delete the metaphor. If the argument loses a step, that step was never argued — write the
mechanism in plain words first.

## Referents

Repeat the plain noun. "The proportionality condition" is "the proportionality condition" every time,
never "the test" or "this machinery." Coin a term only when it is defined at first use, reused, and
load-bearing.

## Editorializing — cut it all

- No meta-statements ranking your own arguments ("the strongest objection is…").
- No pacing sentences ("We have shown X; we now turn to Y") — section structure does this.
- No self-reference ("this section argues…"; "the point of this paragraph is…").
- No reassuring the reader ("we take seriously the concern that…") — engage the concern directly.
- Roadmap: one plain sentence per section, idea-based. No up-front limits paragraph — concessions and
  scope restrictions appear where they arise, stated once, without apology.

## Dialectical engagement

- Produce: state the opposing view briefly and faithfully, then explain why it fails. "It is true
  that X. Nonetheless, Y." A paragraph; move on.
- Avoid: verbal disputes ("what they really mean is X′, which differs from X in that…"). If an
  opponent's position is unclear, take the strongest natural reading and respond to that; footnote
  alternative readings only if they matter.
- **Quote the opposition:** state targeted views in their authors' own words with pinpoints, so the
  disagreement is on the surface. Quotes are for opponents and evidence; paraphrase is for your own
  moves. Every quote comes from sources on disk or verified online, never from memory.

## Known failure modes (self-check on every pass)

1. **Repetition of main claims** — the #1 failure mode: the same point under three framings in three
   sections. Each argument appears in exactly one place; downstream sections cross-reference.
2. Editorializing about the argumentation instead of making it.
3. Long-winded sentences; stacked clauses, dashes, and parentheticals.
4. Bloated framing prose that says nothing substantive.
5. Terms used before they are defined (the single largest source of register defects on real runs).

## The craft layer

For depth beneath this guide, `Playbook/Craft/` holds the field-craft layer: `journal-craft.md`
(structure conventions with a red-flags list), `intro-playbook.md`, `prose-principles.md` (the
8-step self-edit sequence and the two registers), `ai-tells.md`, `abstracts-and-titles.md`, and
`moves-catalog.md` — the anonymous bank of argument architectures and prose moves. Two absolute
rules (canonical in `Craft/README.md`): the playbook names no real authors or papers, and every
technique is executed in the essay's own material — no imported sentences, cases, coinages, or
content from anywhere. The essays themselves, of course, cite the real literature by name, with
verified sources; that is a graded criterion.

## Pre-return checklist

Every principle / the view / every case / the central argument displayed; each display
self-contained; premises visibly valid with defense locations named; objections capped and
steelmanned; each argument in exactly one place; each paragraph one argument; each sentence one move;
no undefined term, no metaphor carrying an argument, no fragment, no drama adjective, no elegant
variation, no filler; concrete before abstract; opposition quoted verbatim with pinpoints; "I" not
"we"; within budget; anonymized; nothing addressed to an AI reader.

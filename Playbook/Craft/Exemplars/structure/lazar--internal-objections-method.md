# Annotated exemplar — Lazar, "Responsibility, Risk, and Killing in Self-Defense" (internal-objections method / antithesis thesis)

## Source
- **Author / Work:** Seth Lazar, "Responsibility, Risk, and Killing in Self-Defense," *Ethics* 119, no. 4 (July 2009): 699–728 · suggested cite key `lazar2009responsibility` (verify against the source before citing)
- **Why this passage:** The thesis is staged as an *inversion of the standing objection* — the view's critics say it is too restrictive; Lazar will beat it by its own lights, showing it is too permissive.

## The passage
> The agency view has been criticized for implausibly restricting the right to kill in self-defense. I disagree with this criticism and will argue that the agency view is not too restrictive; it is too permissive.

## What makes it work (the lesson)
- **The thesis is a flip, not a fresh assertion.** Lazar quotes the field's existing complaint ("too restrictive"), then reverses its sign ("too permissive"). The reader instantly knows the shape of the contribution and that it is non-obvious.
- **The antithesis sentence is a memorable two-beat: "not X; Y."** The semicolon does the work — symmetry sets up the surprise. This is far stickier than "I argue the view over-extends liability."
- **He beats the view on its own ground, not with an outside intuition.** The critique runs through the view's *own* concept — agent responsibility "does not adequately differentiate between A and B" (the attacker and the defender are both agent-responsible for the forced-choice). The opponent is defeated by their own standard, so they cannot dismiss the objection as begging the question.
- **Concession buys authority.** He grants the view's animating insight (responsibility grounds liability) before turning it; the reader trusts that he has understood the position he is dismantling.

## What to borrow
When you attack a rival view, look for the objection it has *already absorbed and answered*, then show that the view fails by its own criterion in the opposite direction — and state the result as a tight "not too X; too Y" inversion (cf. `Craft/structure-casebook.md` §3 dialectic: "steel-man then defeat"; "concede the conditional").

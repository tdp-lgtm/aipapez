# Annotated exemplar — Quong, "Killing in Self-Defense" (the openly iteratively-revised principle: state, break with a case, amend, repeat in full view)

## Source
- **Author / Work:** Jonathan Quong, "Killing in Self-Defense," *Ethics* 119, no. 3 (April 2009): 507–537. · suggested cite key: `quong2009killing` (verify against the source before citing)
- **Why this passage:** Quong builds the principle in numbered stages, each prompted by a case the previous version mishandles — the revision is performed on the page rather than presented finished.

## The passage
> *Infringing in Defense:* You can permissibly infringe someone else's rights when this is necessary to defend something that belongs to you.
> Of course this principle is too simple. First, it needs to be restricted by some notion of proportionality. … So this means we need:
> *Infringing in Defense 2:* … provided the infringement is proportionate.
> But our principle is still incomplete. … we need to modify our principle yet again:
> *Infringing in Defense 3:* … provided (a) the infringement is proportionate and (b) the presence of the person whose right is infringed is not necessary in order for you to keep what belongs to you.

## What makes it work (the lesson)
- **Each amendment is *earned* by a counterexample.** A case the current version gets wrong (kill the friend over an ant colony; pinch the neighbor) drives the very next clause. The reader watches the principle fail and get repaired, so the final clauses feel necessary, not stipulated.
- **Visible version numbers.** "Infringing in Defense 2 / 3" lets the reader hold the moving target in view and see exactly what each revision adds. The labeled iterations are a navigation aid and an honesty device.
- **Candid meta-narration.** "Of course this principle is too simple," "still incomplete," "modify our principle yet again" — the author concedes the inadequacy before the reader can, converting a potential objection into a planned step.
- **The displayed-and-revised form pre-empts referees.** Anyone tempted to lob the obvious counterexample finds it already on the page, generating the next clause.

## What to borrow
When a principle needs several qualifications, don't present the polished final version — state the simple core, break it with a case, add the clause that case demands, label the new version, and repeat, narrating each "this is still too simple" out loud.

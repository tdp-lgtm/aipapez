# Annotated exemplar — Srinivasan, "The Aptness of Anger" (concede-the-empirics scoping; braided close)

## Source
- **Author / Work:** Amia Srinivasan, *The Aptness of Anger*, Journal of
  Political Philosophy 26(2), 2018 · srinivasan2018 (verify against the source before citing)
- **Why this passage:** the strategic concession that *creates* the paper's
  question — grant the critic's empirical premise outright, so the purely
  normative issue stands alone — plus the ending that braids back to the opening.

## The passages
> By contrast I want to grant the counterproductivity critic's empirical
> supposition that anger generally makes things worse, in order to focus on
> occasions where anger would be counterproductive but nonetheless apt.

> the debate about anger was never about them.

## What makes it work (the lesson)
- **The concession is the scoping device.** Rather than fighting the critic's
  empirical claim (a losing, off-topic battle), she *grants it in full* — which
  isolates exactly the normative question she can win, and makes the paper
  immune to empirical counterevidence.
- "In order to focus on…" names what the concession buys: the reader sees the
  question being carved, not dodged.
- **The braided close**: the final line returns to the opening (the
  Baldwin–Buckley debate) and recontextualizes the whole dispute — closure by
  return, not by summary.

## What to borrow
When a critic's premise is empirical and yours is normative, grant the premise
explicitly "in order to focus on" the question that remains — the concession
buys immunity and sharpens the thesis. And consider ending where you began:
one line that reframes the opening scene in light of everything argued.

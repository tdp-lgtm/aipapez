# Annotated exemplar — Howard, "Dangerous Speech" (anaphora + real-victim framing that loads the stakes)

## Source
- **Author / Work:** Jeffrey W. Howard, "Dangerous Speech," *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 47, no. 2 (2019): [start]–[end] · suggested cite key: `howard2019dangerous` (verify against the source before citing)
- **Why this passage:** Before any theory or definition, Howard makes the human cost vivid with named real atrocities and a repeated counterfactual frame, so the reader feels the stakes the argument will have to be worth.

## The passage
> For all we know, had these individuals not encountered websites and online videos that spewed hate and encouraged terrorism, they would not have decided to become murderers. For all we know, had they not been incited by people peddling dangerous ideas, those 14 employees from San Bernardino and 51 worshippers in Christchurch would still be alive.

## What makes it work (the lesson)
- **Anaphora builds gravity through repetition.** The twin "For all we know, had they not…" openings stack two counterfactuals into one rising structure; the parallel makes the second land harder than it would alone, and the hedge ("for all we know") keeps the claim honest while the rhythm keeps it forceful.
- **Name real victims and count them.** "Those 14 employees from San Bernardino and 51 worshippers in Christchurch" puts specific, numbered, real dead people at the end of the sentence — the position of maximum weight. Concreteness, not adjectives, does the moralizing.
- **"Would still be alive" is the payload, held to the end.** The whole sentence is engineered so the last three words are the loss. Save the most affecting phrase for the terminal position.
- **Stakes before theory.** This is paragraph one, before the legal doctrine or the philosophical question ("Is it morally permissible for the state to suppress expression…?"). The reader is made to *want* the question answered before being handed it. Calibrated, not lurid: the counterfactuals are explicitly tentative.

## What to borrow
Open a stakes-laden paper by making the harm concrete and real before you theorize: use a repeated clause (anaphora) to stack two parallel counterfactuals, name and number the actual victims, and hold the most affecting phrase ("would still be alive") to the end of the sentence — then pose the abstract question. Keep an honest hedge so the framing reads as motivation, not melodrama. Cf. `Craft/prose-casebook.md` and `Craft/intro-playbook.md` on motivating a question before stating it.

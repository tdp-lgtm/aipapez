# Annotated exemplar — Muñoz, concession + cadence

## Source

- **Author:** Daniel Muñoz
- **Work:** "Impartiality, Anonymity, and Caring Who," *Free & Equal* (2025).
- **Why this passage:** models the "concede, then narrow" move and the short-
  sentence cadence that makes condensed prose feel decisive rather than terse.

## The passages

> Again, I concede that Majority Rule for ethics isn't plausible. […] But I'm not
> saying that Majority Rule is plausible, only that it's impartial.

> But these principles are different, and the difference matters.

> Just as our criterion for deductive validity should apply to all arguments, not
> just the plausible ones, our criteria for impartiality should apply to all
> principles.

## What makes it work (the lesson)

- **Concede in plain words, then say exactly what survives.** "I concede X… But
  I'm not saying X, only Y." The concession is total and unembarrassed; the
  narrowing is precise. This *buys* authority for the surrounding argument (see
  `journal-craft.md` §3, §6).
- **One-line paragraph for the pivot.** "But these principles are different, and
  the difference matters." Standing alone, it both states and emphasizes — the
  cadence does the underlining, no "crucially" needed.
- **Analogy to carry an abstract point.** The deductive-validity parallel makes
  "an impartiality criterion must cover implausible principles too" instantly
  intuitive. One clean analogy, not three.
- **Confidence at the claim, humility at the inference.** He's flat about the
  structural point and careful exactly where assent should be provisional.

## What I want to borrow

When answering an objection, grant it fully and then name the narrower thing I
still hold. Give pivotal claims their own short sentence. Reach for a single
analogy when an abstraction needs grounding — and stop at one.

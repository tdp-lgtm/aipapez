# Annotated exemplar — Muñoz, "Each Counts for One" (locating a debate; the diagnosis move)

## Source

- **Author:** Daniel Muñoz
- **Work:** "Each Counts for One," *Philosophical Studies* (forthcoming).
- **Why this passage:** model of orienting the reader in a long-running debate in a
  few sentences, and of the "diagnosis" reframing that becomes the paper's spine.

## The passage

> Suppose you are choosing which of two groups to save. Does it intrinsically
> matter if one group is bigger? […]
>
> Most ethicists, like Derek Parfit (1978), say yes: the numbers should count. But
> some, like John Taurek (1977), say no […].
>
> Clearly, at least one of these arguments must fail. But which? […] I argue that
> the answer is neither.
>
> The problem, I think, is that both sides fail to distinguish Anonymity (the
> principle that no one counts for more) from Responsiveness (the principle that
> anyone's interests can break a tie).

## What makes it work (the lesson)

- **Case + sharp question** opens, not a literature survey.
- **Two camps, one-word stances:** each side gets a named representative and a
  "yes"/"no." A 50-year debate is located in two sentences.
- **Short pivot question** ("But which?") drives from setup to thesis; the thesis
  lands flat ("the answer is neither").
- **The diagnosis move:** the contribution is framed as a distinction others have
  conflated (*Anonymity* vs *Responsiveness*). Naming that distinction *is* the
  paper's backbone — everything downstream refers to it.

## What I want to borrow

To enter a crowded debate: one case, one sharp question, name one representative
per side with a one-word stance, then state my thesis flat. If my contribution is
a reframing, announce the exact distinction everyone has been blurring and make it
the recurring referent.

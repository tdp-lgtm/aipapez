# Annotated exemplar — Muñoz, "Three Paradoxes of Supererogation" (cadence; stacking puzzles)

## Source

- **Author:** Daniel Muñoz
- **Work:** "Three Paradoxes of Supererogation," *Noûs* (forthcoming).
- **Why this passage:** sentence cadence (short flat claim + witty fragment) and
  how to motivate a *unified* solution to several puzzles at once.

## The passage

> We don't always have to do what's best. Some good deeds are supererogatory: they
> are optional and yet better than other permissible options. […] Lovely stuff;
> hardly required.
>
> But as innocent as the examples may sound, the theory of supererogation is
> fraught with paradoxes. […] If being the hero is really better, why isn't it
> just obligatory? […] But surely it's always worse to do wrong. How could it not
> be?
>
> These puzzles are usually discussed in isolation. […] But we need a unified
> solution. My goal is to find one.

## What makes it work (the lesson)

- **Flat opening claim:** "We don't always have to do what's best." No throat-
  clearing; the topic is alive in seven words.
- **Witty fragment for cadence:** "Lovely stuff; hardly required." Used once, it
  earns a smile and resets the rhythm before the turn.
- **Each puzzle stated as a question** the reader feels ("why isn't it just
  obligatory?"; "How could it not be?") — the paradoxes are dramatized, not
  summarized.
- **Motivating a unified treatment:** name that the puzzles are usually handled
  separately, assert they're linked, and state the aim in a three-word sentence:
  "My goal is to find one."

## What I want to borrow

Open with a flat, plain claim. Allow exactly one witty fragment to vary the
rhythm. When tackling several problems at once, say plainly that they're usually
treated in isolation, claim the link, and state the aim in a short sentence.

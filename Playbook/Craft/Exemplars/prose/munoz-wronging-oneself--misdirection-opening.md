# Annotated exemplar — Muñoz & Baron-Schmitt, "Wronging Oneself" (opening)

## Source

- **Authors:** Daniel Muñoz & Nathaniel Baron-Schmitt
- **Work:** "Wronging Oneself," *Journal of Philosophy* (forthcoming).
- **Why this passage:** the "misdirection opening" — a viscerally felt puzzle set
  up by narrative, before any technical apparatus.

## The passage

> Earlier today, without asking permission, I took a bike belonging to someone in
> my neighborhood and rode it to work. […] So I pinched their arm, opened their
> mouth, and poured hot coffee inside. […]
>
> Before you reach for any moral sanctions: this "neighbor" of mine was me.
>
> There is something wonderfully elusive about wronging oneself. […] This is a
> striking fact. Why aren't we constantly violating our own rights?
>
> Pinching one's own arm is not like pinching a stranger out of the blue. It is
> like pinching someone else's arm with their consent. No one is wronged because
> there is no unwilling victim.

## What makes it work (the lesson)

- **Narrative misdirection pumps the intuition.** The catalogue reads as a list of
  assaults; the one-line reveal ("was me") makes the reader *feel* the puzzle
  rather than be told it. Stakes shown, not asserted.
- **Short evaluative beat sets the tone:** "There is something wonderfully elusive
  about wronging oneself." One sentence, half-lyrical, then back to business.
- **The puzzle as a flat question:** "This is a striking fact. Why aren't we
  constantly violating our own rights?" Declarative + question = the engine.
- **Triadic short sentences carry the argument:** three clipped sentences whose
  parallel structure ("is not like… / It is like… / No one is wronged…") does the
  inferential work without connectives.

## What I want to borrow

When a puzzle can be *felt*, stage it — narrate a case that lands the intuition,
then name the puzzle in one short question. Let a triad of short parallel
sentences carry the key move instead of a long subordinated one.

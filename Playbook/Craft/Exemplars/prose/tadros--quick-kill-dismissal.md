# Annotated exemplar — Tadros, "Permissibility in a World of Wrongdoing" (quick-kill dismissal)

## Source
- **Author / Work:** Victor Tadros, "Permissibility in a World of Wrongdoing," *Philosophy & Public Affairs* (2016) · suggested cite key: `tadros2016permissibility` (verify against the source before citing)
- **Why this passage:** Tadros flags that a view (*Fair Share I*) needs no laborious refutation, then drops a single decisive case that ends it.

## The passage
> We can dismiss this view without further ado by considering:
> Boat: V is drowning in a pond. He can be rescued by paying £20 … X is surely required to pay £20 to save V's life. Even if Y's failure makes some difference … it cannot make that much difference.

## What makes it work (the lesson)
- **Announce the cheapness of the refutation.** "We can dismiss this view without further ado" tells the reader not to expect a campaign — one counterexample will do. The phrase sets the right tempo and signals confidence without bluster.
- **The flag is a promissory note the case must pay.** Saying "without further ado" only works because *Boat* really is decisive; the rhetoric and the evidence are matched. Don't write the flag for a case that needs three more moves.
- **Rig the numbers so the verdict is forced.** £20 to save a life, against a £5,000 counterfactual duty — the gap is so large that "X is surely required" needs no defense. The case is engineered, not merely chosen.
- **Concede a sliver, deny the magnitude.** "Even if Y's failure makes *some* difference … it cannot make *that much* difference" leaves the opponent a face-saving grain of truth while removing the result they needed. The dismissal is total but not crude.

## What to borrow
When a view dies to one clean case, say so ("We can dismiss this without further ado…") and then make the case carry it — engineer the stakes so the verdict is forced, and grant the opponent a small "even if" before denying the magnitude they need.

# Annotated exemplar — Clark, "The Demands of Necessity" (flat verdict sentences)

## Source
- **Author / Work:** David James Clark, "The Demands of Necessity," *Ethics* 133, no. 4 (July 2023): 473–96 · suggested cite key: `clark2023demands` (verify against the source before citing)
- **Why this passage:** After laying out the *Easy Defense* case, Clark states the moral verdict in a bare three-word sentence, then defers the diagnosis to the sentences that follow.

## The passage
> Pedestrian acts impermissibly. The problem, however, isn't that it is disproportionate to paralyze an attacker to defend against paralysis. Rather, Pedestrian's action is impermissible because she had available another defensive option…

## What makes it work (the lesson)
- **Verdict first, on its own.** The three-word sentence "Pedestrian acts impermissibly." stands alone — no hedge, no "it seems," no clause explaining why yet. The reader registers the judgment before the analysis begins.
- **The full stop does the rhetorical work.** Ending the sentence right after the verdict forces a beat. The diagnosis that follows reads as a separate, deliberate move, not as a qualifier smuggled into the same breath.
- **"The problem, however, isn't…" clears the wrong diagnosis first.** Clark uses the flat verdict as a launchpad: he rules out the tempting (proportionality) explanation before supplying his own. Verdict → wrong diagnosis ruled out → right diagnosis.
- **Confidence is calibrated to the case, not the prose.** The case is built so the intuition is uncontroversial; the bare sentence trusts the reader to share it rather than over-arguing.

## What to borrow
After you present a case, give the verdict its own short declarative sentence ("X acts impermissibly.") and let the next sentence start the diagnosis — don't fuse judgment and explanation into one hedged clause. Cf. `Craft/prose-casebook.md` ("One short declarative…").

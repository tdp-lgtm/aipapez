# Annotated exemplar — Isserow, "Pride and Investment" (parenthetical wit + property-first opening)

## Source
- **Author / Work:** Jessica Isserow, "Pride and Investment," *Ethics* 135, no. 2 (January 2025): 259–[end] · suggested cite key: `isserow2025pride` (verify against the source before citing)
- **Why this passage:** A parenthetical aside marks a limit on the phenomenon with a wink, and the paper opens by leading with a vivid property of the thing studied rather than with the literature.

## The passage
> But one surely cannot be proud of Jupiter for being the largest planet in our solar system, or of one's office door for being sturdy. (Pride may be promiscuous, but it is not that promiscuous.)

## What makes it work (the lesson)
- **The parenthetical does the conceptual work, lightly.** The aside isn't ornament: it states the desideratum the account must respect — pride extends widely *but not without limit*. Putting the constraint in parentheses signals "obvious once said," which is exactly the tone a limiting case wants.
- **Reuse your own keyword for the joke.** The wit reruns the chapter's term of art ("promiscuous") with an intensifier ("that promiscuous"). The humor is free because it's built from vocabulary the reader already owns; nothing new has to be learned to get it.
- **Absurd concrete examples set up the line.** Jupiter and a sturdy office door are deliberately silly instances; the parenthetical then names *why* they fail without belaboring it. Vivid wrong cases first, terse diagnosis second.
- **Open property-first, with the author as the specimen.** The article begins "Pride is impressively promiscuous; we can extract it from just about anything" and immediately uses the author's own manga collection and Yorkshire tan as data — a property-led opening that motivates the puzzle (promiscuity vs. parochial self-focus) before any citation appears.

## What to borrow
Lead a section with a striking property of your target ("X is impressively Y"), illustrate with cheerfully concrete cases, and when you need to fence off the property's limit, do it in a short parenthetical that reuses your own keyword with a wink ("(X may be Y, but it is not *that* Y.)") — the constraint lands as common sense, not as a new burden.

# Annotated exemplar — Srinivasan, "The Aptness of Anger" (declarative polemic that upgrades a phenomenon)

## Source
- **Author / Work:** Amia Srinivasan, "The Aptness of Anger," *Journal of Political Philosophy* 26, no. 2 (2018): 123–144 · suggested cite key: `srinivasan2018aptness` (verify against the source before citing)
- **Why this passage:** A pair of flat declaratives reclassifies a dismissed phenomenon ("insults") as something theoretically loaded ("rhetorical strategies"), raising the stakes before any machinery arrives. The paper also opens under a charged epigraph (Ephesians 4:26, "Be angry, but sin not.") that frames the whole argument.

## The passage
> The misogynist dismisses a woman's anger by calling her shrill or strident; the racist dismisses the black person's anger by calling him a thug or an animal. These are not mere insults. These are rhetorical strategies that shift the explanatory context for the subject's anger from the space of reasons to the space of causes.

## What makes it work (the lesson)
- **Negate the deflationary reading, then assert the inflationary one.** "These are not mere insults. These are rhetorical strategies…" is a two-beat upgrade: the short negation clears the cheap reading off the table, and the parallel declarative installs the loaded one in its place.
- **Two short clipped sentences after a long itemized one.** The preceding sentence is long and concrete (the misogynist *and* the racist, with their actual epithets); the verdict lands in two abrupt declaratives. The rhythm — long, then short-short — is what makes the reclassification feel like a gavel.
- **Earn the upgrade with a precise technical payoff, not just heat.** The second sentence immediately cashes out *why* they are strategies ("shift the explanatory context … from the space of reasons to the space of causes"). The polemic is doing analytic work, so it reads as a thesis, not a slogan.
- **A charged epigraph can pre-load the stakes for free.** Ephesians 4:26 puts the central tension (anger can be apt *and* a moral risk) in the reader's ear before sentence one, so the argument feels answerable to something larger.

## What to borrow
When a referee might wave a phenomenon away as trivial, name the dismissive reading in a short negation ("This is not mere X") and replace it with a precise theoretical claim in a matching declarative ("This is Y, which does Z") — and let the sentence *immediately after* pay out the technical cash, so the upgrade is argued rather than merely asserted.

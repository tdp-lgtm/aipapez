# Annotated exemplar — Muñoz, "The Paradox of Duties to Oneself" (slogan compression)

## Source
- **Author / Work:** Daniel Muñoz, "The Paradox of Duties to Oneself," forthcoming in *Australasian Journal of Philosophy* · suggested cite key: `munoz_dutiestooneself` (verify against the source before citing)
- **Why this passage:** After quoting a dense paragraph from Cohen on Hobbes, Muñoz distills the whole point into a four-beat maxim the reader can carry away.

## The passage
> In a slogan: 'can get free' doesn't mean 'free already'.

## What makes it work (the lesson)
- **Quote the dense version, then crush it to a slogan.** The block quote from Cohen does the careful work (repealing a law ≠ being unbound by it now); the one-liner makes it portable. The maxim only feels earned *because* the rigorous statement preceded it.
- **The "In a slogan:" tag is a contract with the reader.** It openly signals "here is the compression," so the reader files it as a memory aid rather than mistaking it for the argument. The label licenses the brevity.
- **Antithetical, near-rhyming parallelism makes it stick.** "'Can get free' doesn't mean 'free already'" pivots on the shared word *free* across a tight contrast; the symmetry is what makes it repeatable. A good slogan has structure, not just shortness.
- **Reuse the device as a refrain.** Muñoz deploys the same "In a slogan:" move again later ("reasons have to be stable over our decisions"), turning it into a structural signpost across the paper — the reader learns that each one marks a load-bearing claim.

## What to borrow
After you've stated a subtle distinction carefully (ideally just after a quotation that earns it), give the reader a portable maxim flagged with something like "In a slogan:" — and build the slogan on a tight antithesis around a repeated keyword ("'can get free' ≠ 'free already'") so it sticks. Use the tag consistently so it becomes a signpost for your key claims. Cf. `Craft/prose-casebook.md` on compression and memorable formulations.

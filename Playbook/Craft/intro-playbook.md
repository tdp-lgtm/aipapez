# Intro Playbook — openings, motivation, and framing the question

The introduction is the highest-leverage page of the paper. Referees form a
verdict-shaped impression within it. Its job: make the reader *feel a problem*,
believe it matters, and learn what you'll argue — fast.

> For the corpus-grounded conventions, see `journal-craft.md` §1–2; the opening architectures are
> in `moves-catalog.md` Part 1. This file is the practical drill-down for building and fixing an
> opening. Adapted from the entrant's `acdemwrit` writing system.

---

## The four jobs of an intro (in roughly this order)

1. **Create the problem.** Get the reader to feel a tension, puzzle, or gap —
   *before* you name your thesis. The reader should think "huh, that *is* a
   problem" on their own.
2. **Establish the stakes.** Why should a busy person care? Tie it to something
   they already believe, a practice they care about, or a debate they're in.
3. **State the thesis plainly.** Usually by the bottom of page one: "In this
   paper I argue that **P**." Don't bury it. Boldness reads as confidence;
   confidence (when earned) reads as competence.
4. **Orient.** A light roadmap and an explicit scope statement.

## Opening moves that work (pick one)

Top papers rarely open by announcing a topic ("This paper is about X"). They open
with a *move*:

- **The puzzle / inconsistent triad.** "Here are three things we're inclined to
  believe. They can't all be true." Then the paper is the resolution. Powerful
  because the reader is hooked by their *own* commitments.
- **The vivid case.** A concrete scenario that pumps an intuition — a stipulated case built for
  the job, or a well-chosen real example. State immediately what the case is meant to show. Best
  when one clean case carries the paper.
- **The bold thesis.** Lead with a surprising claim stated flatly, then spend the
  paper earning it. Works when the thesis is genuinely counterintuitive.
- **The "they-say / I-say."** "It is widely held that Q. I argue this is
  mistaken." Cleanly situates the contribution; the workhorse opening.
- **The diagnosis.** "Everyone treats this debate as about X. I think it's really
  about Y." Reframings are high-value when they land.
- **The gap opening.** "Much has been written about X; almost nothing about Y" —
  legitimate as the literal opening *when the gap itself is the surprise and
  carries the motivation* (the sharp gap opening, `moves-catalog.md` Part 1; DIGEST §8 Openings).
  Limp gap-framing with no edge stays banned.

Avoid: the dictionary opening, the throat-clearing history of the problem ("Since
Aristotle…"), and the topic announcement with no tension.

## Motivating the question (making it feel important)

A question feels important when the reader sees that **their own commitments are
at risk**. Techniques:

- **Show a cost.** "If Q is true, then [something the reader values] is
  unjustified / incoherent / hypocritical."
- **Surface a hidden assumption.** "This whole debate assumes A. But A is far
  from obvious."
- **Make two intuitions collide.** Pair two things the reader independently
  accepts and show they conflict.
- **Connect to a real practice or case.** Moral and political philosophy has the
  advantage that the stakes are often concrete (justice, harm, rights, the law).
  Use it — but keep the case in service of the argument, not decoration.

The point worth internalizing: academic writing earns its keep by
creating value for a *community of expert readers* — it changes what they think.
Your intro's job is to show *those* readers that they have a problem they didn't
fully see, and that you can move them on it.

## Stating the thesis

- **Plainly and early.** "I argue that P." Resist the urge to defer the thesis
  for suspense; this isn't fiction.
- **At the right grain.** Specific enough to be contestable, narrow enough to be
  defensible (see scope discipline in `journal-craft.md` §3).
- **With the foil attached.** "…against the widely held view that Q." The contrast
  is what makes P informative.

## The roadmap

**A roadmap paragraph ends the intro — that's the field convention and the
house rule** (`journal-craft.md` §7 owns this; DIGEST §2 agrees). Keep it
functional, two to four sentences, sections named precisely. Elegance tips:
give each section's *job*, not just its topic ("Section 3 answers the
strongest objection: that…"), and weave motivation in where you can — but
never weave the roadmap away entirely; referees navigate by it.

Always include an explicit **scope line**: what you will and won't argue. This is
the cheapest insurance against the "overreaches" rejection.

## A worked template (adapt heavily — do not fill in mechanically)

> [Vivid case or puzzle that creates a tension — 2–4 sentences.]
> [Name the tension explicitly; show what's at stake if it isn't resolved.]
> [The standard view Q, stated charitably, and why it fails to resolve the
> tension.]
> [Thesis: "In this paper I argue that P." + the core of R, in one sentence.]
> [Scope: what I am not claiming.]
> [Light roadmap, 2–3 sentences.]

## Red flags in a draft intro

- The thesis doesn't appear, or appears only on page 3.
- The reader is told the topic is important but not *shown* a problem.
- The foil (Q) is missing or is a strawman.
- The intro promises more than the paper delivers (scope mismatch).
- Throat-clearing: history, definitions, or limp "much has been written
  about…" (a *sharp* gap opening is allowed when the gap is the hook —
  DIGEST §8 Openings).

# Prose Principles — clear, concise, effective sentences and paragraphs

Sentence- and paragraph-level craft for analytic philosophy. The goal: prose a
skeptical referee understands on the first read, with no wasted motion. This
draws on the tradition of Williams (*Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*),
Pinker (*The Sense of Style*), and the plain-spoken clarity of the field's best
stylists.

> The move bank that makes this concrete is `moves-catalog.md` Part 2. Adapted from the entrant's
> `acdemwrit` writing system, anonymized per the Craft anonymity rule (`README.md`). Standing rule:
> techniques, never sentences.

## Paragraphs

- **One claim per paragraph.** The first sentence states it; the rest earns it.
  If you can't say what a paragraph's claim is, split or cut it.
- **The given/new contract.** Start a sentence with information the reader
  already has (the "given"); end with the new information. This is what makes
  paragraphs feel like they flow rather than lurch. Old-to-new, sentence after
  sentence, is most of what "good flow" actually is.
- **Make transitions logical, not decorative.** "Moreover" and "furthermore"
  usually mark a list. The strong connectives mark *inference*: "because," "so,"
  "but," "even so," "if that's right, then." Use them to expose the argument's
  joints.

## Sentences

- **Who does what to whom.** Prefer concrete subjects and active verbs. Bury the
  agent in a nominalization ("there is a violation of the principle by the
  action") and the reader loses the thread. Surface it ("the action violates the
  principle").
- **Put the main clause first** for key claims; let subordinate detail follow.
  Save complex, left-branching sentences for material the reader can afford to
  hold in suspense.
- **One idea per sentence for the load-bearing claims.** Complexity is fine for
  detail; it's deadly for the move the whole argument turns on. State pivotal
  claims short and flat.
- **Vary length deliberately.** A short sentence after several long ones lands.
  Uniform medium-length sentences are the texture of forgettable prose (and of
  AI). Read aloud; if the rhythm is flat, vary it.

## Words

- **Repeat your key terms; don't elegantly vary them.** In philosophy, calling
  the same thing "obligation," then "duty," then "requirement" invites the reader
  to wonder whether you mean three things. Fix a term and keep it.
- **Define once, then use.** Introduce a technical term where it's first needed,
  define it crisply, and don't redefine it later.
- **Cut the throat-clearing.** "It is important to note that," "It should be
  emphasized that," "As we have seen," "In order to" → usually deletable with no
  loss. (See `ai-tells.md`.)
- **Prefer the plain word.** "Use" not "utilize"; "show" not "demonstrate" when
  you mean show; "about" not "with respect to" when you can.
- **Make antecedents obvious.** The reader never investigates a pronoun's
  provenance: "this," "it," and "that view" each need one unambiguous referent
  in easy reach. When in doubt, repeat the noun (key-term repetition above —
  it's the same discipline).

## Hedging — the philosopher's specific trap

Philosophers hedge for two very different reasons, and only one is good:

- **Good hedging is precision.** "Roughly," "in most cases," "other things
  equal," "on one natural reading" — these *narrow the claim* and are part of
  arguing carefully. Keep them.
- **Bad hedging is anxiety.** "I think it might perhaps be the case that arguably
  P." Stacked qualifiers that don't narrow anything; they just signal fear of
  being wrong. Cut them. State P, then defend it.

Test: does the hedge change the *content* of the claim (good) or only its
*confidence* (usually bad)? Keep the first kind; delete the second. One
exception (DIGEST §8): an *intuitive case verdict* keeps one light epistemic
hedge ("It seems plausible that…") even though it only lowers confidence —
that's register, not anxiety.

## Concision

- **Cut what the argument doesn't need.** Every sentence should advance the
  thesis or defend a premise. Background that the target reader already has is
  cuttable.
- **One pass for cutting, separately.** After drafting, do a dedicated pass whose
  only job is to remove words. Aim to cut 10–20% with no loss of content; you
  almost always can.
- **Footnotes carry the load you can't cut but don't need inline.** Qualifications
  and literature acknowledgments belong below the line — but not arguments: if a
  footnote argues, promote it or cut it (DIGEST §8 Footnotes).

## The Strunk core (1918), field-calibrated

The durable tenth of *The Elements of Style*, folded in here so nothing needs
the 12k-token original. Adopted, with calibration:

- **Omit needless words — by contraction, not amputation.** The classic swaps:
  "the fact that" → cut or recast; "the question as to whether" → "whether";
  "owing to the fact that" → "because"; "he is a man who" → "he"; "in a hasty
  manner" → "hastily"; "there is no doubt but that" → "no doubt"; "used for X
  purposes" → "used for X". What this rule does *not* license: cutting hedges
  that narrow content (see Hedging above) or connectives that expose inference.
- **Positive form — for descriptions.** "Did not pay attention to" → "ignored";
  "not honest" → "dishonest". Calibration: this governs *description*, not
  *denial*. Negative theses and scope sentences ("My claim is not that…") are
  field practice and house style; they stay.
- **Parallel form for parallel ideas.** Coordinate claims, rival views, and
  matched cases deserve matched syntax, so the reader parses the structure once
  and reuses it. (The attack-one-premise displayed-rival template is this rule at
  paper scale; minimal pairs are this rule at case scale.)
- **Keep related words together.** Subject near verb, modifier next to what it
  modifies; distance breeds ambiguity — "she only said the premise was
  plausible" vs. "she said only the premise was plausible."
- **Emphasis lives at the end.** The given→new contract's other face: the
  sentence's final position is its stressed position, so park the word that
  matters there, not a citation or an afterthought.

Rejected, deliberately: the blanket passive-voice ban (the agent-first rule
above is the accurate version — a passive is right when the patient is the
topic); the that/which superstition; the dated usage lists; and "omit needless
words" read as a taste for terseness — condensed prose is load-bearing prose,
not short prose (see the house-style throughline below).

## The house style you're aiming for (snappy, clear, condensed)

Distilled from the field's admired stylists (kept anonymous per the Craft anonymity rule) plus §10
of `journal-craft.md`. These are the moves that make the style work — techniques to execute in the
essay's own material.

- **Short declaratives for the load-bearing beats.** Let cadence carry weight. When a point
  matters, give it its own short sentence — sometimes its own one-line paragraph. The white space
  is doing work.
- **Open plain, then turn.** Begin with an almost conversational claim the reader accepts, then
  pivot on a "but" into the tension. The turn is where the paper starts.
- **State the thesis flat — no hedging.** Save qualifiers for the inferences, not the thesis.
- **One vivid metaphor, used once.** A single memorable image fixes the stakes. Don't pile them up;
  one earns its keep, three are a tell.
- **Rhetorical question only as a pivot, answered immediately and flatly.** Never leave one hanging
  as if it were an argument.
- **Concede, then narrow.** Grant the objection in plain words, then say exactly what you still
  hold. Conceding buys authority.
- **Make the abstract concrete with a homely image.** Cash out a technical point in something
  physical and everyday — a plain-words parenthetical can make a formal principle undeniable in
  passing.
- **Coin at most one term; define it once; then repeat it.** Don't elegantly vary your key term.
  When a concept is technical, *visualize* it (give the reader an operation to perform on an
  example) rather than leaning on the formalism.
- **End on the strong word, and allow yourself a crisp closing beat.** A short, slightly wry
  sign-off is permitted once the work is done — *if the body earned it*, and one at most (DIGEST §8
  Closings).

The throughline: plain words, short sentences at the pivots, confidence at the thesis and humility
at the inferences, and exactly one image per idea. Condensed prose is not prose with the
connectives ripped out — it's prose where every sentence is load-bearing.

### The warm register (warmth with rigor — an alternative register for socially textured topics)

Where the default register strips cases to schematics, some essays — on topics living in ordinary
social experience — do better in a warmer register that builds social realism into the argument
itself while staying analytically exact:

- **Vignette openings with comic timing**, inside ordinary experience before any literature; the
  pacing (even the typography) can perform the phenomenon.
- **The theory epigram**: once the account is built, compress it into two or three parallel
  subject–verb–object beats the reader carries away.
- **One governing metaphor per paper**, coined once and worked relentlessly; every view baptized on
  arrival ("Call this the … account").
- **Textured cases with load-bearing detail**: named ordinary people, dialogue, domestic and
  workplace reality — and the punchline of a case can *be* the philosophy. **Caution:** every
  detail must do normative work; color without function is padding.
- **Objections voiced in the reader's second person** ("How could that be a wrong, you might
  object…"), answered concessively but firmly; blunt verdicts on rivals stay available.
- **Homely-analogy deflation**: an opponent's over-reaching inference dies in one domestic analogy,
  no apparatus.
- **Cadence tools**: zeugma yoking abstract and concrete; the emphatic tail ("…full stop"); wry
  parentheticals as tonal relief.
- **Closings that re-perform the coinage or call back the title** rather than summarize.
- **Structural note**: this register pairs naturally with the reverse-engineering arc
  (`moves-catalog.md` Part 1) rather than the case-verdict-principle arc. Humour targets situations
  and bad views, never persons.

## A self-edit sequence (for the Stage-7 clarity pass and every quality pass)

1. **Claim check** — does each paragraph have one statable claim up front?
2. **Flow check** — does each sentence open with the given and end with the new?
3. **Agent check** — are subjects concrete and verbs active on the key sentences?
4. **Term check** — are key terms repeated, not elegantly varied?
5. **Hedge check** — are the qualifiers narrowing content or just lowering
   confidence? Cut the latter.
6. **Cut check** — remove 10–20% of words.
7. **AI-tell check** — run against `ai-tells.md`.
8. **Read-aloud check** — does the rhythm vary, or is it flat?

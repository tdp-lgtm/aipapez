# Moves Catalog — argument architectures and prose moves

The distilled move-bank of this workspace: the transferable techniques of top analytic philosophy,
written as **anonymous, abstract rules**. Each move was originally distilled from published papers;
per house rule (see `README.md` in this folder), the playbook names no authors and no papers — the
lesson is kept, the attribution and the quotations are not. These are architectures and techniques to
**adapt to the essay's own material**, never templates to fill mechanically; a good essay uses two or
three of them because the argument needs them, not a parade.

Pick from Part 1 at Plan/Outline time (Stages 3–4) by matching the move to the essay's contribution
type; pick from Part 2 while drafting and in the clarity passes (Stages 5–7).

---

## Part 1 — Structural moves (argument architectures)

### Openings and framing

**The paired-cases opening.** Open by eliciting the target intuition with a tightly matched pair of
cases — identical but for the one feature you claim does the moral work — introduced with a bare
"Compare:" and an "As before, except…" variant. The pair *does the framing* before any thesis: the
reader's diverging verdicts can only be tracking the one difference, the constraint is demonstrated
before it is named, and "Compare:" recruits the reader as a co-reasoner. Keep the furniture minimal;
nothing in a vignette is decorative.

**The taxonomy-first opening.** When the contribution is a new *position*, open by mapping and
naming the existing options, a paragraph each — then introduce your view as the determinate spot on
the map the others leave open. The map creates the vacancy; naming the positions makes the whole
dialectic navigable; and if contrast cases carve the taxonomy (the positions separated by how they
treat the same paired cases), the map is argued, not asserted. In a sprawling literature, map only
the two or three positions the contribution must displace.

**The gap opening (sharp form only).** Name what the field has done and set it against the precise
question it has skipped, in parallel syntax that varies one term ("Much has been written on when X
is permitted. Much less on when X is required."). The near-identity of the clauses makes the
unfilled space unmistakable — the opening *is* the motivation. Immediately credit the predominant
view (with citations) so the gap reads as a genuine omission in a live debate, not a convenient
absence. The limp version ("much has been written about X" with no edge) stays banned.

**The named-view-first opening.** State your position by its proper name in the first sentence,
gloss it in one sentence immediately after, then give a numbered "In Section n, I…" roadmap that
previews the *dialectic* (where the rival appears, where the dilemma bites), not just the table of
contents. Bake the qualifications into the thesis as it is stated, so scope is built in rather than
bolted on.

**The stakes-first opening.** For a stakes-laden topic, make the harm concrete and real before any
theory: stack two parallel counterfactuals with a repeated clause, put real numbers and named
particulars at the sentence's end (the position of maximum weight), hold the most affecting phrase
to the terminal position — then pose the abstract question. Keep one honest hedge so the framing
reads as motivation, not melodrama.

### Positive-account structures

**The reverse-engineering arc.** For account-*building* papers: open by cataloguing the platitudes
of the practice (the data everyone accepts — the referee signs off on the test-set before seeing the
theory); state your hypothesis; give clarification its own stage (separating what the hypothesis
*says* from whether it is *true* pre-empts half the misreading-objections); then put the hypothesis
to work against each platitude in turn; rivals arrive last, judged against the same test-set.

**The coverage-rubric skeleton.** When several worries cluster around one puzzle, coin a closed,
named checklist of desiderata early (three to five capitalized Challenges or conditions). The same
list then does triple duty: the desiderata any answer must meet, the paper's section skeleton, and
the demolition tool turned on rivals — each rival is scored against it line by line. Framing the
desiderata as jointly hard to satisfy pre-sells the achievement of a view that meets all of them.

**The iteratively revised principle.** When a principle needs several qualifications, don't present
the polished final version: state the simple core, break it with a case, add exactly the clause that
case demands, label the new version (visible version numbers), and repeat — narrating each "this is
still too simple" out loud. Every amendment is *earned* by a counterexample the reader watched fail;
the obvious objection is already on the page, generating the next clause.

**Elimination as derivation.** Reach the positive view as the *residue*: sort rivals into a few
named families, defeat each with matched minimal-pair cases, and give the defeats a single repeated
diagnosis (each rival locates the wrong thing in the wrong place) — the shared diagnosis both
unifies the demolition and motivates the survivor. Pair with an explicit numbered roadmap whose
stages map one-to-one onto sections.

**The firm-to-soft bridge.** When a claim in a soft domain (where intuitions are murky) is
contested, find the corresponding claim in a firm domain (where the reader's judgments are already
stable) and build the explicit a-fortiori bridge — announcing the method as you use it, so the
better-understood question visibly stands proxy for the disputed one.

**Stipulation blocks and reader-modularity.** After each load-bearing case, declare assumptions
explicitly and in sequence ("Assume, for now… Assume further…"), then discharge them — nothing
smuggled, no hidden premises for a referee to find. And wherever the argument brushes contested
background theory, add a modularity sentence: readers may substitute their preferred account;
verdicts may shift at the margins, the thesis stands. Both moves shrink the attack surface without
weakening the thesis.

### Critical structures

**Attack one premise.** When refuting rivals who share your ideal: quote the rival exactly, rebuild
it as a *valid* displayed argument (charity first — no strawman charge possible), then localize the
whole dispute to the single premise that goes beyond the shared ideal and deny exactly that. Reuse
the same displayed template across rivals, swapping only the crucial premise: three refutations
become one repeated, auditable engine. Objections to your *own* premises go to footnotes, keeping
the engine clean.

**The internal-objections method.** Defeat a rival by its own standards, not an outside intuition:
find the objection the view has *already absorbed and answered*, then show it fails by its own
criterion in the opposite direction. State the result as a tight two-beat inversion ("not too
restrictive; too permissive"). Concede the view's animating insight first — the concession proves
you understood what you are dismantling.

**The counterexample march.** For a section or paper whose job is destroying a general principle:
classify the counterexamples into *kinds*, announce the plan (so a long negative campaign reads as
a plan, not a pile), and after each blow rebuild the opponent's best weakened version *yourself*
before breaking it in turn. The rival dies by exhaustion of its best forms; the conclusion arrives
as the only survivor. Choose the opening counterexample for fairness and memorability — ideally one
from the principle's own home turf.

**The named-dilemma engine.** If the contribution is a *problem* rather than a fix: coin and name
the dilemma early, name each horn so it recurs as a referent, state the horns as matched
conditionals (If X… / If not-X…) so the trap is visible, and earn the right not to resolve it by
showing it bites a wider swath of views than anyone noticed. The contribution is the pressure, not
a verdict.

**The dialectic as the contribution.** When the real contribution is mapping a problem's dialectical
space: build the section as escalating rescues you raise *on the opponent's behalf* ("could the
defender say…?"), defeat each with a named, reusable distinction, and end by conceding which rescue
survives best. The map of replies plus a ranking *is* the payoff — and steelmanning the loser makes
the defeat feel earned.

**Reply-layering.** When one objection deserves multiple rounds, run the exchange explicitly
labelled — "Here is a reply. … Here is a response. … Here is a reply to the response." — one fresh
stipulated case per layer, stopping one layer after your strongest ground. Depth on one objection
beats width over many shallow ones.

**Pre-emptive defense.** When the thesis will be resisted on sight, rebut the *deflationary
readings* before building the positive case — the most dangerous objection is not "you're wrong"
but "you've redescribed something familiar." Answer each deflation with a purpose-built minimal
variant case, stage the skeptical block as a visible queue dispatched in order, and phrase the
thesis with an explicit "can"/sufficient-not-necessary scope so you defend no more than you need.

**Concede-and-scope.** When a critic's premise is empirical and yours is normative, grant the
premise explicitly and in full, "in order to focus on" the question that remains — the concession
buys immunity to empirical counterevidence and carves the thesis visibly rather than dodging. (And
consider the braided close: end where you began, one line that reframes the opening in light of
everything argued.)

## Part 2 — Prose moves (sentences and paragraphs)

### Openings

**Plain claim, then the turn.** Open with an almost conversational claim the reader already
accepts; pivot on a "But…" into the tension the paper resolves; name the target view; state the
thesis in one flat declarative — all within the first page, before any apparatus. A rhetorical
question may make the pivot, answered immediately and flatly on the next line.

**Two camps, one-word stances.** Locate a crowded debate in two sentences: one named representative
per side, each with a one-word stance ("Most say yes… Some say no."), then a short pivot question
driving to the flat thesis ("Neither is right."). Maximum orientation, minimum words.

**The misdirection opening.** When a puzzle can be *felt*, stage it: narrate something that sounds
outrageous, then pull the rug with a one-line reveal, so the reader experiences the puzzle before
being told it. Then name the puzzle in one short question.

**The vignette opening.** Open inside the ordinary experience the argument is about — scene before
literature, with the typography and pacing performing the phenomenon where possible. Discipline:
every vivid detail must be doing normative work; color without function is padding.

**Property-first opening.** Lead with a striking property of the target ("X is impressively Y"),
illustrate with cheerfully concrete — even deliberately silly — cases, and fence off the property's
limit in a short parenthetical that reuses your own keyword with a wink. The constraint lands as
common sense, not a new burden.

### Verdicts, concessions, and objections

**Flat verdict sentences.** After a case, give the verdict its own short declarative sentence and a
full stop; let the *next* sentence start the diagnosis. Never fuse judgment and explanation into
one hedged clause. Build the case so the intuition is uncontroversial, and let the bare sentence
trust the reader. Useful sequel: rule out the tempting wrong diagnosis before supplying the right
one.

**Concede, then narrow.** Grant the objection fully and in plain words, then name exactly the
narrower thing you still hold ("I concede X. But I am not claiming X, only Y."). Total, unembarrassed
concession followed by precise narrowing buys authority for everything around it.

**Grant the conditional, fight the antecedent.** When an opponent's argument has the form "if P
then Q," say out loud that you grant the conditional — then attack P. The concession shows exactly
where the real disagreement lives and turns the opponent's own logic into your lever.

**Negative specification.** When the thesis is easily over-read, fence off the strong misreading in
your own voice before a referee supplies it: "To be clear: my claim is not that…". Saying what the
claim is *not* does precision work a flat assertion cannot.

**The built-in fallback.** For every load-bearing claim, know — and say — which weaker version
would still deliver the conclusion: "This is stronger than I need; even if only [weaker claim], it
follows that…," and mark modest theses explicitly ("My claim is just that…"). The honest sibling of
overclaiming: state the interesting version *and* show which weaker version carries the paper.

**The quick kill.** When a view dies to one clean case, say so ("We can dismiss this view without
further ado") and let the case carry it — engineer the stakes so the verdict is forced, and grant
the opponent a small "even if" before denying the magnitude they need. The flag is a promissory
note the case must actually pay; never write it for a case that needs three more moves.

**Witty deflation (earned only).** When an objection survives only by demanding something
preposterous, spell out the preposterous requirement plainly, then deflate it with one vivid label
in an "X, not Y" frame — and only after taking the view seriously, so the line reads as a verdict,
not a sneer. At most once or twice per essay.

**Diagnose the rival's appeal.** When a rival view is intuitive, spend one sentence explaining
*why* it seems right before showing it isn't ("Small wonder the view attracts…"). Explaining the
intuition away defuses it far better than denying it.

### Compression, signposting, and cadence

**The self-graded signpost.** At the pivot from ground-clearing to the main event, grade the
objections publicly in one breath: "So much for the easy objection. Now the real challenges." Two
fragments, no connective tissue — dispatch, rank, advance. Once or twice per essay.

**Slogan compression.** After stating a subtle distinction carefully, hand the reader a portable
maxim flagged openly ("In a slogan: …"), built on a tight antithesis around a repeated keyword. The
label licenses the brevity (the reader files it as a memory aid, not the argument), and reusing the
tag turns it into a signpost for load-bearing claims.

**The pivot paragraph.** Give a pivotal structural claim its own one-line paragraph ("But these
principles are different, and the difference matters."). The cadence does the underlining; no
"crucially" needed.

**One image, worked once — or worked throughout.** Either a single vivid metaphor used once to fix
the stakes, or one governing image coined once and worked consistently for the whole essay. Never a
new image per paragraph; when one metaphor absorbs a cluster of similar objections, list the
variants tersely inside it and bookend the list with the upshot, so the variants sit inside a
thesis sandwich.

**Visualize the formalism.** Before any axiom, lemma, or formal definition, write one sentence
promising a concrete picture ("There is an easy way to see this"), then give an operation the
reader can perform on an example already on the page — never just restate the symbols in words. A
parenthetical plain-words gloss of a formal principle ("(better for one, worse for none)") can make
it undeniable in passing.

**The theory epigram.** Once an account is fully built, compress it into a two-or-three-beat
parallel epigram the reader can carry away (subject–verb–object, three short strokes). Earned
compression only — it arrives after the machinery, as the payoff, never in place of it.

**The declarative upgrade.** When a phenomenon risks being waved off as trivial, clear the
deflationary reading in a short negation and install the loaded one in a matching declarative
("These are not mere X. These are Y."), with the very next sentence paying out the technical cash —
so the upgrade is argued, not asserted. Rhythm: one long concrete sentence, then two short ones.

**The triad of short sentences.** Let three clipped parallel sentences carry a key inference
instead of one long subordinated sentence — the parallel structure does the inferential work
without connectives. (One triad; a page of them is a tell — see `ai-tells.md`.)

## Using the catalog

- **Match move to contribution type** (DIGEST §1): refuting rivals who share an ideal →
  attack-one-premise; building an account → reverse-engineering arc; a reframing → two-camps +
  diagnosis; a problem paper → named-dilemma engine; a principle with qualifications → iterative
  revision; and so on.
- **Two or three structural moves per essay, chosen for the argument.** More reads as pastiche of
  the field.
- **Part 2 is seasoning, under the Style Guide's snap budget**: each attention-drawing cadence move
  at most once or twice per essay, never in adjacent paragraphs; most paragraphs end workmanlike.
  The tie-breaker is always invisible over clever.
- **Growth:** at Stage 12 (or whenever a reading shows a technique this catalog lacks), add the
  move — described abstractly, no authors, no papers, no quotes. If a move can't be stated without
  its source's words, it isn't transferable enough to add.

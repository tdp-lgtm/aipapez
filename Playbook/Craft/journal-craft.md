## Writing for Top Analytic Moral and Political Philosophy Journals

A craft-level synthesis drawn from twenty-five articles published in (or in the orbit of) *Ethics*, *Philosophy & Public Affairs*, *Journal of Philosophy*, *Philosophical Review*, *Philosophy & Phenomenological Research*, *Oxford Studies in Normative Ethics*, *Oxford Studies in Political Philosophy*, *Law and Philosophy*, and *Journal of Political Philosophy*. The goal is not to summarise what these papers say, but to extract how they are written, structured, and argued. Use this guide both as a checklist while drafting and as a yardstick against which to test your own drafts.

---

## 1. Opening moves and problem framing

**Dominant convention.** The strongest papers open in one of three modes — and almost never with abstract throat-clearing about the importance of the topic. The three modes are: (i) **an immediate case** that ostensively isolates the puzzle ("Consider the following case: …"); (ii) **a sharply posed question or apparent contradiction** ("Why aren't we constantly violating our own rights?"); or (iii) **a vivid real-world event** that is treated as a hook only long enough to introduce the conceptual puzzle. The opening is short — typically half a page to two pages — and ends with the thesis or a clear roadmap. There is no literature review preceding the thesis; literature engagement is woven in *after* the problem has been made vivid. Stakes are established implicitly, by the bite of the case or the sharpness of the contradiction, not by claims like "this issue is of great importance".

**Examples from the corpus.**

- *Clark, "The Demands of Necessity"* opens with two paired cases — *Fast and Furious* and *Fast and Less Furious* — within the first half-page. The cases differ minimally; the contrast does all the framing work. By the second page the proportionality/necessity distinction is on the table, and by the third Clark has stated his unifying thesis.
- *Muñoz & Baron-Schmitt, "Wronging Oneself"* open in the first person with a deliberately absurd-sounding catalogue ("Earlier today, without asking permission, I took a bike belonging to someone in my neighborhood…") and then deflate it ("this 'neighbor' of mine was me"). The puzzle — why don't I wrong myself? — is in plain view before any technical apparatus enters.
- *Howard, "Dangerous Speech"* opens with two real atrocities (San Bernardino, Christchurch), then poses the legal-philosophical question ("Is it morally permissible for the state to suppress expression to prevent the harms it risks inspiring?"). The events function as motivation, not evidence; he moves to the conceptual puzzle within a page.
- *Srinivasan, "The Aptness of Anger"* opens with the 1965 Baldwin–Buckley debate, lets Baldwin's voice carry the opening rhetorically, and only then names the philosophical question (whether anger can be apt even when counterproductive).

**Practical takeaway.** Within your first paragraph, the reader should see either a fully specified case, a sharply stated puzzle, or a concrete event you will quickly abstract from. Do not begin with "There has been much debate about X" or "X is an important topic". By the bottom of page two — and ideally page one — the puzzle should be vivid enough that a reader can already paraphrase what your paper has to settle.

## 2. Thesis presentation

**Dominant convention.** The thesis lands early — almost always within the first two pages, often within the first paragraph or two after the motivating case. It is stated **at least twice**: once in compressed form (sometimes a named, italicised principle), and once in fuller, scoped form. Authors **name** their view (small-caps or italicised labels like LIABILITY CONTRACTION, the *Limited Use View*, the *Observer Liability* thesis, *Self-Other Symmetry*). This naming earns three things at once: clarity, citability, and a flag for the dialectic that follows. The thesis is presented with confident scoping ("I argue that…", "I defend the view that…") but is immediately accompanied by hedges about what is *not* being claimed.

**Examples from the corpus.**

- *Frowe, "The Limited Use View of the Duty to Save"* names and states the thesis in the first paragraph of section 1, then immediately previews the structure section by section. The thesis is a single sentence, in italics-as-label form: "The Limited Use View holds that the duty to save is a duty to treat oneself, and perhaps one's resources, as a means of preventing harm to others." The scope is then specified by what counts as a "limit".
- *Frowe & Parry, "Wrongful Observation"* set up the field, then offer a displayed, indented thesis statement: "*Observer Liability*: Voluntary and unjustified observation of another agent's degrading wrongdoing, or of the product of their degrading wrongdoing, can render an agent morally liable to bear costs for the sake of the victim of the primary wrong." Note the "can" — modest in modality, ambitious in subject matter.
- *Parry, "Why Paternalism Is Wrong (When It Is Wrong)"* states the thesis in the abstract and again at the end of the introduction, in compressed form: persons have a normative power to control how others may use their *good*. Adjacent views (autonomy-based, competence-based) are named so the contrast is immediate.

**Practical takeaway.** State your thesis early, name it, and scope it with care. The named label should be a noun phrase you can refer back to throughout the paper without rephrasing. Distinguish your thesis from the two closest neighbours in the literature in the same paragraph that introduces it — not later. Use "can" rather than "must", "often" rather than "always", when those weaker modalities will still let you make the contribution you want.

## 3. Dialectical structure and objection handling

**Dominant convention.** Objections are integrated throughout the paper, not bracketed at the end. The strongest papers handle objections in three layers: (i) **preemptive caveats** in the first two pages, ruling out misreadings ("I leave aside…", "I am not here to argue that…"); (ii) **mid-argument micro-objections** addressed in a paragraph or short subsection as the positive case is being built ("One might respond…"); and (iii) **at least one full dedicated section** toward the back half devoted to the strongest objection or to a head-to-head with the most developed rival. Objection-handling typically occupies between a quarter and a third of the paper. Signals that an objection is being taken seriously include: stating it in its strongest form before responding; granting some of its premises explicitly; constructing a new case to test it; and conceding what cannot be saved.

**Examples from the corpus.**

- *Quong, "Killing in Self-Defense"* devotes substantial later sections to working through hard cases (Innocent Threat, Innocent Cause, the Miner) that test his account, and explicitly grants intuitions in cases he must accommodate before deploying his counterfactual "but-for" test. He concedes the objection that the temporal restriction "looks arbitrary" and then defends the asymmetry.
- *Lazar, "Responsibility, Risk, and Killing in Self-Defense"* explicitly considers — and rebuts — the objection that his symmetry argument should make *both* sides liable rather than neither. He gives the objection a paragraph, then offers two reasons in reply, the second of which exposes a structural feature of the rival view.
- *Clark, "Refusing Protection"* presents Parry's EXCLUSION Account as the most developed rival, gives it sustained section-length attention before offering his own CONTRACTION Account, and explicitly catalogues where his view is *less* powerful than the rival as well as where it is more powerful. This even-handedness signals seriousness.
- *Tomlin, "Innocence Lost"* devotes most of the paper to refining and pressing the objection against Tadros's Duty View, then canvasses several responses on Tadros's behalf before settling on the one he prefers — treating the dialectic itself as the contribution.

**Practical takeaway.** Identify the best version of the objection you are most likely to face, and write that version yourself rather than waiting for a referee to do it. Devote a dedicated subsection to it. Before replying, restate it in language an opponent would endorse. Concede whatever you can — the more you concede, the more authority your remaining reply carries. If you cannot fully answer an objection, say so explicitly and explain why your residual view is still worth holding.

## 4. Literature engagement

**Dominant convention.** There is almost never a stand-alone "Literature Review" section. The literature appears in three places only: (a) the opening few pages, where two or three named positions are introduced to locate the contribution; (b) footnotes, where citation density is high and where the author signals familiarity with the broader debate without disrupting the main text; and (c) in dedicated objection or rival-view sections, where one or two interlocutors are engaged closely and at length. Engagement is **selective and asymmetric**: a handful of interlocutors get real engagement (sometimes several pages), while many more are dispatched in a footnote. Survey-style coverage is treated as a sign of inexperience.

**Examples from the corpus.**

- *Tadros, "Permissibility in a World of Wrongdoing"* engages closely with a small number of named views (e.g. Quong on intervening agency) and cases of his own construction, while the broader literature appears in footnotes. He does not paraphrase the field; he picks his fights.
- *Estlund, "What's Unjust about Structural Injustice"* engages Iris Marion Young, Haslanger, and Rawls as live interlocutors, but each engagement is targeted at a particular sub-question (does the view make injustice agent-independent? does it warrant resentment?). The literature is used as scaffolding for the dilemma he wants to press.
- *Howard, "Moral Subversion and Structural Entrapment"* opens with a single page on a real legal case (*Jacobson v. United States*) and uses the existing literature on entrapment only as a foil — "available accounts are incomplete" — and dispatches it in a single section before building the positive view.
- *Sliwa, "Making sense of things"* engages closely with Fricker on hermeneutical injustice, Murdoch, Camp, and Tiberius. Each is summoned for a particular purpose; none gets blanket treatment.

**Practical takeaway.** Identify the two or three interlocutors whose views you must distinguish your view from in order for your contribution to be visible. Engage those closely. Push everyone else into footnotes. Do not summarise the field. If your paper requires more than a paragraph of context before the puzzle is on the table, the puzzle is not yet sharp enough.

## 5. Argument structure and packaging

**Dominant convention.** Arguments are presented in **prose** with occasional, sparing use of numbered premises or displayed principles. Formal apparatus is rare and used only where it earns its keep — usually for a key inference that the rest of the paper turns on. Cases are displayed in indented italic blocks with bolded or italicised case names (*Paradigm*, *Easy Defense*, *Town Hall*, *Medicine*, *Modern Family*). Sub-arguments are signposted with explicit verbs ("I argue…", "I show that…", "I now turn to…", "Here is why…"). Inferential steps are made explicit but rarely numbered. Definitions are introduced where they are first needed, sometimes in displayed form, and labelled so they can be referred back to.

**Examples from the corpus.**

- *Oberman, "Killing and Rescuing"* introduces a displayed *Necessity* definition early, then runs the rest of the paper as a step-by-step exposure of the "description problem" — a name he coins and uses as a recurring referent. The argument is in prose, but the displayed definitions and named problems give it the rigour of a numbered argument.
- *Graham, "A Sketch of a Theory of Moral Blameworthiness"* numbers its three core claims explicitly in the conclusion ("(1)… (2)… (3)…") even though the paper itself is in prose. The numbering at the end acts as a takeaway anchor for the reader.
- *Cullity & Wodak, "But thinking makes it so"* present a quintet of comparative cases (*Gallant Rescue*, *Greedy Rescue*, *Ill-Suited*, *Race Bias*, *Gender Bias*) as a displayed list, then run the rest of the paper as commentary on what the comparisons show. The structure makes the argument auditable.
- *Muñoz & Baron-Schmitt, "Wronging Oneself"* use a "Self-Other Symmetry" thesis that is repeatedly invoked under that name; sub-arguments are clearly labelled (e.g. the "Paradox of Self-Release"), making the argumentative architecture navigable.

**Practical takeaway.** Write your arguments in prose, but display your cases, your principles, and any inference your paper genuinely depends on. Name your cases and your key moves. If a reader cannot, halfway through, point to the sentence where the central inference happens, restructure. Avoid numbered premise–conclusion formats unless you are showing that an argument is invalid — they read as undergraduate in the best journals.

## 6. Tone, authority, and epistemic register

**Dominant convention.** Authors write in the first person, confidently but with calibrated hedges. The register is "informed colleague making a careful case" rather than "expert pronouncing". Authority is projected through three things: precise scoping ("I do not claim that all observation is wrongful"), willingness to concede ("I grant the conditional claim of the Broad Scope View"), and clean separation of what is being argued for from what is being assumed. Hedging language clusters at moments of inference and verdict ("it seems to me", "I take it that", "plausibly", "arguably"), not at moments of definition or restatement. (Corpus description — the house calibration is stricter: hedge *intuitive case verdicts* only; inferences, definitions, thesis, and objection-closers stay flat. DIGEST §8.) Authors do not apologise for their view or pre-emptively soften it.

**Examples from the corpus.**

- *Frowe, "Assisting the Assisters"* opens with confident first-person framing ("Henceforth, I use 'Western states' to refer to…") and uses "I argue", "I claim", "I leave aside" throughout. Hedges are reserved for empirical claims and inference points, not for the structural thesis.
- *Estlund, "What's Unjust about Structural Injustice"* writes with characteristic understatement — "I press the dilemma to scrutinize…", "I think it has become too important and plausible a part of critical discourse to deny that there is any such thing" — projecting authority through admission of difficulty rather than assertion.
- *Tadros, "Orwell's Battle with Brittain"* commits to a contrarian position (siding with Orwell) and writes in plain, declarative prose throughout: "I aim to defend the spirit of George Orwell…". The confidence comes from clarity of commitment, not from rhetorical force.
- *Isserow, "Pride and Investment"* is playful, even self-deprecating in places ("This isn't, to be sure, an article about me. But you'll forgive the egocentrism"), without surrendering authority — the wit is in service of the argument, not a substitute for it.

**Practical takeaway.** Use the first person. Use it confidently. Save "it seems to me" and "plausibly" for moments where you genuinely want the reader's assent to be provisional. Do not hedge your thesis statement, your definitions, or your structural claims. Make at least one explicit concession to a view you are arguing against — it buys you credibility for the rest of the paper.

## 7. Signposting and transitions

**Dominant convention.** Heavy, almost over-engineered roadmapping is standard. After the thesis is introduced, a "the paper proceeds as follows" paragraph maps out the sections by number, with one or two sentences per section explaining what that section will do. Sections are numbered with Roman or Arabic numerals; subsections are often lettered (II.A, II.B) or numbered (2.1, 2.2). Section openings restate where we are in the dialectic ("In the previous section, I argued…; in this section I turn to…"). Paragraph openings frequently use a topic sentence that announces the move ("There is a further problem.", "One might object that…", "Consider next…"). Transitions are explicit, never implicit.

**Examples from the corpus.**

- *Frowe, "The Limited Use View"* gives the reader a paragraph-by-paragraph map of all eight sections in the introduction, then opens each section with a one-sentence reminder of what was just established and what is now being done.
- *Frowe & Parry, "Wrongful Observation"* end the introduction with: "Section IV argues that observation can compound a primary wrong… Section V argues that observation can constitute degrading treatment… Section VI shows how observation can enable primary wrongdoing…" Each section then opens with the same construction.
- *Clark, "Refusing Protection"* opens section VI by naming the case it considers, recalling the result of the previous section, and stating its job ("So why is it that refusal does not undermine the police's permission to intervene? I will argue…").

**Practical takeaway.** End your introduction with a structural paragraph that says, in plain language, what each section will do. Begin each section with a single sentence that recalls where you have got to and announces what this section adds. Begin each new paragraph with a topic sentence that tells the reader what work the paragraph is doing — motivating, objecting, conceding, illustrating, generalising. Repetition of structure is a feature, not a bug.

## 8. Use of examples and cases

**Dominant convention.** Cases are central. Almost every paper in the corpus uses constructed cases as load-bearing machinery, not as illustration. Cases come in **pairs** or **series** that vary one feature at a time to isolate what is morally significant. They are short — usually three to six sentences — and stripped of unnecessary detail. They are named in italics or bold (*Paradigm*, *Easy Defense*, *Whom to Rescue*, *Medicine*, *Town Hall*, *Modern Family*, *Infidelity*). The author invests just enough in setting up the case to make it stipulate-able; readers are expected to share the relevant intuition or have it pumped explicitly. Authors then build cases out into variants ("As before, except…") so that small variations can be compared cleanly. Real-world events are sometimes used at the opening to motivate, but the analytical work is almost always done with stipulated cases.

**Examples from the corpus.**

- *Clark, "The Demands of Necessity"* introduces *Fast and Furious*, then immediately gives *Fast and Less Furious* ("As before, except the threat to Pedestrian is much smaller"), then *Easy Defense* ("As in Fast and Furious, except…"). The minimal-variation method is the engine of the paper.
- *Oberman, "Killing and Rescuing"* introduces *How to Rescue* in two sentences, then immediately uses it to define the problem he wants to solve. The case is the smallest one that does the work.
- *Lichter, "Wrongdoer-Centered Reasons for Blame"* uses a case from the sitcom *Modern Family* and a constructed *Infidelity* case to motivate the existence of the phenomenon. The constructed case is paired with the borrowed one to triangulate the intuition.
- *Cullity & Wodak, "But thinking makes it so"* present five paired cases in sequence (Gallant vs Greedy Rescue; Ill-Suited vs Race Bias vs Gender Bias) and use the comparative judgments to set up the puzzle that the rest of the paper resolves.

**Practical takeaway.** Build your central case first, in the smallest form that does the work. Then build at least one variant that differs by exactly one morally salient feature. Name your cases. Italicise or bold the names. Use the cases as recurring referents through the paper — readers will navigate by them. Do not pad the case with irrelevant detail; every clause should either set up the intuition or block an obvious dodge.

## 9. Conclusions

**Dominant convention.** Conclusions are short — typically half a page to two pages — and disciplined. They do three things, in roughly this order: (i) restate the thesis in compressed form; (ii) summarise the key moves of the argument as a short numbered or labelled list; (iii) gesture briefly at broader implications or open questions (house calibration: an *explicitly flagged* open question is fine; future-work gestures as filler are not — DIGEST §8 Closings). The strongest conclusions resist re-arguing. They do not introduce new material. They sometimes end with a single rhetorical or aphoristic sentence — but only after the substantive summary has been made. Overreach is rare; when authors gesture beyond the argument they have made, they signal it explicitly ("This raises a further question that I cannot pursue here…").

**Examples from the corpus.**

- *Lichter, "Wrongdoer-Centered Reasons for Blame"* ends with a one-paragraph "VI. CONCLUSION" that names the contribution ("we have wrongdoer-centered reasons for blame"), summarises the mechanism ("contribution recognition"), and ends with one synthesising sentence about the moral ecosystem of blame.
- *Tadros, "Permissibility in a World of Wrongdoing"* ends with a "VI. CONCLUSIONS" section that says what survives, what does not, and where the support was found — including for the "unpopular view" that turned out to have some support. He is explicit about what was *not* established.
- *Howard, "Moral Subversion and Structural Entrapment"* ends with a one-paragraph aphoristic close ("The most important way for democracies to respond to structural entrapment is, then, to stop perpetrating it") — but only after several pages of careful implications work.
- *Lazar, "Responsibility, Risk, and Killing in Self-Defense"* ends starkly: pacifism is "a live possibility" if his argument goes through. The conclusion does not retreat from the dialectical position the body of the paper earned.

**Practical takeaway.** Your conclusion should be re-readable as a standalone abstract of what you did. Restate the thesis. Enumerate the moves. Do not introduce new arguments. Be willing to end on a clean, even bold, sentence — but only if the body of the paper has earned it. Resist the temptation to expand the implications beyond what the argument supports.

## 10. Sentence-level style

**Dominant convention.** Sentences are mid-length and varied; the prose is plain but precise. Technical vocabulary is used sparingly and is always defined — and defined *once*, usually in the sentence or paragraph where it first appears. Plain English is preferred even for technical concepts where possible ("treats oneself as a means", "rights forfeiture", "agent-relative prerogatives"). Rhetorical questions are used very sparingly, almost always as a transition or to flag a puzzle the paper will resolve ("Why aren't we constantly violating our own rights?"). The voice is predominantly active. Passive voice is reserved for moments where the agent is genuinely beside the point. Italics are used for case names and for emphasis on the inferential pivot of a sentence. Footnotes are used liberally for qualifications, secondary literature, edge cases, and replies to anticipated objections that would clutter the main text. (Corpus description — the house rule differs: no substantive argument in footnotes; if a footnote argues, promote it or cut it. DIGEST §8 Footnotes.)

**Examples from the corpus.**

- *Muñoz & Baron-Schmitt, "Wronging Oneself"* deploy short, varied sentences and even occasional one-clause sentences for emphasis: "Pinching one's own arm is not like pinching a stranger out of the blue. It is like pinching someone else's arm with their consent. No one is wronged because there is no unwilling victim." The cadence carries the argument.
- *Srinivasan, "The Aptness of Anger"* mixes rhetorical force with analytic discipline: "These are not mere insults. These are rhetorical strategies that shift the explanatory context for the subject's anger from the space of reasons to the space of causes." Short declarative sentences carry the polemical edge; the analytic move is in the second sentence.
- *Tadros, "Orwell's Battle with Brittain"* uses plain English throughout, even when the topic (vicarious liability, obliteration bombing) could invite jargon. He defines terms once and uses them.
- *Parry, "Why Paternalism Is Wrong (When It Is Wrong)"* coins one substantive technical term — using a person's "good" as a justificatory resource — and uses it consistently as a referent throughout the paper. No other coinages are made; the prose around it stays plain.

**Practical takeaway.** Default to plain English. Coin at most one or two new terms per paper; use them with consistent definitions; do not vary your terminology for stylistic relief. Use rhetorical questions only at structural moments. Prefer the active voice. Use footnotes generously, but only for content that would otherwise distract; if a footnote is doing real argumentative work, consider whether it belongs in the body.

## 11. Structural conventions

**Dominant convention.** Typical paper length runs from roughly 8,000 words (*Ethics* and *Philosophy & Public Affairs* short pieces; some OSNE chapters) to 20,000+ words (*Philosophical Review*, longer *PPA* and *Ethics* pieces). Most fall in the 10,000–15,000 word range. Section headers are numbered, usually in Roman numerals at the top level (I, II, III) with section titles in small caps, italics, or bold; subsections are either numbered (II.A, II.B) or have descriptive titles. An "Introduction" section is standard; a "Conclusion" section is standard; both are explicitly labelled. Abstracts are short — typically 100–200 words — and state the thesis, the central move, and what is shown, without describing the structure of the paper. Footnotes are dense (often 80–150+ in a long paper) and carry citations, qualifications, replies to anticipated objections, and engagement with the secondary literature. Acknowledgements are placed in an unnumbered first footnote on the title page.

**Examples from the corpus.**

- *Quong, "Killing in Self-Defense"* (Ethics) runs ~30 pages, with seven sections and numerous sub-cases, dense footnotes, and a short abstract; sections are Roman-numeralled.
- *Frowe & Parry, "Wrongful Observation"* runs ~34 pages with nine sections, each opening with a one-sentence orientation, an unnumbered acknowledgements footnote, and dense citation footnotes.
- *Parry, "Why Paternalism Is Wrong"* (PPR) runs ~16 published pages with a tight abstract that states the contribution in three sentences and a final section that gestures at extensions rather than re-arguing.
- *Lichter, "Wrongdoer-Centered Reasons for Blame"* (Ethics) uses six numbered sections plus a conclusion; the first footnote on page 1 handles acknowledgements and the substantive footnotes do real dialectical work.

**Practical takeaway.** (Corpus norm: 10,000–15,000 words. Competition essays: 6,000 hard cap — see the calibration note at the top.)  Number your sections. Give them descriptive titles. Write your abstract last, in 100–200 words, stating: the puzzle, the thesis, the central move, and what you show — in that order, without describing the paper's structure. Put acknowledgements in an unnumbered first footnote. Use footnotes generously but keep your main text readable when the footnotes are stripped out.

## Red flags and common failure modes

These are patterns that mark a paper as aiming *below* the level of the target journals. They are derived from what is conspicuously *absent* in the corpus, or what appears only in weaker passages.

**Opening failures.**
- Opening with "There has been much debate about X". Strong papers open with a case, a puzzle, or an event.
- A long literature review before the thesis. The literature should be woven in, not surveyed.
- Vague stakes-setting ("This is an important question because…"). Stakes are shown by the case, not asserted.

**Thesis failures.**
- A thesis stated only once, in passive voice, somewhere in the middle of the introduction.
- An unnamed thesis. Strong papers name their view so it can be referred back to.
- A thesis that does not distinguish itself from the two closest neighbouring positions at the moment of introduction.
- Over-claiming: "always", "must", "no one can deny". The corpus prefers "can", "often", "in the cases I will discuss".

**Dialectical failures.**
- All objections shunted to a final section. Strong papers integrate objection-handling throughout.
- Strawman versions of opposing views. Strong papers present opponents in their best form.
- No concessions anywhere in the paper. A paper that grants nothing reads as defensive.
- Failure to engage the most developed rival head-on, in its own subsection.

**Literature failures.**
- Long passages of paraphrase of secondary literature without a clear use for it.
- Even citation density across the paper, rather than dense engagement with a few interlocutors and footnote-level acknowledgement of the rest.
- Citing without showing one has read — citations without engagement.

**Structural failures.**
- No roadmap paragraph at the end of the introduction.
- Section openings that do not recall the dialectical position.
- Paragraphs without topic sentences.
- Subsections that do not earn their separation from the surrounding text.

**Argumentative failures.**
- Numbered premise–conclusion arguments used as the primary mode of exposition (rather than reserved for a critical move).
- Cases that are over-described — paragraph-long set-ups for a one-sentence intuition.
- Cases that are not named, and so cannot be referred back to.
- Cases used only once. Strong papers re-use cases throughout the paper to track how a verdict changes under different assumptions.
- Failure to construct minimally varying pairs of cases when the argument depends on a single morally salient difference.

**Stylistic failures.**
- Reliance on rhetorical questions for argumentative work.
- Jargon used before it is defined.
- Multiple terms used interchangeably for the same notion (variation-for-its-own-sake).
- Heavy use of "clearly", "obviously", "surely" at the inferential pivots — strong papers prefer to *show* the move.
- Excessive hedging that obscures the thesis ("I tentatively suggest that perhaps it may be that…").

**Conclusion failures.**
- Conclusions that re-argue rather than restate.
- Conclusions that introduce new material.
- Conclusions that overreach beyond what the body argued for — sweeping claims about social or political implications that the technical argument cannot support.
- Conclusions that gesture at "future work" as a substitute for closure.

**Tone failures.**
- Anonymous third-person prose ("It is argued in this paper that…") as a default register.
- Hedging the thesis itself.
- Apologising for the limits of the project in the introduction.
- Heavy use of the passive voice in inferential sentences.

**Length and packaging failures.**
- Papers that are too long for the contribution they make.
- Long sections without subsections to orient the reader.
- Abstracts that describe the structure of the paper rather than its argument and contribution.
- Sparse or empty footnotes — the corpus uses footnotes as a working second layer of the paper.

## Companion: a quick diagnostic (use alongside the headings above)

**The one-sentence test.** Before drafting, fill in: *"I argue that **P**, against
the common view that **Q**, on the grounds that **R**."* If you cannot, you have a
topic, not a thesis. (Cross-check against §2.)

**Contribution — name which one you are making:** a new argument; a new
distinction that dissolves a confusion; a counterexample; a reframing; or a
diagnosis of *why* an attractive view fails. Pick one and make it the spine.

**Pre-submission checklist** (each item maps to a section above):

- [ ] Opening is a case / puzzle / event — or a *sharp* gap that is itself the hook (DIGEST §8); never limp "much has been written about X" (§1)
- [ ] Thesis by p.2, named, scoped, distinguished from its two nearest rivals (§2)
- [ ] Strongest objection written by *you*, in its best form, with a real concession (§3)
- [ ] 2–3 interlocutors engaged closely; everyone else in footnotes (§4)
- [ ] Cases named, minimal, paired to vary one feature; reused as referents (§5, §8)
- [ ] First person, confident; hedges only on intuitive case verdicts — inferences, definitions, thesis flat (DIGEST §8)
- [ ] Roadmap paragraph; each section opens by recalling the dialectic (§7)
- [ ] Conclusion restates and enumerates; introduces nothing new (§9)
- [ ] Plain English; at most 1–2 coined terms, defined once, never varied (§10)
- [ ] Length matches the contribution; sections earn their subsections (§11)
- [ ] Every citation resolves in your library — run the citation-auditor before submission

## Addendum — cross-validation against the casebook mining (Fable 5, 2026-07)

This repo's independent extraction (all 36 corpus papers → `structure-casebook.md`)
was reconciled against the guide above. Verdict: **no material misdescriptions**;
the guide's conventions hold across the wider set. Five conventions the original
pass did not *name* as devices, now documented (worked examples in the casebook
and `Playbook/Craft/Exemplars/structure/`):

1. **The openly revised principle** (Quong, *Killing in Self-Defense*): state a
   principle, break it with a case, amend it, repeat — in full view. Rigor as
   visible process; the reader audits each repair. → exemplar
   `quong--iteratively-revised-principle`.
2. **The coverage-rubric skeleton** (Cullity & Wodak's four *Challenges*;
   Isserow's four *P*'s; Sliwa's six components): a coined checklist that is
   simultaneously the paper's section structure and its objection-discharge
   list. → exemplar `cullity-wodak--coverage-rubric-skeleton`.
3. **Internal objections / by-their-own-lights** (Lazar on McMahan; Frowe on
   Quong): defeat the rival with *its own* commitments rather than an external
   intuition — the strongest form of refutation in this literature. → exemplar
   `lazar--internal-objections-method`.
4. **The dialectic as the contribution** (Tomlin, *Innocence Lost*): a critique
   paper built as escalating rescue-tests of the target view, conceding which
   rescue is best — the survey of repairs *is* the payoff. → exemplar
   `tomlin--dialectic-as-contribution`.
5. **Elimination-as-derivation** (Parry, Fritz, Graham, "Price of Duty"): the
   positive view earned as the residue after candidate views are serially
   defeated by matched minimal-pair cases — not asserted, arrived at. → exemplar
   `parry-paternalism--elimination-structure`.

# Model Paragraphs — the register, shown

Calibration specimens for every prose pass. These paragraphs are **synthetic**: written for this
workspace, on an invented running example (a duty to warn strangers of danger), with placeholder
citations "(N.N. year)". They exist because rules alone push prose toward over-execution — a writer
needs to *see* the register. **Read this file in full, fresh, at the start of every drafting session
and every prose round; the patterns fade fast.** Read for how the sentences move, never for content.

What to notice across all specimens: sentences mostly mid-length and plain, with short ones only at
real pivots; paragraphs that end on ordinary workmanlike sentences, not epigrams; terms repeated
rather than varied; reasoning spelled out rather than compressed; almost no dashes; nothing that
sounds quotable. The prose is deliberately a little boring. That is the target: the reader should
come away holding the argument and unable to remember a single flashy sentence.

---

## 1. Stating a case, then the verdict

*Missed Train.* Ana is running for the last train. Ben, a stranger on the platform, sees that the
doors are about to close and says nothing. Ana misses the train and waits an hour for the next one.

Ben has not wronged Ana. He could have helped her at almost no cost, and it would have been kind to
do so. But Ana had no claim on his help. If she learned afterward that he had watched in silence,
she could think him unfriendly; she could not demand an apology. The case is worth fixing in mind
because the duty to warn, if there is one, must differ from this kind of optional kindness in some
principled way. The next case shows where the difference begins.

**Why this works.** The case is three sentences, and every detail in it is used. The verdict comes
first, flat, in its own short sentence. The explanation follows in plain declaratives. The paragraph
ends by saying what the case is for, in an ordinary sentence, not a flourish.

## 2. Stating the thesis

I argue that there is a duty to warn strangers of serious danger, and that it is a genuine duty
rather than an optional kindness: a stranger who could easily warn and does not wrongs the person he
fails to warn. I do not claim that every failure to help is like this. Most are not, and *Missed
Train* shows why: help with an ordinary inconvenience is not owed. My claim concerns danger to life
and limb, where the warning costs little and no one else is positioned to give it.

**Why this works.** The thesis is one sentence, unhedged. The scope sentence follows immediately and
says what is not being claimed. The paragraph refers back to the case by name instead of re-telling
it. No coinage is introduced, because none is needed yet.

## 3. Introducing the one piece of apparatus

It will help to have a label for the situation the thesis concerns. Say that a person is a
*last resort* with respect to a danger when she can avert it at small cost and no one else can. The
term does ordinary work in what follows: the duty I defend attaches to last resorts and only to
them. Nothing turns on the label itself; readers who prefer a different one can substitute it.

**Why this works.** One term, introduced when first needed, defined in one sentence, with its job
stated. The modularity sentence closes off a verbal dispute before it starts. This is the essay's
whole coinage budget being spent, visibly and once.

## 4. Explaining an imported idea from zero

The argument uses one idea from the theory of rights. A claim, in the sense I need, is something a
person can demand as her due, so that failing her is not merely unkind but wrongs her (N.N. 1970).
The mark of a claim is what happens after the failure. If Ana had a claim to Ben's warning, she
could resent him, ask for an apology, and expect third parties to take her side. If she had no
claim, she could at most regret his coldness. The question about the duty to warn is therefore a
question about which side of this line the platform cases fall on.

**Why this works.** The imported notion is defined in the body, from scratch, in a sentence — no
allusion, no assumption that the reader knows the literature. It is immediately cashed out in the
case already on the page. The last sentence says plainly what the machinery will be used for.

## 5. An objection, steelmanned, then answered

One might object that the duty I defend collapses into a general duty of easy rescue, which many
reject. The objection deserves its strongest form. Warning is a kind of rescue; if there is no duty
to rescue at small cost, there is no duty to warn at small cost; and the rejection of easy-rescue
duties has serious defenders (N.N. 1984). I answer by conceding the conditional and denying its
antecedent for the special case. What the defenders of the no-duty view reject is a duty that would
leave agents perpetually on call, liable to interruption by any nearby need. A duty confined to
last resorts has no such profile. It binds rarely, ends quickly, and cannot be outsourced, because
by definition no one else can discharge it. Whatever force the on-call worry has against general
rescue duties, it has none here.

**Why this works.** The objection is stated better than an opponent would state it, with its best
motivation. The reply concedes what it can, names exactly what it denies, and gives the reason in
full sentences. The paragraph ends on the conclusion of the reply, stated plainly, not on a quip.

## 6. A transition between sections

So far I have argued that last resorts owe warnings in the two-person case. The harder question is
what happens in a crowd, where each bystander can see that others could also warn. Section 4 takes
this up.

**Why this works.** Three sentences: where we are, what is next, where it happens. No "having
established," no recap of the argument just given.

## 7. Working through a small piece of reasoning

Suppose each of five bystanders reasons as follows: someone else will warn, so I need not. If all
five reason this way, no one warns, and the danger lands. The reasoning cannot be sound for all of
them at once. But it does not follow that each bystander is a last resort, since for each of them it
is true that others could warn. What follows is something weaker: each is a *potential* last resort,
whose duty is conditional on the others' failure, and the condition is satisfied in the very
situation the reasoning produces. The duty to warn survives the crowd, then, though it binds later
than in the two-person case: it binds when waiting for the others has visibly failed.

**Why this works.** The reasoning is unpacked step by step, one move per sentence, with the
tempting mistake ruled out on the way. Where the outline might have said "diffusion of
responsibility → conditional duty," the prose walks the reader through why. The conclusion sentence
is workmanlike and carries a real qualification.

## 8. A concession that narrows the thesis

I concede that the duty I have defended is easier to state than to apply. Whether a cost is small
and whether anyone else is positioned to help are matters of judgment, and agents will sometimes
get them wrong blamelessly. My claim is not that the duty's boundaries are sharp. It is that within
the clear cases, the ones where the cost is plainly trivial and the agent plainly the last resort,
the failure to warn is a wrong and not merely a coldness. The unclear cases inherit whatever
unclarity the notions of cost and position already have, and I leave them where they lie.

**Why this works.** The concession is real and unembarrassed. "My claim is not that… It is that…"
fences off the over-reading in plain words. The final sentence closes the point without ceremony.

## 9. A conclusion paragraph

I have argued that strangers positioned as last resorts owe warnings against serious danger, that
this duty survives the crowd in conditional form, and that it does not collapse into a general duty
of easy rescue. The argument ran through one distinction: between help that would be kind and help
that is owed, with the last-resort position marking the line. I have not argued that the duty
extends to ordinary inconveniences, and nothing here settles how it interacts with duties to
intimates. Within its scope, though, the conclusion stands: the silent bystander on the platform is
not merely unfriendly.

**Why this works.** The conclusion restates the results and the scope, enumerates without
re-arguing, and permits itself exactly one mildly pointed final sentence, which the body has earned.
This is the only paragraph in the essay allowed to land that way.

## The negative specimen — what the same content looks like over-written

> Warning, then, is not charity; it is currency. Each bystander holds a note the endangered may
> call in — and the crowd, far from diluting the debt, merely staggers its maturity. Call this the
> Maturity Structure. The upshot is stark: silence is not coldness but default.

(Running `prose_lint.py` on this file flags exactly this specimen and the meta-labels — the
linter and the register agree.) Every sentence above is a defect. The metaphor (currency, notes, maturity) is carrying the argument
rather than illustrating it. A coinage is minted that the essay does not need. Three sentences in a
row land as epigrams. "The upshot is stark" announces significance instead of earning it. Nothing
tells the reader what actually follows from what. When a draft starts sounding like this specimen,
stop and rewrite toward the nine above.

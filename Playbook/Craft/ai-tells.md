# AI Tells — phrases and habits that make prose read as machine-made

The fastest way for AI to ruin academic writing is to make it sound like AI: the texture reads as
careless and hides the argument. The competition's judges know the essays are AI-written — the goal
here is not disguise but prose quality: every tell below makes the philosophy harder to grade. This
file is the banned-list the copy-critic and the Stage-7 clarity pass enforce. Adapted from the
entrant's `acdemwrit` writing system.

---

## Stock phrases to cut or replace

These rarely earn their place. Cut, or replace with the plain thing.

- "It is important to note that…" / "It is worth noting that…" → just say it.
- "It should be emphasized that…" → just emphasize it.
- "Delve into," "dive into" → "examine," "consider," or just do it.
- "A rich tapestry of," "a nuanced interplay," "a complex landscape" → name the
  actual thing.
- "Plays a crucial/pivotal/vital role in" → say what it does.
- "Navigate the complexities of" → "address," "handle," or be specific.
- "In today's [adjective] world," "in an era of" → cut.
- "Multifaceted," "myriad," "plethora" → "many," or a number.
- "Underscore," "highlight," "showcase" (as filler verbs) → "show," "stress."
- "It is not merely X but rather Y" (when overused) → vary it.
- "Furthermore / Moreover / Additionally" stacked as paragraph openers → use
  inferential connectives instead (see `prose-principles.md`).
- "Resonate with," "speak to," "lean into" → say what you mean.
- "Testament to," "stands as a testament" → cut.
- "Crucial," "key," "vital," "essential" as reflexive intensifiers → usually
  deletable; if everything is crucial, nothing is.
- "Serves as," "stands as," "boasts," "emerged as" (copula-avoidance) → "is,"
  "has." Direct predication is not a weakness.
- "Ensures / ensuring," "reflects," "supports" as padding verbs → the concrete
  verb for what actually happens.

## Structural tells

- **Listicle-ification of an argument.** Turning a chain of reasoning into a
  bulleted list of "key points." Arguments are prose; keep them prose.
- **The triadic sentence on autopilot.** Endless "X, Y, and Z" parallel triples.
  One is fine; a paragraph of them is a tell.
- **Em-dash overuse.** *(Register note: the em-dash gloss — "bearing an unreasonable — that is,
  supererogatory — cost" — is not itself a tell in this field, but the house preference is em dashes
  kept to a minimum: default to commas, colons, or parentheses; use a dash only where it is clearly
  the best tool — DIGEST §8 owns this rule.)* Three per paragraph is a machine signature regardless.
- **The "Not only… but also" / "It's not just… it's…" reflex — and plain
  "not X, but Y" antithesis run as a rhythm.** Fine once, and fine where two
  explanations genuinely compete; a tic when repeated as cadence.
- **Symmetrical, equal-length sentences** marching in a row. Vary length (see
  prose principles).
- **Hollow summary sentences** ("In conclusion, this analysis has explored…")
  that restate the journey instead of the result.
- **Hedge inflation.** Stacked qualifiers that lower confidence without narrowing
  content (see prose principles on hedging).
- **Hedge-and-reassure stacking.** The specific machine form of hedge inflation:
  "While this may vary, generally speaking, in most cases…" One hedge per claim,
  chosen by function (case verdicts hedged; definitions and theses flat).
- **Section-final "takeaway" recaps** that repeat what was just said. Check every
  paragraph's *last* sentence too — the redundant restatement is usually there.
- **Participial tack-ons.** Sentences ending ", highlighting the importance
  of…", ", underscoring…", ", reflecting broader…". If the clause adds a step,
  make it a sentence; if not, delete it.
- **False ranges.** "From X to Y" where there is no real scale ("from intimate
  worries to global movements"). Reserve for genuine, orderable ranges.
- **The four-beat paragraph arc on repeat** (setup → expansion → complication →
  resolution, every paragraph). Keep the topic-sentence discipline — that's
  field practice, not a tell (`journal-craft` §7) — but vary how paragraphs
  *end*: on the argument's edge, a case verdict, or the unresolved cost, not a
  wrap-up beat.
- **The colon reveal.** Noun phrase, colon, dramatic payoff: "The detail that
  makes it work: a separate agent grades it." A displayed principle after a
  colon is field practice; the rhythm-crutch reveal is the tell. Rewrite as a
  plain sentence.
- **Negative listing.** "Not X. Not Y. Just Z." State Z.
- **The fake-profound kicker.** A closing aphorism or cute metaphor that
  upgrades the point into wisdom. Don't rewrite it — delete it, and end on the
  strongest concrete sentence already there (cf. the Closings adjudication in DIGEST §8).
- **Stacked compression.** The mechanism behind "dense AI prose": a concept
  turned into a metaphor, a verb frozen into a noun phrase, and the compressed
  units packed side by side. Any one alone is fine — the damage is adjacency.
  Repair: unpack into a plain sentence with the verbs doing the work; never
  two compressed units in a row (one image per idea is already house style).
- **Depth-signaling.** "The real issue underneath," "at a more fundamental
  level," "at bottom" as a reflex. If the point is deep, the structure shows
  it; make the deeper point directly. (A philosopher's earned, argued "at
  bottom" is fine — the tell is the signaling phrase doing the work the
  argument didn't.)
- **Fragment openers as announcing.** A verbless fragment that names a topic
  before the sentence that says something about it ("Two things matter here."
  / "The difference."). Fold the label into the working sentence. Calibration:
  the once-per-paper witty fragment is a house move ("Lovely stuff; hardly
  required.") — the tell is the fragment that *announces*, not the one that
  *lands*.

## Cadence checks (countable — run these on any suspect passage)

- **Length-band run:** 3+ consecutive sentences inside the 17–23-word band →
  break one short or push one long. (Uniform cadence is the single most
  recognized tell in 2026; varying it is also just good prose — see §3 of the
  DIGEST. Flag only when the rhythm is also flat: register-normal analytic prose
  lives at 15–40 words and passes.)
- **Opener share:** more than half the sentences in a paragraph starting with
  "The / This / It / In" → vary openers (name, verb, subordinate clause,
  question).
- **Seesaw test:** mechanical alternation of punchy fragment / 40-word sentence
  is as machine-like as uniformity; most sentences should live in the middle,
  with occasional real swings.
- **Counts per ~300 words:** em dashes (house preference: minimal — DIGEST §8), "not
  only… but also" (max 1 per piece), triads (one is fine, three is a pattern),
  colons used rhetorically (a couple at most).

## Content tells (the dangerous ones)

- **Confident vagueness.** Smooth sentences that assert without arguing. In
  philosophy this is worse than awkward prose, because the *job* is the argument.
- **Fabricated or fuzzy citations.** Any reference, page number, or quotation not
  verified against the library. This is the cardinal sin — see the citation-auditor agent.
- **Misattributed positions.** Ascribing a view to a philosopher "in effect"
  without grounding. Mark reconstructions as reconstructions.
- **False balance / both-sidesing** where the author actually has a view. Take the
  position the paper is defending.

## How the linter should behave

- Flag, don't auto-rewrite content. For stock phrases, propose the plain
  replacement. For structural tells, point to the pattern and the count
  ("six em-dashes in this paragraph"), and let the author decide.
- **The general repair for announce-variants no entry catches:** the label
  names, the next sentence asserts — one sentence can do both, so fold the
  wind-up into the sentence that does the work.
- Never "fix" a tell by introducing another (don't replace one cliché with a
  fancier one).
- Calibrate to the field, not a generic blocklist: a habit that is standard practice in top-journal
  philosophy (displayed principles, "I argue", explicit signposting, a colon before a displayed
  view) is field register, not a tell.
